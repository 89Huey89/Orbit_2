# Orbit

The playable game is a single file. Run `npm run build` to produce **dist/index.html**, which contains the complete game, artwork, sound, and styles, with the seventeenth-century Fell typefaces embedded in `dist/assets/fonts.css` beside it. That built page needs no installation, server, or network access to play, and it is what GitHub Pages serves. During development, open `src/index.html` directly or run `npm start` and visit the printed address: the template loads the same code as separate module scripts, so no build is needed between edits.

## Gameplay overview

- **The loop:** capture a node, orbit it, then release along your current tangent to glide into the next one — tap/click or press Space/Enter, the entire input. A pricked preview line shows your predicted path and marks the release point for a perfect transfer.
- **The goal:** climb as many rows as you can before the rising darkness catches you. Perfect transfers, skipped orbits, and completed three-star constellation routes all score points; slingshot stars add speed and refill your ink.
- **The resource:** every flight spends ink from a nib that recharges while you orbit; run it dry mid-flight and the run ends.
- **The chapters:** the ascent runs through four illustrated chapters — The Quiet, The Drift, The Eclipse, The Deep — each with its own backdrop plate, escalating hazards (vortices, sunspot flares, wind-heads, nebulae), and a 12-figure constellation catalogue to draw from.
- **The pressures:** every run opens on a choice of three difficulty worlds — Tiro, Adeptus, Magister — and a daily plate deals the same seeded chart to everyone each day.
- **The record:** a persistent ledger unlocks cosmetic plates, trail inks, capture marks, frame ornaments, and named-feat medals — all kept in the browser's local storage, no account or install required.

What follows is the full mechanical reference — every rule and number below is deliberate and load-bearing.

Tap or click to begin. While circling a node, tap to release along your current tangent. The next node captures you automatically. Space or Enter performs the same action. Tap after a loss to restart.

The pricked line of small wedges shows your predicted trajectory. Pale ticks on your orbit mark clear tangent release points: skim the next orbit's rim so your incoming flight continues smoothly into the new orbit. A perfect preview includes a short curve showing the arrival direction. Near a vortex, a flare or a wind-head, the guide bends and a bright mark appears at the current release point when the curved flight gives a perfect landing. The wider faint arc remains a forgiving capture window, and on Tiro it is drawn 30% wider still, since missing a body outright is a harsher loss than only missing the perfect band inside it. Stay ahead of the rising dark and give the dark eye of a vortex room. Blue orbit rims mark drifting nodes; copper orbit rims mark nodes that fade 4.5 seconds after capture. Small gold nodes offer a harder bonus route.

Every region includes a fork: follow the wider orbits or take a narrower three-star constellation route. Both paths reconnect. Stars light as you visit them. At a fork, two release marks indicate the two paths. Follow the gold links and capture all three marked planets to complete a constellation, earn 60 extra points, and make the darkness retreat for four seconds. Unfinished constellations expire when you pass their reunion point or an unvisited star sinks completely into the darkness. Each constellation can be rewarded once per run; your completed total appears at the end.

The catalogue holds twelve figures. The first four regions always carry the Needle, the Sail, the Lyre, and the Crown, in that order. Every region after them draws from the catalogue in an order fixed by the run's seed, taking the eight later figures — the Compass, the Hourglass, the Serpent, the Argo, the Astrolabe, the Quill, the Lantern, and the Moth — before returning to the opening four, so no figure repeats until all twelve have been used. Each chart keeps its region index as its identity and records which catalogue entry it drew. The daily plate is the one chart this fixed opening does not bind — see the daily plate below.

The chart is cut for a pace rather than for a row count. Every transfer is drawn to take about 1.28 seconds at the pace the chart expects at that row, which opens from the opening speed toward the chart's top speed over the first twenty-five rows, so the gulfs widen as the early slingshots put that pace within reach. Free flight between two orbits runs about 265 world units on average where it was 166, and the deepest crossings reach 474 where they reached 262. That pace depends only on the row and never on the traveller, so a seed still deals one plate for everyone and the daily plate is unaffected: how a run is flown cannot change the chart it is dealt. The orbits open with it, up to half as wide again, and their capture bands with them. A wider ring is swept more slowly at the same pace and presents a larger rim from further off, which is what keeps the release window in the hand — the angle that finds a rim narrows as the gulf grows, and the window would otherwise have halved as the distances doubled. A slingshot ring is the one exception and keeps its size, since its charge is earned per lap and a wider one would only cost more time against the flood for the same 90 units of speed. Making the sheet narrower pulls the whole chart back inside its own edge, so a rotated screen never leaves an orbit standing where the run is lost.

The opening speed is 150 world units per second, down from 205. Speed no longer rises automatically with progress. A slingshot star appears on the third planet, followed by the first constellation reunion and every eight main rows. Its engraved inner band fills as you orbit. One full lap adds 90 units of speed, up to a launch speed of 360; leave earlier for a smaller increase. Acceleration happens during the orbit, so releasing does not suddenly double your velocity. Each star provides at most one lap of acceleration. Waiting longer earns no extra speed or points, and pausing preserves your charge.

The nib carries a charge of ink, and every flight spends it by the distance flown, so a transfer costs what it is long rather than what it takes: the same crossing costs the same ink whether it is flown at the opening pace or at the chart's top speed. A full nib carries 2,000 world units. Holding an orbit re-charges it slowly, at 0.13 of a charge a second; a landing pays a dividend, 0.12 for a clean tangent arrival against 0.05 for a hard turn; each main orbit skipped along the way pays half that dividend again, since the flight covered its distance without stopping to collect it in full; and one lap of a slingshot star fills it outright, so a star buys reach as well as speed. Run the nib dry in flight and the line stops and the run ends, though the landing is settled first, so a transfer that arrives on the last drop stands. The pressure scales the drain rather than the gauge: Tiro spends 0.82 of what Adeptus does and Magister 1.22, and every plate reads the same reservoir. That reservoir is engraved under the score as a short rule that drains and turns copper as it empties, the bead of wet ink at the nib dries away with it, and a standing note is written beside the orbit being held once the charge is low.

Dwelling on a ring buys ink and spends the time the flood is counting, which is the whole of the trade. A run that leaves at the first opening every time spends distance faster than any landing pays it back and dries up around row 16; a run that waits for a centred line every time keeps ink in hand and is caught by the dark instead. Only flying tangent entries and charging the stars pays for both.

Earned speed carries between planets. A perfect transfer preserves the incoming velocity and joins at the closest point on the rim, including for a drifting planet. The game accepts a small band around the drawn orbit and keeps that arrival radius, avoiding a position or heading snap. That band reaches a fifth of the target's own radius, or eight world units, whichever is larger, and Tiro widens it by 35%, since a tight orbit sweeps through its release window quickly however careful the pilot; Adeptus and Magister keep the drawn band. An angled flight that is not a tangent remains a forgiving ordinary capture, but the sharp turn removes up to 28% of speed above the opening pace. Every accepted landing earns its base capture points. From a rough sixteen-degree arrival to a tangent at ninety degrees, the crossing adds a continuous angle bonus from 0 to 10 points before the speed multiplier; below sixteen degrees it adds none, pays no ink dividend and leaves the hand colour visibly out of register, but never blanks the base or a skip reward. About a quarter of the arrivals the chart offers land this way; a tangent-seeking run never meets the rough threshold at all, since a tangent-seeking pilot lands between 78 and 102 degrees. The guide marks the rough impression with an open chevron before release. The three opening targets are always printed cleanly. The guide and capture use the same moving-target solver. Flight launch speed is capped at 360 so repeated moving-planet assists cannot compound indefinitely. After the introductory star, each slingshot still offers its wider route and a direct transfer to a more distant planet.

Flights have no time limit, but they do have a length the nib can pay for. The guide prices the course it is drawing: past the point where the ink would give out the pricking is starved to almost nothing, and a course the nib cannot reach the end of is barred with a copper stroke and a small ring where it runs out. Such a course is still aimed and still drawn, and may still be flown — the choice is the player's, made knowing where the line stops. A long transfer continues until a planet captures you or you encounter a real hazard, the rising darkness, the side of the chart, or the end of the ink. The aiming guide checks all generated destinations, including distant planets beyond its former 1.9-second horizon. Every full main orbit skipped earns an additional 10 points before the speed multiplier, so at the opening speed skipping three earns 30 and skipping seven earns 70. The landing message reports the skipped count and multiplied bonus; time in flight does not reduce the award.

Every hazard the chart carries is one row of a single table — which way its field turns a flight, how much of its drawn radius kills, the reach of the field, and the loss it reports — rather than a branch in the code that flies through it, so a later chart's own dangers are added to the table instead of threaded through the flight. Three rows are cut so far. **Vorago**, the whirlpool in the aether that the old charts engrave at the edge of the world, has a local field extending 100 world units beyond its radius. Close flybys bend the airborne trajectory toward it, with slower and closer passes turning more strongly. This is a deliberately simplified arcade effect: the field steers the direction while preserving the player's chosen speed. The draw fades smoothly to zero at the edge, and it does not disturb an attached orbit. The dark eye remains lethal; a close graze can still earn its once-per-vortex bonus.

Through the opening regions a hazard is placed clear of every route between two main nodes, so it is scenery to be given room. From row 12, and then only on every third row, one may instead sit on one of the two ways across and shut it, so the side you cross on becomes a decision rather than a formality. What it may never do is close the last way through: whichever side it leaves open is flyable both ways it can be flown, by a smooth tangent for a perfect transfer and by a centre-directed line for a player not yet flying them, and is clear of the whole gravity field rather than merely of the lethal core, since a route inside the field would be bent into the hazard it was drawn around. About one hazard in three closes a route. The slingshot's own shortcut is never touched. From the third region onward, sunspot flares alternate with vortices under the same placement rules. A flare's field has the same reach and the same steering with the sign reversed: it pushes the flight outward instead of drawing it in, again without changing the speed. Only a smaller core, six tenths of the drawn radius, is lethal, and a close pass earns the same once-per-hazard bonus. The aiming guide and real flight agree around a flare exactly as they do around a vortex. A flare is engraved as a sunspot in the Galileo manner: a dark umbra covering exactly the lethal core, a penumbra of fine radial strokes out to the drawn edge, two lesser spots of the same group, and burin rays leaving the limb that breathe slowly with the plate's own time. Everything but the rays is baked into a sprite. The field is drawn at its true radius as a pricked ring with small outward barbs — a repulsion ring, told apart at a glance from the solid cut edge and wound arms of a vortex.

**Ventus**, the wind-head puffing in the margin of every chart of the century, begins at row 20 and appears at most on one row in five. It is the third row of the hazard table and the only one that kills nothing at all: it has no lethal core, it cannot be grazed, and it cannot shut a route, so it is placed the way a nebula is — lying across one of the tangent routes between two main planets — rather than through the placement that lets a lethal hazard close a way across. What it does is blow. Its field is the one that does not lie along a radius: instead of drawing a flight toward a centre or pushing it away, it carries the flight one steady way over the whole of its reach, across the crossing rather than along it, and always toward the middle of the sheet, so a course led into a gust is never led into the chart's own edge. Like the radial fields it steers without touching speed, and one consequence is worth stating plainly: a force lying along the flight has nothing left to turn, so a crossing flown straight down a gust is untouched by it, and one flown across it is carried most. That is why the head is laid across its route rather than down it. What the wind really sets, then, is a turn rate: over a whole gust a crossing at the chart's middle pace is carried about 40 world units off the line it left on, and one at full pace about 20 — most of an orbit's own radius where there is time to read it, and less where there is not. Its reach is shorter than a gravity well's, 55 world units beyond the head rather than 100, which is the thing itself and also a price: any field a course passes through puts the aiming guide onto its curved prediction for that whole frame, so the reach of a hazard met often is bought with the cost of drawing every course near it. The head is grown to the largest whose *whole field* still clears every capture band, drift envelope, other hazard field and cloud around it, between 16 and 34 world units, and is dropped if that is under 16. It is the field and not merely the head that is kept clear: a wind blowing over a capture band would bend the arrival itself, and a landing that cannot be aimed is not a landing — away from one, the bend falls in the middle of the crossing, where it can be led into. The curved guide and real flight agree around a gust exactly as they do around a vortex, and the clear-tangent release ticks are withheld through one, since a tick promising a smooth tangent through a gust would be a lie. It is engraved as the margins engrave it: a cheek-blown profile at the upwind edge of its own field — skull, a cheek puffed round with the breath in it, pursed lips, loose curls, and the shaded side hatched down and to the right as every other body on the plate is hatched — breathing a band of long tapering strokes clean across the field, between two pricked bounds that open at the mouth and close again at the edge, a leaf rather than a ring because what it bounds blows one way instead of standing round a centre. It is the one hazard mark with a direction, so it is cut once blowing along +x and turned where it is drawn, which lets every gust on the plate share one sprite. Everything but the breath is baked; the breath is cut live so it can drift down the wind with the plate's own time, and it stands still under pause and reduced motion.

Nebula patches begin at row 12 and appear at most once every four rows, lying across one of the tangent routes between two main planets. A patch is inert: it neither kills nor pulls, and it never covers a capture band, a drift envelope, or a hazard field. What it does is hide the chart. The pricked guide is cut at the near edge of the cloud and marked with a small ring instead of a landing mark, so the far half of the transfer has to be flown without a preview. The release marks and the perfect-window arcs on the orbit you are leaving are unaffected, and the flight itself crosses the cloud and captures normally. The patch is engraved as a faint stippled haze — a few hundred seeded dots, densest at its heart and faded off with the cube of the distance so the stipple has no edge of its own, chalky at night and dilute sepia on paper — under one mostly lifted contour, and is blitted from a sprite, since nothing about it moves. It is meant to be noticed rather than looked at; what tells the player the chart is hidden is the cut guide and its fogged ring, not the weight of the cloud.

The pace decides how much of a course the pen will set down. At the opening speed the whole transfer is drawn; as the chart's speed is earned the far part of a fast crossing is left unset, the horizon closing from 900 world units to 260 at the chart's top speed. At the opening pace this touches about one aimed transfer in fourteen and hides nothing of an ordinary one; at full pace it cuts nine in ten, leaving about two thirds of a typical crossing drawn. The cut is marked exactly as a nebula's is, with a small fogged ring in place of the landing mark, and the release marks and the perfect-window arcs on the orbit being left are untouched — so what a fast run gives up is knowing where the flight ends, not whether it leaves on a tangent. Speed buys reach and costs sight. The fog is only what is drawn: the aim, the arrival it predicts and the landing behind it are unchanged, which is what lets the guide still be checked against real flight.

The curved aiming guide uses the same 120 Hz steering and swept collision rules as real flight, including moving planets and tangent-perfect arrivals. It stops at the first predicted capture, collision with a lethal hazard, or chart edge; a dangerous path ends in a copper warning mark. Prediction skips force-free straight sections, samples curves into a bounded polyline, and cannot award points or mutate the course. The long-transfer guide remains available.

A vortex winds the actual atlas background and starlight into itself. A reusable 640-pixel canvas snapshots each visible region; concentric sampling bands are each turned further about the dark eye than the one outside them, by the cube of how deep they lie, so the sheet is only just off true where the field begins and fully caught by the time it reaches the dark. The winding settles to nothing at the field boundary, where the chart stands square again. Three arms wound into the eye, each doubled by a fainter one behind it, are cut in the copperplate palette — round rather than flattened, since a whirlpool is charted from above. Targets, orbit markers, the guide, and the player remain sharp and accurately positioned above the distortion. The slow drift of the whirl freezes on pause and is disabled for reduced motion, while the standing winding and the gameplay draw remain.

The darkness starts advancing after 1.5 seconds at 22 world units per second, accelerating by 0.55 units per second of active play up to 150, and takes a quarter of the chart's own growth on top, so the fully developed pursuit reaches about 191 rather than 150 deep in a run. A tide fixed in world units would slacken exactly where the chart opens up; a quarter of the growth keeps the squeeze on a long transfer about what it is on a short one without letting the flood outrun what a skilled run can climb. Its edge stays near the bottom of the view while you ascend, ordinarily trailing the camera by no more than 25 world units. A skipped orbit widens that trailing room: each one banks 55 units of slack, capped at 260 and leaking away at 28 units a second whether spent or not, so a burst of skips can outrun the pursuit and hold it off-screen for a handful of seconds rather than finding it re-painted at the sill the instant the camera catches up. The opening allows roughly ten seconds before idle play loses; later, consistently slow transfers become unsustainable. Speed-building stars and accurate transfers let the player stay ahead. The constellation reprieve still provides four seconds to recover or build speed, a carried Aurora charge buys the same four seconds at the moment the flood would otherwise have taken the run (see the three carried charges below), and pausing freezes the pursuit.

Skill relieves the pressure directly. Each consecutive perfect transfer past the second slows the pursuit by 3%, to a limit of 15% at seven in a row. An ordinary capture resets the streak to nothing. The relief scales the whole advance, so it is worth most against the fully developed pursuit: a run holding a long chain of perfect transfers faces 127.5 units per second where a run of ordinary captures faces 150.

The rising darkness is painted as indigo-black ink soaking into the atlas: overlapping washes, pooled pigment, paper fibres, and fine suspended flecks. A thin copper shoreline sits at the actual loss threshold and becomes clearer as it approaches the player. Completed constellations turn the edge pale gold as it retreats, then the colour settles back as the reprieve ends. Both pigment plates are cached and reused across viewports. Pause freezes the wash, and reduced motion keeps its decorative grain and fringe still while the real boundary continues to advance. The flood carries marginalia along its shoreline, and the gloss keeps off the chart: where it would drift across an orbit ring or a hazard it fades to nothing over a short reach and comes back once it is clear. The marginalia: a Leviathan of three coils, a thrown tail and a spouting head surfaces slowly at his own place along the edge every twenty-seven seconds, cropped by the waterline as he rises and sinks, and an engraved gloss `HIC SUNT DRACONES` drifts with the ink below the line. Both are cut once into sprites in the darkness's own pigment — the monster only bobs and fades, he is never re-engraved — both take the pale gold of a constellation reprieve, and both stand still under pause and reduced motion.

Ocean worlds, cratered moons, ringed giants, fractured ice planets, desert worlds, volcanic planets, and storm giants each have their own procedural surface and size. Restrained verdigris, ochre, warm grey, chalk, and sepia washes sit on a paper-coloured base. On the paper plate the engraving carries the form and the colour is secondary: the body is a dilute wash rather than a printed flat, the hatching and stipple are cut about a third heavier, the wash is about a third weaker, and it runs one to two pixels past the plate in the off-register direction before the burin keyline is printed over it, so the black line always reads on top of the colour. The night plate keeps its original balance. The hatching is laid as a left hand lays it, every stroke running down and to the right and darkening toward the limb, and it is never crossed: on paper the dark limb is deepened with a second set of parallel strokes that curve with the form, concentric with the limb, the way a pen follows a rounded body rather than crossing it. The same slant is kept on the chapter plates, the moon's night side and the eclipsed disc among them. On paper every keyline was first tried in red chalk: a broken sanguine circle a little off true, and a shorter trial arc, show beside the ink wherever the pen did not follow the chalk exactly, and each orbit ring carries the same chalk pass under its ink. On paper the dark side of every body is first laid in as a dilute sepia wash, deepening toward the limb, before a stroke goes on, so the hatch reads as strokes over tone. A cratered world carries a Sidereus Nuncius terminator of its own — a ragged day-and-night line a little right of the middle, the night side laid in behind it and the hatch beginning at the line, so the lit hemisphere is left as clean as Galileo left his — with small crater rims on the dark side catching the light on their far walls, and an earthshine, after the Codex Leicester's account of the old moon in the new moon's arms, that lifts the dark side faintly toward the limb. Its craters are drawn as Galileo drew them, with the sun on the left: a rim, a crescent of shadow on the floor against the wall nearest the sun, the far wall catching the light, and no rays. Uneven pigment pools, dry-brush flecks, dark coastlines, and slightly offset outlines give them a hand-coloured copperplate character. Fine globe meridians and broken survey arcs accompany each specimen. Every seven main nodes include all seven families, in a run-dependent order, with individually seeded terrain and axial tilts. Planet appearance is separate from the orbit's behavior.

Etched surfaces turn slowly beneath fixed lighting and ring shadows. Cloud filaments drift separately; volcanic fissures gently brighten, and ice worlds have a faint polar shimmer. These decorative movements stop when reduced motion is requested or the game is paused. Surfaces, lighting, and weather are generated once per planet and reused as cached layers, with at most 24 planets retained.

The four chapters each have a full illustrated atlas plate. The Quiet opens beside a monumental engraved moon and a smaller companion. The Drift is crossed by a huge tilted ring system and a distant desert moon. The Eclipse has a dark solar disc with a corona of fine copper strokes and heavy bands of ink below. The Deep reveals a large spiral star field built from chalk-like contours and thousands of stippled stars. These distant scenes have distinct indigo, copper, violet-grey, and slate palettes, with a darker central channel for readable foreground play. Coordinate arcs and small plate captions tie them together. The brighter stars have eight-point compass forms.

A scene change is a page turn. The new chapter's plate slides up from below the frame as a fresh sheet, carrying its own dust and its own marginalia, with a soft shadow along its leading edge and its own plate-mark inside it; the old sheet stays where it lies and fades away underneath. Each chapter is also pulled with its own registration offset of a pixel or two, carried in the world-to-plate mapping, so the whole chart — orbits, hazards, the guide, the comet and the rising ink together — sits very slightly off true against the frame as the sheet changes. Nothing moves relative to anything else in the chart, so no distance the player reads changes. The sheet lands after about three seconds and the blend settles on the new chapter over the same span as the old dissolve. Reduced motion cross-fades instead, with no sheet and no movement. The turn briefly writes a large chapter name and plate numeral onto the sheet itself, in the play channel below the HUD band. The line it is written on is chosen once, as the reveal begins: the line under the HUD band, or one of two lower lines when a planet or a hazard already sits across it, so the lettering never jumps while the pen is still writing. That line is then taken into world coordinates, and the chart carries the name exactly as it carries an orbit — a fast ascent slides it down the sheet, and it is held at the edge of the play channel rather than allowed to print into the margin. It is pulled on a small leaf of its own, one soft pass of the sheet's ground feathered to nothing, so the name reads over whatever the chart has scrolled beneath it, the Eclipse's dark disc included. Chapter lettering sits beside the play area on short landscape screens. Node captions ride above their planet but flip below it, clear of the HUD band and of the chapter lettering, when the node is too near the top edge to print them inside the frame. Illustrated scenery shifts gently with the ascent behind two finer dust layers. That whole distance — the illustrated plate, the wash it is knocked back with down the play channel, and both drifting dust layers, everything the ascent carries more slowly than the chart — is a catalogue selection, and a sheet ships with none of it: see **Scenery** under Catalogue. The page turn is not part of it, since the chapter still changes on a bare sheet, so the fresh sheet still rises with its shadow, its cut edge and its own plate-mark whatever is or is not printed on it. Reduced motion keeps the decorative scenery still and removes the chapter lettering's small upward movement; pause freezes transitions. All four illustrations are cached at a fixed resolution, preserve their proportions across viewports, and use a separate random stream from gameplay. The entire game remains procedural and offline.

Quiet ambient events alternate between a small distant comet at the edge of the view and a brief glint on the moon, rings, corona, or spiral arms. A glint picks out a mark the chapter plate has already engraved, at the coordinate that plate engraves it at, so it is only thrown while the chapter plates are the chosen scenery: a bare sheet has no such mark, the rhumb web has none of these, and a counterproof is reversed, so the coordinate no longer says where the mark is. In all three the sheet keeps its comets, which are its own and never the illustration's. Only one can be present at a time, followed by 19–30 seconds of quiet time. Events fade away near playable planets, hazards, the player, the aiming line, and rising darkness. Chapter reveals, constellation celebrations, recent captures, and immediate danger hold back new events. The ambient layer adds no sounds or interactions, uses its own seeded random stream, pauses with the game, clears on restart, and is disabled by reduced motion.

The whole background is held back inside the play channel so the moving parts read first, and left fuller in the margins where nothing is played. The channel is the middle of the sheet, 168 scaled pixels either side of the centre or 30% of the width, whichever is wider, up to half the sheet. The illustrated chapter plate is left whole in the margins and washed back down the channel by a single pass of the sheet's own ground colour at 0.34 at night and 0.30 on paper, at full strength across the channel and fading out over a 60-pixel feather either side, so the contrast under the chart drops by about a third and the transition is graded rather than cut. The two drifting dust plates carry a third fewer marks, 2,150 / 3,500 / 1,950 / 910 far and 650 / 1,100 / 620 / 290 near against 3,300 / 5,400 / 3,000 / 1,400 and 1,000 / 1,700 / 950 / 440, and are printed at 0.40 and 0.62 of their weight at night and 0.17 and 0.27 on paper, where they were 0.54 and 0.84, and 0.22 and 0.34; the region wash beneath them drops from 0.31 to 0.25 at night and from 0.050 to 0.042 on paper. Inside the channel the background star field is set at half the stars, every other one skipped, and 0.55 of the contrast, while the margins keep the whole field at full strength. The Eclipse's radiating corona is cut from 560 strokes to 300, a quarter shorter and at about two fifths of the weight, and its dust shadow from 0.36 to 0.22. The Latin rim caption is lettered round every fourth main orbit rather than every third, at 0.15 rather than 0.24 at night and 0.22 rather than 0.34 on paper. The laid-paper tile multiplies at 0.35 instead of 0.44 on paper and is screened back at 0.055 instead of 0.07 at night.

Nothing printed over the plate shares a line with anything else, and nothing prints through a leaf. The frontispiece is a leaf: while the run is still to be dealt, the chart beneath it carries no captions at all, so no node label, chart name or slingshot caption can show through the title cartouche, and a light veil of the ground sits under the title. The frontispiece and the colophon are leaves: nothing the run has to say is written onto the chart while either is on the page, and the sheet is wiped of it when a run ends. The DOM score, pace and flow sit on a leaf of their own while a run is on, a soft pass of the ground under the middle of the HUD band, feathered to nothing, so the figures never print straight across a planet that has scrolled up beneath them; the pass reaches as deep as the block actually stands, since every charge in hand sets another line under the score. The plate captions and Galileo's marginal figures are set live at the foot of the sheet rather than baked into the print, so a narrow sheet no longer crops them against the frame. The `HIC SUNT DRACONES` gloss and the Leviathan stop rising at the footer band — the bottom 70 CSS pixels, 58 on a short landscape screen, plus whatever safe-area inset the browser reports, and the frame's inner rule above that — so the flood goes on past them but they never reach the chapter name or the buttons. The two upper corner ornaments are cut at 45% of the head and half the breath and tucked further into the corner, clear of the DOM `ORBIT` and `BEST` headings; the two lower corners are unchanged. A chart's caption rides above its star, flips below it and clears the HUD band exactly as a node caption does. The HUD's centre column is taken to reach 150 pixels either side of the middle rather than 104, so a caption set beside the score keeps below the whole band; the caption on the orbit being held, which is always set below its planet so that it cannot cover the release marks, is pushed clear of the band when below would still be inside it. A constellation's name, lettered round the rim of its entry star, turns to the bottom of the rim under the same rule when the star sits too near the top edge to print it inside the frame.

Everything the run has to say is written into the map rather than over it. There is no message bar and no instruction line: each description — a slingshot taken, a transfer landed square, orbits skipped, a chart named as its stars are traced, an observation made, the pressure the opening orbit set — is lettered onto the sheet beside whatever it is about, in the plate's own small caps, on a hairline leader that runs from the subject's rim out to the lettering with a tick left where it started, and ruled underneath as a plate rules a legend. The standing instructions of the opening rows are written the same way in the marginal italic, unruled, beside the orbit being held or the vortex that is bending the flight; each is kept on the sheet while its condition holds. An inscription is set once, in world units, so the chart carries it exactly as it carries the orbit it belongs to: it keeps its place on the sheet as the ascent scrolls beneath it, and it never fades — what the pen has set down is ink, drawn at full strength until the sheet has carried it under the plate's inner rule, where it is cut off as the orbits are. It is placed in whichever of eight positions round its subject is clearest of the chart, of the traveller, of the constructions still being drawn up at either end of the last flight, and of whatever is already written, and then slid back onto the sheet, so it is never set into the frame's margin, into the score band, or into the footer. Lettering is never set over lettering: if none of the eight places is clear the note goes further out on a longer leader, or is stacked above or below whatever stands in its way, and a note beside a wandering orbit is set clear for the whole of its drift, so two notes can never be carried into each other. A standing instruction the sheet has carried off the plate is set again rather than lost, and one that stops being asked for stays where it was written, as ink, rather than being struck out; asked for again while it is still on the plate, it is taken up rather than written twice. The sheet holds at most twenty-four at once and the lowest gives way; the pen writes them a glyph at a time with the nib riding the line, reduced motion prints them whole, a paused run freezes the pen where it stopped, and a proof pulled before letters keeps them, since what the run says is not part of the engraving. The same words go to a live region of their own as they are written, so a reader hears the page. The plate's own number and name are engraved at the foot of the sheet in the frame's ink — `TAB. II · THE DRIFT` — where a printer sets a running head.

Named feats are recorded as they happen, at most once each per run, and inscribed on the chart beside the traveller. Three perfect transfers in a row is Tres Perfecti; five orbits skipped in one flight, Saltus Quinque; reaching the chart's top speed, Velocitas Summa; grazing a vortex at that speed, Periculum; completing a constellation without a single ordinary capture on its route, Linea Pura; reaching row 40, Altitudo; and surviving three minutes, Vigilia. The run's observations are listed under the table on the end screen.

Every landing is surveyed. The arrival angle is the angle between the incoming line and the orbit's radius at the contact, so a line exactly tangent to the drawn ring reads 90 degrees; a smooth entry that joined a little inside or outside the ring reads a few degrees under or over, and a hard turn toward the centre reads far below. An arrival within a degree and a half of 90 is a square: it earns a further 10 points multiplied by arrival speed beside the landing reward, counts toward the ANGULUS RECTUS observation, and the guided perfect band spans roughly 78 to 101 degrees, so about one perfect transfer in seven is a square.

Both ends of a flight are then drawn up as a geometer's construction, and the construction stays on the sheet as dried ink. At the release, over four tenths of a second, the pen lays the radius from the planet's centre out to the release point, the departure line carried thirty world units along the tangent and closed with a small arrowhead, and the release bearing: a dotted north reference from the centre up to the ring, an arc swept clockwise from that north to the release radius with a tick at each end, and its reading in engraved figures set outside the arc, 0 to 359 — `212°`. The bearing figure is kept inside the ring, at about half its radius, so it can never cross the release marks or the perfect window; it is begun at the moment of release, which is the moment the player has already gone. At the landing, over six tenths of a second and in this order, the pen draws the radius from the centre out to the contact, the incoming line taken from the arrival velocity and carried a little past it, and between the two the arrival angle: a small arc with two tick ends and its numeral, `87°`, set outside the angle on its bisector. A square is marked with the geometer's right angle instead of the arc — a small square in the corner with a dot inside it, in gold on the night plate and ochre on paper. A rough impression is marked the opposite way: the incoming line cannot be joined, only arrested, so the nib scuffs sideways at the contact instead of lifting clean and pools where it caught — a small ragged skid and stain, struck in the same ink the landing earns no dividend back from. The points of each construction are lettered as a geometer letters a figure, in the same italic hand: `a` at the centre, `b` at the point on the ring, and `c` at the far end of the line, each written as the pen reaches its point, and set across the line on the side away from the other strokes so they never sit on a stroke. Beside the construction a short note is set in Fell italic: the arrival speed as `×1.4`, the landing reward, `ANGULUS RECTUS · +N` for a square, and `SKIP N` when orbits were skipped. The nib and its wet bead ride the end of whichever stroke is in hand, exactly as they do everywhere else the pen works; reduced motion prints both constructions whole, a paused run freezes one mid-stroke, and every stroke is cut in the ink the pen is charged with. The constructions are bounded, pruned as the camera climbs and cleared when a new run is dealt, precisely as the dried route is, and at most 48 are held. The marginal score note is unchanged and the score is never repeated in the construction. A square also inscribes `RIGHT ANGLE · +N` on the orbit it was landed on, when nothing with a stronger claim is already there, under two short tones.

Each capture starts at 10 points. Releasing before 1.25 turns increases flow, up to ×5; each flow step adds 2 points per capture. Every accepted arrival receives that base instead of losing the whole landing below sixteen degrees. From sixteen degrees to a tangent at ninety, the angle adds a continuous 0–10 points; a smooth perfect transfer adds 5 more, each full main node skipped adds 10, and a gold detour adds 15. These capture rewards are multiplied by arrival speed divided by 150, from ×1.0 to ×2.4, with each component rounded to whole points. At twice the speed, the same transfer earns twice the points. The current multiplier and angle award appear with the landing reward. Constellation completions remain a fixed 60 points, and grazing a lethal hazard remains 5 once per hazard.

The end screen's table reports orbits, perfect transfers, best flow, and the row reached. The highest row of any run is kept separately from the score, and a run that beats it is marked with a note under the score. Local browser storage holds the best score in `orbit.best.v1`, the highest row in `orbit.bestRow.v1`, the sound preference in `orbit.sound.v1`, the difficulty in `orbit.difficulty.v1`, the chosen plate in `orbit.plate.v1`, the daily plate's record in `orbit.daily.v1` as `{"date","best"}`, and the almanac of drawn daily plates in `orbit.dailyLog.v1`.

The player is drawn as the pen itself: an engraved quill whose nib leads, cut to a point at the traveller's exact position and turned along the flight, with the stripped barrel and then the feather trailing behind it. The barrel is bared where a hand would hold it and printed as two outlines with the sheet showing through, which is the only way an engraver had of saying that a thing was translucent. The vane is laid twice off one curve — once as the wash its barb tips enclose, once as the barbs themselves, thirty a side, each leaving the rachis across it and swept back along it so the plume closes to its tip instead of standing off the shaft like the teeth of a comb — and it is cut broad on one side of the rachis and narrow on the other, which is what makes it a flight feather rather than a leaf. A feather does not grow when the hand moves faster, so the plume keeps very nearly its own length whatever the flight is doing and answers to speed in the flex of the vane instead: it flexes back as the flight quickens and breathes a little, and is held still under reduced motion. The nib is one taper from the barrel to the cut point, with the slit and the vent that stopped it splitting further, keylined light-on-dark so the moving point stays legible over a pale planet; and on paper a ring of reserved, unprinted sheet separates the whole pen from the vane. A bead of wet ink at the point brightens with the charge in hand. The whole route the run has flown stays on the chart as dried ink beneath the wet trail: a world-space line, one wash pass and three weights of burin stroke, heavier where the flight was faster, so a slingshot orbit leaves a ring and a long transfer a straight rule. It is bounded twice — everything that has passed below the sheet is dropped as the camera climbs, and a hard cap of three thousand points holds the rest — cleared when a new run is dealt, kept under reduced motion since a line already on the page is not motion, and frozen with a paused run because nothing is sampled. Its ink is registered per plate and follows the chosen trail ink. The wet trail follows the actual flight path, tapers with age, dries into the route at its head so the two are one line, and broadens subtly at slingshot speeds. It is laid as a section rather than as an outline: five passes on a ramp from the damp margin inward, each narrower and stronger than the last, so what accumulates is one gradient across the stroke's width — dilute and dull where it soaks into the sheet, full ink down the middle — and not a pale band with a dark line dropped on top of it, which the eye reads as two marks overlapping rather than as one. The margin is an even band and the core alone carries the nib's thick and thin, since what wicks into the paper spreads by the paper's own capillary action and knows nothing about how the nib is held; a dry-brush edge is the section leaning to one side as the stroke runs out, not a second line ruled beside it. The pieces the trail is sampled into are laid end to end rather than lapped over one another, because a round cap on a translucent stroke paints every joint twice and beads the line at every sample. Each segment of a liquid medium is laid wet and dries as it ages: glossy blue-black to matte sepia on paper, bright to dim ink at night. A dry one — chalk, a metal stylus, leaf — has no wet stage to lose and arrives at its settled tone the instant it touches, which is one of the nine things an ink's substance decides about it (see Catalogue below). A bead of ink pools where the player let go and dries lighter over about a second and a half. Captures leave a broken etched ripple at the planet; perfect transfers add a second ring, survey ticks, and a small fan at the contact point. Scores are written up as marginal notes in Fell italic beside the play field, each with a small engraved manicule pointing back in at the event. Effects pause with the run. Reduced motion shortens the trail and removes decorative filaments from the trail, particle bursts, expanding ripples, the creep of the pricked guide, the pooling of a release blot, marginal-note drift, and screen flashes. Trail history stays bounded and is cleared on restart.

The game fills the available viewport and requests the browser's Fullscreen API on the first tap when supported. The corner control toggles fullscreen. On browsers without that API, including some iPhone browsers, the game uses the full available dynamic viewport. Switching away pauses the run; tap to resume.

A run can also be set down deliberately. While one is in hand the footer carries a pause control of its own, standing exactly where the catalogue's button stands when no run is in progress, so the row of marks in that corner keeps its count either way. It is engraved as an **hourglass**, since every other mark in that row is an object the century actually had and the one the century used for a held moment is the glass: keylined in the same hairline as the speaking-trumpet, the book and the sheet beside it, its sand stopped in both bulbs, and carrying the same faint off-register ghost of itself that every other mark carries. The control halts a run and takes it up again, and is read as a switch the way the sound control beside it is. Escape does the same from the keyboard.

Either raises the pause leaf. A halt is not the end of a run, so it is not pulled on the colophon's full leaf: it is the same paper inside the same double engraved rule, cut to a slip, and the plate is left legible around and faintly through it rather than flooded — the press has stopped with the sheet still on it. On the slip, in the order the colophon uses: a small-caps line saying what has happened — `THE PRESS STANDS IDLE` — the plate's own word for the halt, `Suspended.`, the title rule, the breathing italic that every screen in the atlas prompts a tap with, and then the two things that can be done with a run held in hand, ruled as a table of two equal entries rather than set loose as chips: `TAKE UP THE PEN`, which is also still what a tap anywhere on the sheet does, or `RETURN TO THE FRONTISPIECE`, which sets the plate aside unfinished. The halt is spoken as well as printed, in the same word.

Leaving is not a death: nothing is scored and no colophon is pulled, the frontispiece simply comes back up with a fresh chart dealt behind it. What the run did is still folded into the ledger, exactly as it is when the page is switched away from, so the orbits already flown are never lost with the sheet and anything the run earned waits for the next colophon to name it. Every word on the slip belongs to the plate rather than to the interface, and no century is made to borrow the atlas's workshop for it: era II holds the hours still, rests the barque, and offers to take up the course or to leave the voyage, its slip framed top and bottom in the wall's own block rule as every panel on that wall is; era I, where nothing is printed and no frontispiece stands behind the wall, stays the hand and offers to take up the crayon or to leave the wall.

The sheet is cut for the hand and not for the desk. **A phone held upright is the plate the atlas is drawn for — an iPhone 15 Pro Max, 430 by 932 points in portrait, is the reference sheet every size in the game is set against.** The chart's scale factor is `min(width/440, height/780)`, which stands at about 0.98 there, so a radius or a weight written in the source is very nearly the printed measure on the target. A wide screen is welcome and fully served, and it earns the marginalia the flanks have room for — the compass rose, the scale bar, the engraver's line and the MAGNITUDINES key — but nothing on the chart itself is gauged for it. Where a size reads well at arm's length on a laptop and badly in the hand, the hand wins.

A real new run opens on three parallel targets, side by side in place of the usual single first node, each captioned in the atlas's own Latin rather than a modern difficulty label: **Tiro** the raw recruit, **Adeptus** the one who has attained, and **Magister** the master. Each is drawn as its own kind of world so the choice reads before its caption is even legible — Tiro a calm ocean world with a verdant ink accent, Adeptus the plainest ringed world in the atlas's ordinary ink, and Magister a volcanic world ringed in rubrication red — and all three are revealed at once so every choice is visible before the player commits. The opening orbit itself is held at a gentler pace than ordinary play, and the rising darkness does not advance at all while the choice is pending, so there is no rush to read the three worlds before aiming. Course generation likewise waits for the choice; capturing one sets the run's pressure, removes the other two, restores the ordinary pace, and continues exactly as an ordinary capture would. The choice scales how quickly the rising darkness accelerates thereafter (×0.72, ×1, ×1.35), is remembered in local storage for the next visit, and every other rule stays the same. The daily plate and every fixed course skip the offer and open on the ordinary single node.

Beside them is the daily plate. Switched on, the run's seed comes from a stable hash of the current UTC date, so everyone playing on the same day is given the same chart and every attempt that day repeats it. The daily plate is always played at Adeptus pressure, and its best is kept separately from the ordinary best under that date, so a daily attempt never disturbs the record for the free courses. The title and end screens read `Tabula diei` with the date, and the end screen offers a copy button that puts a line like `Orbit · Tabula diei 2026-09-03 · 420 points · row 31 · 2 constellations` on the clipboard where the browser allows it. The toggle itself is not remembered between visits: a new visit always opens on the ordinary chart.

The daily plate is a showcase as well as a fixed course: the same date hash that deals its chart also draws its whole look, one entry from every cosmetic category — the plate itself among them — in place of whatever the ledger has actually unlocked and the player has actually chosen for ordinary play. Today's sheet may therefore be pulled in a colourway, a trail, an observer's mark, a frame ornament, a figure hand, a construction or a distance the player has never earned, and everyone who opens today's plate is shown the same one. None of it is granted for being shown: the catalogue's own selections are never read or written while the daily's setup is on the press, so leaving the daily puts back exactly the plate and cosmetics the player had before, earned or not.

The daily plate's own seed reaches past its look and into the chart itself. Every other course opens the same way whatever it was dealt — the second and third planets always at the same two points, the first four regions always carrying the Needle, the Sail, the Lyre and the Crown in that order — so a run's opening is recognisable however far its later regions have shuffled. The daily plate keeps none of that fixed: its seed places the second and third planets itself, exactly as it already places every later one, and draws all twelve regions' figures from one shuffle of the whole catalogue rather than holding the first four back from it, so no two days need open alike and no figure is owed its place before the twelve are used.

Beside the switch on the frontispiece is the **ephemeris**, an almanac leaf of the daily plates: a ruled month table in the same hand as the catalogue, headed with the Latin month and the year in Roman numerals and its columns with the days of the planetary week, `Sol` through `Sat`. A day is written into the log the moment a daily run begins on it, and only while it is still that day; the ephemeris then prints that square as an entry in gold, the day's numeral over its best score, and choosing it deals that day's plate again from that date's own seed at the same Adeptus pressure. Every other square is printed too, and closed: a past day that was never drawn is a numeral over a blank rule, a day still to come is a numeral alone. Today's square is always open, captioned `hodie` until it has been drawn, and the plate now in hand is pricked with a small lozenge. A repeat says so — `Tabula diei 2026-09-01 · iterum` on the title and end screens, `(iterum)` in the copied line — and never enters a day in the log or touches the current day's record, though it does improve the record of the day it repeats. The leaf turns between the month of the earliest plate on record and the current month and no further, opens on the month of whichever plate is in hand, holds the gameplay input while it is open, and closes on its own button or on Escape. The log is one JSON document under `orbit.dailyLog.v1`, a map of date to `{"best","plays"}`; a day that predates it is folded in once from `orbit.daily.v1`, and a blocked or malformed store simply reads as an empty almanac, in which case today's plate is the only one open.

A further switch beside the pressures and the daily plate puts real gravity into the traveller's own flight rather than only into the chart's dangers. **Newtonian gravity** — *Vis Gravitatis*, captioned in the atlas's own Latin exactly as the pressures and the carried charges are — is earned rather than offered outright: 500 lifetime perfect transfers and 25 grazed vortices, together, at any pressure and over any number of runs. Where a vortex or a flare only ever steers a flight without touching its speed, every main body on the chart now pulls the traveller with a real acceleration once it is clear of the ring it was released from, falling in on the approach and giving back on the way past almost exactly what it took, so a close pass earns real speed at the cost of a harder line to hold and a wide one costs almost nothing. The pull reaches four fifths of a body's own radius beyond its drawn rim and fades to nothing at that edge, the same shape a vortex's own field fades on, so the chart is never far from some body's reach but never wrapped in one further than its size warrants either. What it never touches is the orbit itself: capture, the tangent release and its window, and every point the score is built from stay exactly what they have always been, so a Newtonian chart asks for a truer aim through a bent approach, not a different instrument to fly it with. The bend also spends the nib faster than the straight line it replaces, since the ink is billed by the distance actually flown rather than by the time it takes. The switch stacks with whichever of Tiro, Adeptus or Magister is chosen and keeps a record of its own, apart from the three pressures' own entries, exactly as the daily plate keeps a record under its own name rather than the ordinary one; it plays no part under the daily plate, which keeps its own fixed setup, or inside either century's own door, which draws its own flight regardless of the switch.

The frontispiece carries two further doors beside the pressures and the ephemeris, and neither leaves the game: `ERA I · THE ROCK` and `ERA II · THE CEILING` each put a whole century on the press instead of dealing another chart of this one. The atlas the rest of this file describes is itself an era — the fifth, the Renaissance engraving — and the two beside it are the same simulation drawn by another hand: the same seeded generation, the same swept collisions, the same tangents, the same release window a seventh of a second wide. What changes is the material and everything said in it. A century is entered and left from the frontispiece alone, never out from under a run in progress, and it keeps its own record rather than writing the atlas's; the plate on the press when it was entered is put back on the way out. See [The eras](#the-eras).


Three carried charges are captioned in the atlas's own Latin rather than a modern power-up label, the way Tiro, Adeptus and Magister are, and between them they answer the three ways a run is taken from outside the nib: a hazard, the chart's own edge, and the rising dark. None of them is read by its colour. A pale blue disc and a violet one are the same mark at arm's length, and by the time the caption lettered beside one is legible the fork it hangs off is already behind the traveller — so each wears the device of the thing it does, engraved around the body in the plate's own burin, and cut with the pen reaching the page rather than with the observation, exactly as a slingshot's charge band and every caption are. What a body will give the run is the whole reason to leave the main line for it, so it is owed to the player before the orbit is taken rather than after.

A pale blue **Scutum** star appears roughly every ten main rows, off the main line like the gold detours, engraved with the heater shield Hevelius cut for Scutum Sobiescianum — the chief flat, the flanks falling to a point, the second fainter pass inside it where the hand went round twice. Capturing one arms a single charge, shown at the top of the screen and as a faint ring around the ship. The next contact with a lethal hazard consumes the charge and deflects the flight outward instead of ending the run; only one charge can be carried at a time.

A violet **Repulsa** star appears roughly every thirteen main rows, off the main line the same way, engraved with the geometer's own figure of a line thrown back from a bound: the margin ruled twice and hatched outward at one side, a line coming in at it and leaving again under an open chevron, and the body itself standing in the mouth of the V. Capturing one arms a charge of its own, shown beneath the Scutum line and as a broken, wider ring around the ship, so the two read apart at a glance even when both are carried together. The next time the flight would leave the chart's edge, the charge turns it back into the chart instead of ending the run — the sideways velocity is reversed and the traveller is set a little clear of the margin — rather than deflecting off a hazard; only one charge can be carried at a time. Without a charge in hand, the same exit is still a loss, spilling ink at the edge the flight left by rather than the ordinary burst.

The third is the rarest, and answers the loss most runs actually end on. An **Aurora** star — the dawn, engraved as a sun coming up out of the flood, its spokes struck long and short off the body above a ruled waterline, and inked in minium on the paper plate — is cut on the eighteenth main row and every thirty-second row after that, off the main line the same way. It begins no earlier because what it answers is the pressure a deep run is flying against rather than a mistake an opening row can make, and its rows are counted from the eighteenth rather than off a bare multiple because the flank it hangs off is the one the gold detour and the other two charges already use: a plain nineteenth or twentieth row would have offered it only where a fork or a satellite was standing in the single place it could go, and the chart would have dealt it almost never. Capturing one arms a charge shown beneath the Repulsa line and as twelve short spokes struck outward from the ship, so the three read apart by what kind of mark they are and not only by how wide they stand. The moment the rising darkness would take the traveller the charge is spent instead: the flood is driven 230 world units back down the sheet at once, measured from the traveller rather than from the waterline so the charge is worth the same whether the dark took a flight that had outrun it or an orbit it had crept up under, and far enough that the pen is clear of it on the very frame it would have been caught. It is then held off for four seconds, exactly as a completed constellation holds it off. The edge is ordinarily not allowed to trail more than 25 world units below the camera, so the charge grants its own trailing room to open that floor, above anything a burst of skipped orbits can bank — this is the one credit that has to be enough on its own rather than adding to what the run already had in hand, and a skip flown while it still stands never cuts it back to the skip cap. Only one charge can be carried at a time, and it is spent on the dark alone: a vortex, the chart's edge and a dry nib all still end the run.

A landing below the sixteen-degree bonus threshold leaves a rough impression: it still earns the capture's base points, but no angle bonus, and its hand-applied colour is displaced and turned increasingly far out of register from the engraved keyline as the arrival approaches the radius. A lifetime total of these rough impressions unlocks five more trail inks, common pigments first and precious ones last — **Umber**, **Woad**, **Vermilion**, **Malachite**, and finally **Ultramarine**, ground from lapis lazuli and once worth more than gold, at 10, 25, 75, 200 and 500 rough impressions.

A warm brown inkwell appears roughly every seventeen main rows, engraved with the device of the pot itself — the rim it is filled to, the belly the specimen stands inside, and a nib dipped in from the right, since a well is only a well because something is dipped into it. It always scores as an ordinary orbit, whether or not it pays out anything further. What it pays out depends on the streak standing when it is reached: three or more captures in a row, each one either landed below the sixteen-degree graze or a flight that skipped an orbit to get there, still earns a fresh colour for the trail; any capture in between that does neither breaks the streak, and an inkwell reached on a clean run simply runs dry. Filling enough of them on a standing streak earns the two hardest trail inks in the catalogue: **Bistre**, a warm sooty brown, and then **Orpiment**, the old illuminators' bright and faintly dangerous yellow-orange.

## Plates

A cached laid-paper tile is pressed over the finished frame in one fill per frame: laid wires every 1.5 pixels, chain lines every 27, and short fibres. On the paper plate it multiplies over everything, so every stroke breaks across the wires instead of lying on top of them; at night the same tile is screened back at a whisper. The score band and the screens are DOM and are unaffected; the inscriptions are on the plate and take the tile with everything else.

The atlas can be printed on either of two plates, chosen with the page icon in the footer and remembered in local storage. The **night plate** is the original artwork: pale ink and starlight on indigo. The **paper plate** is the same chart pulled on aged cream laid paper, with iron-gall sepia and black ink, ochre where the night plate uses gold, rubrication red-brown where it uses copper, and Prussian blue for drifting orbits. The sheet carries laid and chain lines, fibres, foxing, and old tide marks. On both plates the astronomical construction is earned rather than supplied as backdrop: captures first prick the pole, then sweep the celestial equator and oblique ecliptic, leave two pale trial circles, add parallels and meridians, and finally cut 120 edge graduations, twelve Roman hour marks and the Latin labels `ÆQUATOR CÆLESTIS` and `ECLIPTICA`. Which figure those ten stages construct is itself a catalogue selection, and it may equally be none at all: see **Constructions** under Catalogue. On paper the planets are hand-coloured specimens whose registration records arrival quality, with hatched night sides; vortices are pooled ink blots, and the rising darkness is literally spilled ink bleeding up the page along the fibres. Every render section registers its colours for both plates in one place. Switching plates rebuilds the cached artwork and never touches the simulation.

The Renaissance sheet also carries a proper impressum cartouche. Its place, workshop, plate number and sheet signature are reserved from the first pull; the year, title, correction mark, engraver's mark, dedication, privilege and exact Daily Run date fill their own fixed lines as the ledger earns them. The cartouche is world-anchored like every other engraving, so the fixed playfield gains no follow-camera overlay: when the camera rises, the already engraved cartouche moves down with the sheet and leaves below the inner rule.

Both plates sit inside an engraved frame: a plate-mark where the copper pressed the sheet, a heavy double rule, and a graduated scale with right-ascension hours along the top and bottom and declination degrees down the sides that scroll with the ascent. A cherubic wind-head is cut into each of the four corners, a few dozen burin strokes each, seeded so no two are the same head, blowing along the diagonal into the chart at the margin's own tone. Wide screens add a compass rose in the left flank with `SEPTENTRIO · ORIENS · MERIDIES · OCCIDENS` lettered round its ring, a scale bar, an engraver's credit, and a MAGNITUDINES key to the six star forms, all in the margins outside the play channel. The frame is cached and costs one blit per frame.

Every engraved stroke in the atlas is cut by the same pair of burin primitives: the width swells and tapers along the run, the path wobbles by a fraction of a pixel, and the line skips where the tool lifted. They draw orbit rings, lit constellation routes, planet keylines, capture ripples and perfect-transfer rings, vortex edges and their wound arms, and the plate frame's double rule. Static shapes are baked once into sprites or into the cached frame layer and blitted; the strokes drawn live each frame use short segment counts. Each orbit ring also carries a second faint hairline pass where the hand went round twice.

Every mark on the chart is drawn as the traveller reaches it. A render-side map keyed by element identity holds when the pen began each mark and how long it takes; it reads the simulation clock, so pausing freezes the pen mid-stroke, and reduced motion prints the whole sheet at once. The cartographer works in view: a planet, hazard or chart begins as soon as it has entered the top of the view by 24 world units and is finished within a second and a quarter — the orbit ring by six tenths of that, the caption a glyph every 40 ms afterwards. The view keeps the traveller well below its top, so the next ring is closed before the player can reach it even on a long transfer at full speed. At most three marks are in hand at once, and anything already inside the view is begun immediately, so a fork with two routes and three stars queues instead of stuttering while nothing that can kill you is ever invisible. Rings, their capture bands and their ticks are cut by a wedge opening from the ring's own start angle, with a bead of wet ink at the leading end. A planet is the one thing on the sheet the pen does not finish on its own. Two clocks run over it and they answer different questions. The first is the pen reaching the page, and everything a trajectory depends on rides it — the orbit ring, its capture band and ticks, the release marks, the charge band of a slingshot star and every caption. The second is what the orbit has actually observed, and the drawing of the body itself rides that instead. Until an orbit has taken it a planet is a phenomenon rather than a specimen: a single point of light inside a ring that is not engraved but only set down — gone round more than once, well off true and open in more places than a cut ring is ever left — so the sheet carries a position and a magnitude and nothing about what the thing is. Taking the orbit is what turns the light into a body: the disc is laid in over a third of a second, a circle gone round once by a hand that was not being careful, grained with the tooth of the sheet and dirty at the rim, because a first sight of a thing is never a clean drawing of it, and the ring closes to the cut one behind it. Two thirds of a turn held around it is a full observation, and the specimen is then drawn on over that arc, starving the first sight away behind it, staged as a colourist works: the keyline cut around by angle over the first two fifths, the hatching swept in by a band travelling along the strokes, the wash bloomed as an irregular blot from a seeded point off centre under a wet rim that dries lighter over the last third, the survey arcs and rings last and closing exactly on completion. Let go early and the body keeps what was drawn of it, frozen at the fraction the orbit reached, for as long as the sheet carries it; die holding one and it stands at what the last orbit had observed. That fraction is read from the world directly rather than through the pen's own queue, so a body already paid for is never blanked by a fourth mark entering the view. The opening choice is the one exception: its three pressures are cut as three kinds of world so that the choice reads before its caption is legible, and all three are therefore drawn in full before a single orbit has been flown. Vortices, flares, wind-heads and nebulas are a drop of ink that spreads through a ragged mask and darkens before the arms are scratched in by wedge. Cached figure layers sweep along the spine through their three stars, and a route line is held back above whichever planet is still being drawn. A nib on a hairline shaft, with the occasional seeded fleck of spatter, rides the leading end of whichever stroke is in progress, and one very quiet scratch of the brushed noise marks its start. A used orbit is struck through with a single diagonal hairline over 300 ms before it dries to a hairline, and the plate frame draws itself once per run: the double rule by dash offset, then the graduated ticks around the perimeter, then the marginal ornaments, over 1.4 seconds on the opening sheet and half a second on a restart. Only a mark still being drawn costs anything; a finished one is drawn exactly as it always was, and the simulation is never told any of this is happening.

The chapter lettering is written rather than printed. `scripts/glyphs.mjs` reads the Fell faces out of the full stylesheet with fontkit, flattens and simplifies the capitals, figures, middle dot and solidus the plates actually set, and writes `src/glyphs.js`: advance widths and contours at 512 units to the em, about twenty kilobytes, committed, so neither the runtime nor the build nor the workflow ever needs the dependency. At runtime each letter's contours are stroked on by dash offset with a bead of wet ink at the nib and its counters then flood with ink, about 120 ms a letter and overlapping, and the rule beneath draws outward from its centre. Once the writing is finished — and always under reduced motion — the ordinary text rendering takes over, unchanged.

The same hand writes the DOM. A changed score, pace, flow or shield line is wiped in with a small nib crossing it; the frontispiece's title settles letter by letter while the cartouche draws itself around it; the colophon rules its leaf, its score frame and its table before the numbers are written into them. Every base style is the finished state and every animation starts from the unwritten one, so the reduced-motion rule already in the stylesheet prints all of it at once.

Lettering also runs on curves, set glyph by glyph and turned tangent to the arc, with a letter-spacing of its own: browser text metrics are used where the canvas provides them and an average advance where it does not. Three things are lettered this way. Every third main orbit carries a Latin caption round its outer rim in small caps at a whisper, drawn from a small seeded vocabulary — `ORBITA · TABULA · MOTUS · SILENTIUM · ASCENSUS · VIGILIA` — and printed only on orbits the player is not holding, so it can never cross the release marks. A live constellation's name is engraved round the rim of its entry star. The compass rose's four winds are named round its ring.

The background stars are set in six magnitude classes derived from each star's own brightness and size: a plain dot for the sixth, a ringed dot for the fifth, then four-, six- and eight-pointed forms, and a haloed eight-point for the first. The first magnitude is rare, a handful of stars in the field.

Lettering uses IM Fell English and IM Fell English SC, digitisations of the Fell types cut in the 1600s and used at Oxford, under the SIL Open Font License. Every font in the game is set through one of three registered faces — the roman, its italic, and the small caps — named on the plate beside its colours and read by `plateFace()` on the canvas and by `--face-text`, `--face-sc` and `--face-body` in the stylesheet, so no size or family is written out at the place the text is drawn. The faces are shipped cut down: `assets/fonts.source.css` holds them as their publisher drew them, all two hundred and thirty characters, and `scripts/fonts.mjs` writes `assets/fonts.css` containing the same three faces with only the hundred and two characters the atlas can actually set — every character in the source, plus printable ASCII whole — which is a third of the weight for a sheet that reads identically. The source stylesheet stays in the repository and is never served, because a subset cannot be widened back out. The title screen is an engraved frontispiece with a strapwork cartouche, a Latin subtitle, and a printer's line; the end screen is a colophon with a ruled table of the run, pulled on its own leaf — a near-opaque paper panel inside a double engraved rule, so every line stays legible however far the spilled ink has risen behind it.

A star of a constellation fork is not a world and is never drawn as one. The printed atlas treats it as a point of light, punched as one of the six traditional magnitude classes read off the star's own assumed brightness: a plain prick for the sixth and the fifth, a four-rayed sign for the fourth, the same under a fine circle for the third, six rays and an outer circle for the second, and eight alternating long spokes and short ticks for the rare first. The six forms are cut at the gauge a sheet held in the hand needs rather than the smallest one a plate could carry — the class radii run from 3.2 to 13 against an orbit ring of about 48 — the spokes are struck from outside the punch and carry well past it, both class circles ride outside the spokes so ray and ring stay two marks, and on the pale sheet the ochre point is bedded on a fine dark ring first, the way a gilder cuts his line before the leaf goes into it. The MAGNITUDINES key in the margin holds the same six forms at the smaller size its twelve-point rule has room for. A point is lettered with its Greek letter only once the orbit has classified it: about 160 degrees of observation for an ordinary star, and almost a whole one for the occasional faint and uncertain point.

A slingshot star is the one specimen the catalogue has no class for. The atlas has watched this one flare before — Tycho's star of 1572, Kepler's of 1604 — so it is engraved as a stella nova rather than given a fixed magnitude: a modest ember at rest that brightens lap over lap as it is orbited and bursts near a full charge, reading by the same charge the instrument band around it fills with, so the specimen and the gauge always agree. Nothing in it emits light, because a plate has no lamp in it. It blazes by the four things a burin can actually do — a reserve of bare sheet left round the point, long spokes alternating with short ticks thrown off it, up to three coronas rung around it, and sparks flecked onto the ground beyond — and it spends more of all four the fuller the lap runs, while every mark of it is kept inside the charge band so the star never crowds the instrument measuring it.

Each constellation fork carries the figure it is named for, drawn behind its three stars in the manner of Hevelius. All twelve catalogue entries are cut: a sailmaker's needle threaded through the stars; a lateen sail on its spar; a chelys lyre; a jewelled diadem; a pair of dividers hinged on the top star, gripping its lead at the middle one, with a graduated sector arc across the opening; an hourglass whose waist pinches at the middle star and whose plates rest on the outer two; a serpent whose body crosses the spine at each star and rears into a head above; a ship with its keel deepest at the bottom star, its mast stepped on the middle one and its truck on the top; an astrolabe whose graduated limb rings the middle star and whose alidade carries a sighting vane on each of the others; a quill cut to a nib at the bottom star, opening its vane at the middle one; a hanging lantern whose flame at the middle star throws its light past the glass; and a moth with its wings either side of the middle star and its feathered antennae on the top one. Each figure is mirrored onto the side its fork branches from. All are broken ink contours that hatch in as stars are visited, take a dilute wash on completion, and fade to hairlines when a chart expires. Each is cached per chart and stays clear of the orbit rims. A generic asterism is kept only as a fallback for a name the plate has no engraving for.

The chapter plates quote Galileo. The Quiet's moon has a Sidereus Nuncius terminator with crater rims catching light on the dark side; The Drift carries his 1610 sketch of Saturn as a disc with two handles beside the caption; The Eclipse shows sunspot groups with hatched penumbrae across the disc; The Deep notes Jupiter and the Medicean stars as a small circle and four asterisks. Each plate has a second caption line naming the figure.

## Catalogue

Everything the atlas records is kept in one JSON document under `orbit.ledger.v1`: lifetime `captures` and `perfects`, the best `bestFlow` and `bestRow`, `constellations` as a map of figure name to completions, `deepestChapter` and `deepestHardcoreChapter`, `grazes`, `shieldsSpent`, `reflectorsSpent`, `dawnsSpent`, `maxSpeedSlings` (slingshot stars left at the chart's top speed), `inkwellsFound` (inkwells filled while a reckless streak still stood), `badAngles` (lifetime rough impressions below the angle-bonus threshold), `runs` and `personalBests` as maps keyed by difficulty with daily runs counted under `daily` and Newtonian runs under `newton` regardless of the pressure they were flown at, `playSeconds`, `observations` as a map of key to count, and `allFourInOneRun`. The run's own figures are tallied in memory from the same simulation events the screens are drawn from, and folded into the document when the run ends and whenever the page is hidden, so a run that is never finished still counts what it did; a second fold adds only what has happened since the first. Blocked storage and a document that is not JSON are ordinary conditions: the ledger falls back to an empty one and the game plays exactly as it does with a full one. The two records that predate it, `orbit.best.v1` and `orbit.bestRow.v1`, are folded in once on the first load and left where they are — the best score becomes the personal best for the difficulty last played.

Storage keys are therefore `orbit.best.v1`, `orbit.bestRow.v1`, `orbit.sound.v1`, `orbit.tutorialSeen.v1` (whether the frontispiece's full instructions have already been shown once), `orbit.difficulty.v1`, `orbit.newton.v1` (whether the Newtonian-gravity switch is on, honoured only once earned), `orbit.plate.v1`, `orbit.daily.v1`, `orbit.dailyLog.v1` (the days whose daily plate was drawn on the day itself, as a map of date to `{best, plays}`), `orbit.ledger.v1`, `orbit.cosmetics.v1` (the chosen cosmetics as `{plate, mark, trail, capture, frame, figures, sphere, scenery}`), and `orbit.initials.v1` (three letters). The plate is written to both `orbit.plate.v1` and the cosmetics document, and `orbit.plate.v1` wins when the two disagree; a selection naming something the ledger has not earned is never honoured. The cosmetics document keeps its version across the two categories added to it, because it is read one category at a time against the list of categories that exist now: a document written before `sphere` and `scenery` existed simply has nothing to say about them and they open at their defaults, where bumping the key would have thrown away the choices it does hold.

Fifty-two entries can be earned. Six plates: the **Cellarius plate**, gold leaf on a deep blue ground, at 1,000 lifetime captures; the **Verdigris plate**, green-tinted copper on dark, for reaching chapter IV, The Deep, at Magister pressure; the **Foxed plate**, a sheet left a further century in a damp room, at 100 runs; the **Proof before letters**, the paper plate pulled with no captions, labels, numerals or legend at all, for tracing four constellations in one run; the **Carta azzurra**, blue prepared paper as the Florentine workshops made it, on which the night plate's pale ink becomes white heightening over a blue-grey ground, for grazing 25 vortices; and the **Sepia plate**, the whole chart in one brown ink as Galileo washed his moons — no hand-colouring, no rubrication, no Prussian blue, every body a sepia wash under a sepia line on the cream of the sheet — for tracing twelve constellations in all. Seven observer marks: the **comet** the atlas used to be travelled by, at 25 runs, and then a Galilean **telescope**, a **moth**, and **Saturn with handles** as Galileo drew it — three bodies and no ring, since three bodies is what his glass showed — at 100, 500 and 1,500 perfect transfers; and three more that are the observing rather than the sky, each hung off a figure of its own rather than a fourth rung of the same ladder: the **cross-staff** an angle was actually taken with, for tracing 25 constellations in all; the engraver's **burin** this plate was cut with, at 2,500 lifetime captures; and the **moon** as Galileo washed it, at best row 50. The quill is the default and needs no earning. Twelve trail inks: red-chalk **sanguine**, **silverpoint** with a faint travelling shimmer, **gold leaf** laid over a dark keyline, and **quicksilver**, which never actually dries, at 10, 50, 200 and 500 slingshot stars left at top speed; five more for a lifetime of rough impressions — **umber**, **woad**, **vermilion**, **malachite** and **ultramarine** — at 10, 25, 75, 200 and 500 of them; two more, hardest of all, sooty **bistre** and the old illuminators' **orpiment**, at 3 and 10 inkwells filled while a reckless streak still stood; and, alone of the twelve, calcined **Bologna stone**, which glows for a moment on the dark plate before it settles back to ash, at 50 grazed vortices. Three capture marks — a compass **rose**, a wax **seal**, and a printer's **manicule** — at 250, 1,000 and 5,000 lifetime captures. Three frame ornaments — interlaced **strapwork**, **acanthus** scrolls, and **sea monsters** in the two lower corners — at best rows 20, 40 and 60. Two figure styles, the finer and more geometric **Bayer** hand at ten completions of any single constellation and the heavier, far more shaded **Bode** hand at twenty-five. Three further constructions for the captures to build in place of the graticule — an astrolabe's **rete** at 500 lifetime captures, the **Ptolemaic orbs** at best row 30, and a **volvelle** at five completions of any single constellation. Three distances to print behind the chart — the illustrated **chapter plates** for reaching chapter II, The Drift; a portolan's **rhumb lines** at 15 runs; and a **counterproof** of the chapter plates for reaching chapter IV, The Deep. The engraver's credit **Delineavit**, which sets the player's initials in the frame's engraver line, at 50 runs; and the **Ex libris** stamp on the colophon once the ledger holds a score at all three pressures. **Newtonian gravity**, a harder plate to fly rather than another selection, for 500 lifetime perfect transfers and 25 grazed vortices together — see Gameplay above — listed under its own Natural philosophy heading beside the named feats rather than among the eight chosen categories, since it is switched on for a run rather than chosen for one. The remaining ten are the atlas's own named feats struck as medals — **Tres Perfecti**, **Saltus Quinque**, **Velocitas Summa**, **Periculum**, **Linea Pura**, **Altitudo**, **Vigilia**, **Angulus Rectus**, **Evasio**, and **Myrias** — each earned lifetime: the first eight the first time the ledger has ever recorded that observation, the last two simply a lifetime count crossing its own threshold; and listed under their own Named feats heading rather than among the eight chosen categories, since a medal is held rather than selected. What is earned stays earned, and each category keeps one selection, defaulting to the classic look: the night plate, the quill, iron gall, the broken ripple, the wind-heads, the Hevelius figures, and the celestial graticule. Scenery is the one category whose default is nothing rather than something, since an atlas is a sheet with a chart on it before it is a view: a fresh sheet prints no distance at all, and both it and the construction can be set back to nothing at any time from their own always-available cards.

The cosmetics run through the machinery the two plates already use. A plate may now be *derived*: it names `night` or `paper` as its base and passes every token that base registers through one colour transform, so `definePlate` builds all six plates from the same two sets of values and `ink` still points at whichever is on the press. Cellarius, Verdigris and the Carta azzurra are duotones over the night plate — every tone re-inked along a three-stop ramp from ground to highlight, the blue paper's running from blue-grey through a paler grey to lead white — and Foxed ages the paper plate toward foxing brown without disturbing its hues, while Proof is a clean duotone that also sets the flag the lettering checks. Sepia is a duotone over the paper plate whose ramp is read so that its light stop is reached at the sheet's own tone rather than at white, so the ground keeps its cream while everything printed on it goes to the one brown; and because the planet painters use a few colours as literals rather than tokens — a shoreline's verdigris, a fissure's glow, a storm's indigo bands — this plate also presses every finished planet layer through its tint pixel by pixel, once, when the specimen is cached. The painted sheet underneath is dressed to match: the base plate's own fibres, laid lines and tide marks are washed toward the new ground colour and then given whatever else belongs to the plate — gold leaf, oxidation, a prepared blue ground with its pale fibres and pooled edges, or another century of foxing. The footer's page icon still cycles night and paper only; the other four are chosen in the catalogue, and every change rebuilds the cached artwork exactly as switching plates always has. On the proof plate the pen simply never writes: node captions and numerals, rim captions, constellation names, chapter lettering, the frame's numerals, legend, scale label, credit and wind names, the chapter plates' captions and the gloss on the flood are all skipped, and the figures, rings and ornaments are printed as usual.

The other seven categories are the same idea one level down. Observer marks are alternative glyphs cut in the same local space — heading along the axis, the moving point at the origin — and every one of them carries the same shield ring and either the shared comet head or, as the quill and the graver do, its own keylined point, so the traveller stays legible over a pale planet whatever is chosen. Three of them answer to the run and not only to the heading: the moon's phase opens with the slingshot charge in hand, so the mark waxes as the flight is loaded and stands at its thinnest with nothing to spend; the cross-staff's transversary slides down its own graduations toward the eye as the flight quickens, which is exactly the motion of taking a reading; and the burin lifts a longer curl of copper out of the line the faster that line is being cut. The comet is a bundle of divergent rays rather than a shape with an outline, as the cometary plates of the century cut one — they leave the coma's own limb rather than the nucleus, so the tail opens out of a head that has width; they widen away from it and lean off the axis, as the dust of a real tail lags behind its own motion; and they run out of ink at different lengths, each walked in four pieces that fade as they go, so the tail ends ragged rather than closing to a point and reading as a leaf. Trail inks are registered per plate like every other colour, as a wet and a dry tone with the wash, dry-brush edge, bleed and release bead that go with them, and each carries the dried tone the run's route is printed in. Colour is only half of an ink, though, and the other half is not registered per plate at all, because chalk is chalk on either sheet: every ink also names the *substance* it is, as nine numbers saying how it behaves under the hand — how much a cut nib swells the stroke across its edge and thins it to a hairline along it, how much speed alone broadens it, the weight it lays overall, how far it breaks on the grain of the sheet, how far it wicks into the fibres, how much of it is mineral grain that drops into the hollows instead of dissolving, the strength and breadth of the damp wash (or the dust) around the line, and whether it is laid liquid at all and so dries after it is laid. The wet trail and the dried route read the same record, so a run is one substance from the head of the stroke to the bottom of the sheet, and what the catalogue holds is thirteen materials rather than thirteen colours: iron gall in a cut quill swells and thins as the flight turns, so an orbit is written the way a letter is, and feathers along the fibres as iron gall notoriously does; sanguine rides the tooth of the paper, breaking at the core while the dust it sheds stays continuous around it; silverpoint cannot be pressed darker or drawn broader, and holds one faint even hairline whatever the hand does; gold leaf is not a stroke but flakes laid into a mordant line, so the keyline stands bare where a flake failed and a burnished facet catches the light where one took; quicksilver never actually sets, so its wet tone and its dry tone are the very same colour and the last stretch of trail behind the pen stays glassy instead of dimming into the record the way every other liquid does; the earths and minerals — umber, vermilion, malachite ground coarse to keep its green, ultramarine, orpiment — drop visible grain inside the stroke, where the dyes, woad and iron gall, stain evenly and drop nothing; bistre is a transparent soot wash that lays light and wicks freely; and calcined Bologna stone holds no light of its own by daylight, but gives back, for about a second, some of whatever brightness the flight just crossed, before it is absorbed into the dried route exactly as dull as it always was. All of that texture is read off a field on the sheet — a value noise a few units across, sampled at the position the mark fell at and interpolated — rather than off the clock or off a fresh throw of dice at each sample. Off the position, it belongs to the paper and stays on it as the camera climbs; interpolated, a stroke crossing it thins and thickens along its length instead of breaking into a string of separate beads, and a medium that loses the sheet loses it for a run of samples, the way a stick that skips actually skips. Grain is drawn the same way and for the same reason — as density, a fine thread of the same ink laid again inside the core where the grain collected, never as specks, since at a line a pixel and a half wide a speck is not a grain of pigment but a bead on a string. Being the shape of the mark rather than motion, the break-up and the wander survive reduced motion, which still drops the travelling shimmer, the burnished facet, the feathering, the settling grain and the afterglow. The plate's own ink names its medium as a token beside its colours, which is how the rock wall's ochre crayon is a crayon and not a quill. Capture marks are alternative drawings of the ripple, in the planet's own space with the contact point along the axis. Frame ornaments are cut into the same four corners with the same burin, seeded, at the margin's tone — except the sea monsters, which are printed in the flood's own pigment so they still read once the page is half drowned. Figure styles are one object consulted by the four primitives every engraving is built from, and by the pen the layer is cut with, whose line weight is scaled as it is set, so all twelve figures answer to the chosen hand without being rewritten.

Constructions are four hands over one measure. Every one of them stands on the same centre and the same axes and arrives at the same ten stages, so what a style changes is which figure those stages assemble, never where it stands or how fast it is earned; the compass prick, the part-swept ellipse each is built out of, and the 120-division graduated limb three of them are finished with are cut once and shared. The graticule is the atlas's own, described under Plates. The **rete** is the pierced plate that turns over an astrolabe's tympan: the limb, the two tropics between which the whole zodiac lies, and the eccentric ecliptic ring laid tangent inside the one and outside the other — the only construction here that is a real theorem rather than a decoration, since a circle tangent inside Capricorn and outside Cancer can be drawn in exactly one place — finished with six star pointers, each a tongue curling off the ecliptic ring to a barbed point on the star it names. The **orbs** are the spheres as Sacrobosco's readers were taught them, and the one construction whose stages are a list rather than a geometry: a hatched earth at the centre, then seven heavens bought one capture at a time, each carrying its planet on a small epicycle, and outside them the doubled firmament pricked with the fixed stars. The **volvelle** is Apian's paper instrument — dials cut one inside another on a common pin, divided into twelve, with the index arm swung across them and its plumb thread hanging where the reading is actually taken. Each is drawn once into a cached layer keyed by the plate, the style and the capture count, and an unruled sheet keeps no layer at all, so a construction that is not wanted costs nothing.

Scenery is the whole distance behind the chart rather than one mark on it, and the layers of it stand or fall together. Its three earned styles are cut two ways. The **chapter plates** are the four illustrations described under Gameplay, unchanged. The **rhumb lines** are a portolan's web in their place: a hidden circle pricked with eight wind-nodes, each throwing its own lines clean across the sheet with the four winds heavier than the rest, a sixteen-point rose set on the node the chart is oriented from and a lesser one on another, and a stepped scale of leagues in the corner — every line laid as a gradient that dies away from the node it leaves, because a chart's rhumbs are ruled in one charge of ink and run out of it, and because a web of hard lines all the way to the edge would read as a grid over the chart rather than as something printed a long way behind it. They take their own captions, by the quarter of the wind rather than by a draughtsman, and carry none of Galileo's marginal figures. The **counterproof** is not a second cut at all but the chapter plates' own plate pulled a second time: a counterproof is taken off an impression while it is still wet, onto a second damp sheet, so what comes back is reversed, weaker and softened by the pass — the same cached plate laid mirrored, at rather less than half a charge, and again a hair off itself. Mirroring the whole transform mirrors the blit's own clipping with it, so the plate is asked for at its usual coordinates and still lands, and is still cut to the sheet, on the other side of the middle. The rhumb web is cached per plate and chapter exactly as the illustrations are; the counterproof asks for the illustration's own cached plate and adds nothing to the store.

The catalogue itself is a ruled library page in Fell type, opened with the book icon in the footer from the title and end screens and closed by its own button or Escape. It opens on two tabs, **Record** and **Catalogue**, both always drawn onto the leaf with only the inactive one out of sight, so switching between them never redraws either. `Record` prints the ledger's figures as it always has — orbits captured, perfect transfers, constellations traced, highest row, runs, and time in the chart — and beside them everything else the ledger keeps that otherwise surfaces nowhere in the game on its own: best flow, the deepest chapter reached at any pressure and at Magister's, vortices grazed, Scutum, Repulsa and Aurora charges spent, slingshot stars left at top speed, inkwells filled on a reckless streak, and rough impressions, followed by the daily streak, current and longest, read off the ephemeris's own log. Under that, one table gives the best score and the runs played at each of the three pressures and under the daily plate; a second lists all eight named feats by their Latin caption against how many times each has fired in all; a third lists all twelve catalogue figures against how many times each has been traced, an untraced one printing a plain nought rather than dropping from the list. `Catalogue` holds what the leaf has always held: one ruled section per category listing every item with its Latin caption. Every card carries a small engraved preview of the thing it offers, cut as line work in the plate's own gold rather than set as a character — an observer mark as the pen actually cuts it, a capture mark as the burin leaves it at a planet, a frame ornament as the margin takes it, and a figure style shown on the Lyre, so the three hands can be read against one another. A construction is shown as its own hand sets it out, and a distance as what it actually prints — the chapter plates as plate I's monumental limb running off the card as it runs off the sheet, the rhumb web as its nodes and its rose, the counterproof as that same drawing put through the very transform the blit puts it through, and a bare sheet as a plate-mark, an inner rule and its laid lines with nothing inside them. Two of the eight are printed in colour instead, because colour is what they are: a plate is a swatch of its own ground under its own ink, with one orbit and one star of its own gold, so the eight sheets can be compared without pulling any of them; and a trail ink is a stroke laid in that very ink, read back to front — the dried route the flight is printed in, the body drying out of it, the wet leading half, the dry-brush edge, the wash beneath and the bead held at the point. Where that stroke begins the card spills one, and the spill is not a drawing of a splat but a splat: the card carries a small canvas over the engraving, and the same routine the chart spills with paints it in the ink being offered, at its own blotWet-to-blotDry mix and its own alphas, caught a seventh of the way through a splat's life — the moment it has finished spreading and has barely begun to dry. The seed is the ink's own name, so a card keeps one blot rather than a fresh one every time the leaf is opened. An earned item is a button that selects it; an unearned one is a blank rule, its preview a dashed frame with a question mark in it, with its condition and progress set greyed beside it. A three-letter initials field appears once the engraver's credit is earned. The page scrolls inside its own leaf on a phone, holds the gameplay input while it is open, and is printed on whichever plate is on the press. When a run's write earns something new, the colophon prints an engraved line, `NEW IN THE CATALOGUE · <name>`, under two short tones; nothing else interrupts play.

## The eras

The atlas is one century's way of drawing the sky, not the only one. Two more are on the press, reached by their own doors on the frontispiece, and a third is a row in a table rather than a rewrite of the game. Nothing in the simulation knows any of this: `OrbitWorld` deals the same seeded chart, sweeps the same collisions, cuts the same tangents and holds the same release window open for the same seventh of a second whichever century is drawing it. What changes is the material, the hand, and every word said about it.

A plate says what it is in three declarations and no conditional anywhere. `can` names what it keeps to itself — its own record, and whether it is entered and left as a mode at all. A registered vocabulary names what it calls things: the atlas's own words stand under whatever a century renames, so a chart, a course, a loss, a chamber, a warning and a fixture each read from one table and no call site carries a fallback. And a named hand lists the painters that century draws in — the ground, a body, a ring, a hazard, the traveller, the boundary, and the fixtures of a printed sheet it does not have. Everything a hand does not name is still the atlas's own, which is what lets two of them work into one frame and what a page turn between centuries will need.

**Era I, The Rock.** Franco-Cantabrian Europe, centred on Lascaux. The ground is not a colour but a material: three fields of value noise built once — six octaves of relief, three of calcite sheen running vertically for the water that has come down this wall a long time, two of broad iron staining under it all — shaded from the relief's own gradient against one torch held low and to the left, with a per-pixel hash at the mark scale for the tooth. It is baked into a single tile whose noise lattice wraps, so it repeats and scrolls with no seam, and it is drawn at one baked sample to one device pixel, because the tooth is the whole point and any other ratio smears it into fog. The torch is a second bake and falls twice: at full strength on the wall alone, then at a little over two fifths over everything laid on it, so the rock takes the whole fall of the flame and the marks take under half. The dark that bites in at every edge is the torch's own reach, not a vignette drawn over the top.

Every mark comes from one of four tools. A dab, whose opacity ramps in from a slightly irregular silhouette with no direction and no leading edge, is baked once per pigment and per silhouette and blitted after that. A wash is never a flat fill but a scatter of dabs. A scratch is a flint incision — a charcoal shadow offset a constant, then a pale kaolin core on the true line. A peck is a dark hollow with a lit lip. The palette is short by two on purpose: there is no gold, so the reddest ochre stands in and is spent as sparingly as gold ever was, and there is no blue at all, so what the atlas says in blue this era says in its black.

A body is built in the era's own order of work rather than the colourist's, and over the observation clock alone. A crude mass of ochre goes on at the capture itself, ungated, because that is what taking the orbit is — colour before contour. Then the edge is walked round it off true and firms over the first tenth to just under half of the observation; the wall's tooth comes up through the pigment from about a quarter to seven tenths; the dabs accumulate from about a third to nine tenths, and accumulate is the word — not a bigger dab but the same seeded scatter carried further, since raising its count only ever adds; and last, from seven tenths to completion, the thing that says which body this is. A body observed halfway is a mass with an edge and no identity at all. Bodies come in three brightnesses of ochre dab, and one is granted a face: a kaolin-pale disc with a few manganese dabs laid over it, the one light a naked eye can tell from every other.

The ring is a different thing entirely, and the separation is the sheet's whole argument. It is twenty-eight dabs struck at the node's real capture radius — never staged wider or narrower than the truth the code will test — seeded so the same twenty-eight sit in the same twenty-eight places every frame, and structurally unchanged for as long as the observation is being made, because the one mark a release is timed against must not be the one that keeps moving. The ring says where an observation is possible; the body says what has been learned. One brief change of state on the ring is the completion cue: at the crossing into a full observation the dots draw together into a single struck line and a little dust lifts off it, and two thirds of a second later it has settled back to being geometry. It reads the run's own clock, so a pause freezes it as it freezes every other mark.

The dangers are the era's own. The Shaft is a true void with a pecked rim, and the eye is drawn down into it rather than around it. The Flare is an ember core under soot, deliberately not the traveller's colour — an early pass let the two share one warm glow and they read as the same thing at a glance. The Draught is a charcoal smear dragged sideways. Unlit rock is drawn with total fidelity: nothing at all. The traveller is not the crayon but the point on it that is actually touching the wall, a worn contact point with the Observer Core at it, the one pure light on the sheet and the rest of the lump dead weight around it. The boundary is the forgetting, and it fails in two ways at once rather than fading: the pigment's film lets go and powders away, and the limestone itself exfoliates in scales, so the loss has broken edges and hard, sharp-lipped margins with chips drifting free of them.

Nothing surrounds this sky. No plate-mark, no engraved rule, no graduated scale, no wind-heads, no laid wires, no running head at the foot and no chapter title written across the sheet — a figure's edge is wherever rock or torchlight stops. The engraved constellation figure and the geometer's survey of a landing are not drawn either, the first because this era draws animals and never constellation-figures and the second because it has no geometry and no script to letter one in; the route through a chart's stars is a separate mark and is still drawn, so nothing a transfer depends on is ever withheld. What it does set — the count, a standing instruction, a warning — is set in a modern slab serif, openly a curator's gloss, because a wall with no script of its own cannot be given one without inventing it.

Some of the atlas's own hand still falls on this wall and is the next pass rather than this one: the aim guide, the wet trail and the dried route, the connection lines between charts, the particle effects and the written inscriptions. All of them carry planning information, all of them now draw in this plate's own pigments rather than in iron gall, and none of them is wrong — only foreign.

**Era II, The Ceiling.** Deir el-Bahri, the astronomical ceiling of TT353, drawn as a playable preview: lime plaster, fine black drawing, red setting-out, restrained mineral fills, decan columns and hour-circles in place of constellations and orbits, and its own five sounds. It draws its whole frame in its own hand, which is one painter named on its row.

Neither century writes the atlas's record, and neither is offered as a daily plate. The full eight-rung ladder they are the first two of, and how a run would climb it, was planned in [docs/archive/eras/JOURNEY.md](docs/archive/eras/JOURNEY.md); that plan is archived and indefinitely postponed, is not built, and is not the current focus, which is polishing the atlas above.

The source lives in `src/` as classic scripts that share one global scope, listed in load order in `src/index.html`. `simulation.js` holds the independent `OrbitWorld` class: seeded generation, fixed-step movement, swept collisions, scoring, and difficulty. `audio.js` synthesizes the Web Audio sounds. `plates.js` owns the render globals, storage, and the night and paper colour tokens along with the derived plates built from them; `replay.js` rebuilds a finished `OrbitWorld` from nothing but its seed and when the traveller released, with no rendering and no real time spent doing it; `ledger.js` holds the persistent ledger, the catalogue of unlockables and the chosen cosmetics; `backdrop.js` paints the two sheets; `planets.js` builds the procedural specimens; `celestial.js` draws the chapter plates and ambient events; `marks.js` bakes the burin rings, the route lines and the lettering set along a curve; `glyphs.js` holds the extracted Fell outlines and `reveal.js` the living pen: reveal state, the nib, and the masks each kind of mark is drawn through; `figures.js` draws constellation figures, nodes, lenses, hazards, and the aim guide; `inscriptions.js` writes what the run has to say onto the chart; `effects.js` paints the rising darkness, the comet, and its trail; `ceiling.js` and `rock.js` hold eras II and I, each registering the painters its own century draws in; `frame.js` builds the plate frame and composes each frame; `ephemeris.js` rules the almanac leaf of the daily plates and deals a past one again; `review.js` holds a free-scrolling camera over a `replayRun()` world and the versioned `orbit.lastReplay.v1` record that lets the plate a run last finished on be opened again after the tab itself is gone; `ui.js` handles the screens, viewport, and the single input. `scripts/bundle.mjs` inlines the modules into one script for both the build and the tests.

Optional development commands use only Node.js built-ins:

```
npm start
npm test
npm run build
npm run glyphs
```

`npm run glyphs` and `npm run fonts` are the only commands that need a dependency (fontkit and subset-font, both dev dependencies). Both read `assets/fonts.source.css` and are needed only when the faces change or when the atlas gains a character it did not set before: `glyphs` regenerates `src/glyphs.js`, `fonts` regenerates `assets/fonts.css`.

The verification script checks the main, constellation, and charged-shortcut paths on 60 generated courses through row 48, including 360 completed constellations and 360 shortcut transfers, and runs the constellation route on the same 60 courses through row 60 to prove that the forks past the fourth region are reachable and completable. It checks exact tangent contacts in both directions at three speeds, moving-target guide agreement, symmetric gravitational flybys, retained speed, curved landing predictions at three speeds, collision warnings, lens sampling at clipped viewport edges, star acceleration and caps, speed-based rewards, long-flight regressions, darkness pressure and the perfect-streak relief, the capped and decaying head start a skipped orbit banks against the pursuit, the charge carried against the rising dark — dealt off the main line clear of everything already on the chart, spent once at the waterline where it drives the flood clear of the traveller and holds it off, and never spent twice — the opening difficulty choice setting the run's pressure and clearing the two unchosen targets, rewards and expiry, duplicate capture protection, bounded generation, pause/restart, and complete-script execution at five viewport sizes using native-API stand-ins. It also checks the living pen: that every mark of a fresh chart's first row goes from nothing to finished within its second, that the frame finishes drawing itself, that no more than three marks are ever in hand at once, and that reduced motion reports every mark as already printed. It checks the observation clock the same way: that a body freezes the fraction of a full observation its orbit actually held, exactly once, at the release; that it clamps at one however long the orbit was held past it; that nothing captured afterwards can rewrite it, though the very next capture zeroes the sweep it was measured from; that dying in orbit freezes nothing, because the node is never let go of and the live sweep is still there to be read; that a body neither captured nor released carries no fraction at all and is drawn as nothing; that a body earned in full is never blanked by the pen's own three-mark limit on marks still entering the view; that the completion flourish fires on the crossing alone, once, never before it and never for an orbit given up short of it; and that one seed held two ways deals one chart regardless. It also checks the repulsive flare field for symmetry, preserved speed, its smaller lethal core and guide agreement; a Newtonian body's own pull for the same symmetry and a cleanly bounded reach, and for the one way it actually differs from every other field on the chart — that it speeds the traveller up at closest approach and very nearly gives that back again over a full symmetric pass — with the flag itself proven inert wherever a caller does not ask for it; nebula placement and the cut aiming preview; the seeded twelve-figure catalogue and that every entry in it has an engraving of its own rather than the fallback asterism, baked part-traced, completed and expired; that lettering along an arc reports and draws a finite arc; that the page turn is drawn part way across the frame, freezes with a paused run and settles exactly on the new chapter; observations firing once per run; the daily plate's seed, forced pressure, separate record and copied line; that its varied opening draws the second and third planets and all twelve regions' figures from that same seed, that an unvaried opening keeps the two fixed points and the fixed four every other fixture already reads, and that a course drawn varied remains exactly as playable through the same depth an ordinary seed already is; and the ephemeris: that a day is opened only by a run begun on the day itself, that a day that was never drawn can neither be chosen nor dealt, that a repeat is dealt from its own date's seed at Classic pressure and says so on the screens and in the copied line, that a repeat opens no new day and never touches the current day's record while it does improve the day it repeats, that the leaf prints today's square as open and an undrawn day as a blank rule, that it turns no further back than the earliest plate on record, that it holds the gameplay input and closes on Escape, and that a seeded log is read only where it is a past day with a record on it, with the record that predates the log folded in once. It checks the ledger and the catalogue: that a fresh or unreadable document opens an empty ledger with every cosmetic at its default and every locked item unselectable, that each of the fifty-two conditions is met exactly at its threshold and not one short of it, that a named feat becomes its medal the first time the ledger has ever recorded it or, for the two counted by a plain lifetime total, the moment that total crosses its threshold, that the end of a run writes the document and raises the lifetime figures by exactly what the run did, that every plate boots and prints all four chapter plates and a frame while only the proof plate omits its lettering, that every cosmetic selection renders a frame with a capture mark in hand, that the dried route stays inside its cap and inside the sheet and is cleared when a new run is dealt, that an exact tangent flown through the whole runtime lays a departure construction with a bearing from 0 to 359 at the release and an arrival construction carrying the right-angle mark at the landing, both capped and cleared with the run, that a nebula patch is cut once into its own sprite, that the gloss on the flood stays clear of the footer band at every waterline and every layout, that the run's descriptions are inscribed on the chart rather than printed over it — the standing instruction held beside the orbit it is about, an announcement carried by the sheet rather than held on the screen, nothing set into the margin from any corner of the plate, a standing instruction re-set when the sheet has carried it off, and the whole of it capped and cleared with the run — and that the catalogue lists the ledger, its locked rules and its conditions, holds the gameplay input while it is open and closes on Escape. It checks the pause leaf the same way: that the footer's own control suspends a run and stands its clock as still as a switched-away page does, that the halt is spoken in the plate's own word for it, that the leaf's control and Escape both take the run up again, that leaving deals a fresh chart at the frontispiece with no colophon pulled while still counting the abandoned run in the ledger, and that with no run in hand neither the control nor the key raises the leaf at all. Two of the six runtime layouts are booted from seeded storage, one with a document that is not JSON at all and one with a ledger that has earned the whole catalogue. Route probes can take an ordinary capture if they miss a perfect timing window for more than 1.5 turns. It also checks the replay every one of those six layouts is rebuilt from: a middling pilot, flown through the real gameplay input and taking any release that is not steep, plays a run to its actual death; `replayRun()` (`src/replay.js`) is then handed nothing but that run's seed and the moment of every release, and the world it rebuilds — unpruned, so it outgrows what the darkness left the live run holding — is checked to agree with the live one on score, cause of death, everything else pruning does not touch, and the exact point the traveller came to rest. It also checks that this same run, saved under `orbit.lastReplay.v1` at the colophon, is named on the frontispiece's own REVIEW LAST PLATE button — row and score read straight off the saved record — and opens the identical seed from there, closing back onto the frontispiece rather than a colophon it was never shown; where storage is blocked, the button simply stays off rather than the boot failing. These are simulation/runtime checks, not browser screenshots. `npm start` serves the repository for development. The build inlines the modules, validates the resulting script, and writes the single-file page and the `assets` folder into `dist` for static hosting; it also carries `docs/archive/eras/prototypes/rock-read.html` across untouched to `dist/rock-read.html`, held to the same rule against a live script, style, image, audio or video reference that the bundled page itself is checked against, so the readability spike reaches GitHub Pages exactly as free of the network as the game beside it.
