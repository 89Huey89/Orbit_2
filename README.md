# Orbit

The playable game is a single file. Run `npm run build` to produce **dist/index.html**, which contains the complete game, artwork, sound, and styles, with the seventeenth-century Fell typefaces embedded in `dist/assets/fonts.css` beside it. That built page needs no installation, server, or network access to play, and it is what GitHub Pages serves. During development, open `src/index.html` directly or run `npm start` and visit the printed address: the template loads the same code as separate module scripts, so no build is needed between edits.

Tap or click to begin. While circling a node, tap to release along your current tangent. The next node captures you automatically. Space or Enter performs the same action. Tap after a loss to restart.

The pricked line of small wedges shows your predicted trajectory. Pale ticks on your orbit mark clear tangent release points: skim the next orbit's rim so your incoming flight continues smoothly into the new orbit. A perfect preview includes a short curve showing the arrival direction. Near black holes, the guide bends and a bright mark appears at the current release point when the curved flight gives a perfect landing. The wider faint arc remains a forgiving capture window, and on Tiro it is drawn 30% wider still, since missing a body outright is a harsher loss than only missing the perfect band inside it. Stay ahead of the rising dark and give black-hole centers room. Blue orbit rims mark drifting nodes; copper orbit rims mark nodes that fade 4.5 seconds after capture. Small gold nodes offer a harder bonus route.

Every region includes a fork: follow the wider orbits or take a narrower three-star constellation route. Both paths reconnect. Stars light as you visit them. At a fork, two release marks indicate the two paths. Follow the gold links and capture all three marked planets to complete a constellation, earn 60 extra points, and make the darkness retreat for four seconds. Unfinished constellations expire when you pass their reunion point or an unvisited star sinks completely into the darkness. Each constellation can be rewarded once per run; your completed total appears at the end.

The catalogue holds twelve figures. The first four regions always carry the Needle, the Sail, the Lyre, and the Crown, in that order. Every region after them draws from the catalogue in an order fixed by the run's seed, taking the eight later figures — the Compass, the Hourglass, the Serpent, the Argo, the Astrolabe, the Quill, the Lantern, and the Moth — before returning to the opening four, so no figure repeats until all twelve have been used. Each chart keeps its region index as its identity and records which catalogue entry it drew.

The chart is cut for a pace rather than for a row count. Every transfer is drawn to take about 1.28 seconds at the pace the chart expects at that row, which opens from the opening speed toward the chart's top speed over the first twenty-five rows, so the gulfs widen as the early slingshots put that pace within reach. Free flight between two orbits runs about 265 world units on average where it was 166, and the deepest crossings reach 474 where they reached 262. That pace depends only on the row and never on the traveller, so a seed still deals one plate for everyone and the daily plate is unaffected: how a run is flown cannot change the chart it is dealt. The orbits open with it, up to half as wide again, and their capture bands with them. A wider ring is swept more slowly at the same pace and presents a larger rim from further off, which is what keeps the release window in the hand — the angle that finds a rim narrows as the gulf grows, and the window would otherwise have halved as the distances doubled. A slingshot ring is the one exception and keeps its size, since its charge is earned per lap and a wider one would only cost more time against the flood for the same 90 units of speed. Making the sheet narrower pulls the whole chart back inside its own edge, so a rotated screen never leaves an orbit standing where the run is lost.

The opening speed is 150 world units per second, down from 205. Speed no longer rises automatically with progress. A slingshot star appears on the third planet, followed by the first constellation reunion and every eight main rows. Its engraved inner band fills as you orbit. One full lap adds 90 units of speed, up to a launch speed of 360; leave earlier for a smaller increase. Acceleration happens during the orbit, so releasing does not suddenly double your velocity. Each star provides at most one lap of acceleration. Waiting longer earns no extra speed or points, and pausing preserves your charge.

The nib carries a charge of ink, and every flight spends it by the distance flown, so a transfer costs what it is long rather than what it takes: the same crossing costs the same ink whether it is flown at the opening pace or at the chart's top speed. A full nib carries 2,000 world units. Holding an orbit re-charges it slowly, at 0.13 of a charge a second; a landing pays a dividend, 0.12 for a clean tangent arrival against 0.05 for a hard turn; and one lap of a slingshot star fills it outright, so a star buys reach as well as speed. Run the nib dry in flight and the line stops and the run ends, though the landing is settled first, so a transfer that arrives on the last drop stands. The pressure scales the drain rather than the gauge: Tiro spends 0.82 of what Adeptus does and Magister 1.22, and every plate reads the same reservoir. That reservoir is engraved under the score as a short rule that drains and turns copper as it empties, the bead of wet ink at the nib dries away with it, and a standing note is written beside the orbit being held once the charge is low.

Dwelling on a ring buys ink and spends the time the flood is counting, which is the whole of the trade. A run that leaves at the first opening every time spends distance faster than any landing pays it back and dries up around row 16; a run that waits for a centred line every time keeps ink in hand and is caught by the dark instead. Only flying tangent entries and charging the stars pays for both.

Earned speed carries between planets. A perfect transfer preserves the incoming velocity and joins at the closest point on the rim, including for a drifting planet. The game accepts a small band around the drawn orbit and keeps that arrival radius, avoiding a position or heading snap. That band reaches a fifth of the target's own radius, or eight world units, whichever is larger, and Tiro widens it by 35%, since a tight orbit sweeps through its release window quickly however careful the pilot; Adeptus and Magister keep the drawn band. An angled flight that is not a tangent remains a forgiving ordinary capture, but the sharp turn removes up to 28% of speed above the opening pace. Below 16 degrees of arrival, though — a flight falling at a planet rather than crossing its rim — the landing pays nothing at all: there is no way to steer a flight once it is released, so refusing the orbit outright only ever stranded a mistimed release with nothing left to recover onto. It joins as the same forgiving hard turn any angled arrival gets, shedding speed the same way, but earns no score, no skip bonus and no ink dividend, so a careless release is still worse than a patient one. About a quarter of the arrivals the chart offers land this way; a tangent-seeking run never meets the rule at all, since a tangent-seeking pilot lands between 78 and 102 degrees. The guide says so before the release is made: a course that will earn nothing is drawn in the warning ink and marked with an open chevron across the line instead of the landing square. The three opening targets never cost a landing its credit, so the choice of pressure cannot be lost to it. The guide and capture use the same moving-target solver. Flight launch speed is capped at 360 so repeated moving-planet assists cannot compound indefinitely. After the introductory star, each slingshot still offers its wider route and a direct transfer to a more distant planet.

Flights have no time limit, but they do have a length the nib can pay for. The guide prices the course it is drawing: past the point where the ink would give out the pricking is starved to almost nothing, and a course the nib cannot reach the end of is barred with a copper stroke and a small ring where it runs out. Such a course is still aimed and still drawn, and may still be flown — the choice is the player's, made knowing where the line stops. A long transfer continues until a planet captures you or you encounter a real hazard, the rising darkness, the side of the chart, or the end of the ink. The aiming guide checks all generated destinations, including distant planets beyond its former 1.9-second horizon. Every full main orbit skipped earns an additional 10 points before the speed multiplier, so at the opening speed skipping three earns 30 and skipping seven earns 70. The landing message reports the skipped count and multiplied bonus; time in flight does not reduce the award.

Black holes have a local gravitational field extending 100 world units beyond their radius. Close flybys bend the airborne trajectory toward the hole, with slower and closer passes turning more strongly. This is a deliberately simplified arcade effect: the field steers the direction while preserving the player's chosen speed. The pull fades smoothly to zero at the edge, and it does not disturb an attached orbit. The dark center remains lethal; a close graze can still earn its once-per-hole bonus.

Through the opening regions a hazard is placed clear of every route between two main nodes, so it is scenery to be given room. From row 12, and then only on every third row, one may instead sit on one of the two ways across and shut it, so the side you cross on becomes a decision rather than a formality. What it may never do is close the last way through: whichever side it leaves open is flyable both ways it can be flown, by a smooth tangent for a perfect transfer and by a centre-directed line for a player not yet flying them, and is clear of the whole gravity field rather than merely of the lethal core, since a route inside the field would be bent into the hazard it was drawn around. About one hazard in three closes a route. The slingshot's own shortcut is never touched. From the third region onward, sunspot flares alternate with black holes under the same placement rules. A flare's field has the same reach and the same steering with the sign reversed: it pushes the flight outward instead of drawing it in, again without changing the speed. Only a smaller core, six tenths of the drawn radius, is lethal, and a close pass earns the same once-per-hazard bonus. The aiming guide and real flight agree around a flare exactly as they do around a hole. A flare is engraved as a sunspot in the Galileo manner: a dark umbra covering exactly the lethal core, a penumbra of fine radial strokes out to the drawn edge, two lesser spots of the same group, and burin rays leaving the limb that breathe slowly with the plate's own time. Everything but the rays is baked into a sprite. The field is drawn at its true radius as a pricked ring with small outward barbs — a repulsion ring, told apart at a glance from the solid cut edge and accretion rings of a black hole.

Nebula patches begin at row 12 and appear at most once every four rows, lying across one of the tangent routes between two main planets. A patch is inert: it neither kills nor pulls, and it never covers a capture band, a drift envelope, or a hazard field. What it does is hide the chart. The pricked guide is cut at the near edge of the cloud and marked with a small ring instead of a landing mark, so the far half of the transfer has to be flown without a preview. The release marks and the perfect-window arcs on the orbit you are leaving are unaffected, and the flight itself crosses the cloud and captures normally. The patch is engraved as a faint stippled haze — a few hundred seeded dots, densest at its heart and faded off with the cube of the distance so the stipple has no edge of its own, chalky at night and dilute sepia on paper — under one mostly lifted contour, and is blitted from a sprite, since nothing about it moves. It is meant to be noticed rather than looked at; what tells the player the chart is hidden is the cut guide and its fogged ring, not the weight of the cloud.

The pace decides how much of a course the pen will set down. At the opening speed the whole transfer is drawn; as the chart's speed is earned the far part of a fast crossing is left unset, the horizon closing from 900 world units to 260 at the chart's top speed. At the opening pace this touches about one aimed transfer in fourteen and hides nothing of an ordinary one; at full pace it cuts nine in ten, leaving about two thirds of a typical crossing drawn. The cut is marked exactly as a nebula's is, with a small fogged ring in place of the landing mark, and the release marks and the perfect-window arcs on the orbit being left are untouched — so what a fast run gives up is knowing where the flight ends, not whether it leaves on a tangent. Speed buys reach and costs sight. The fog is only what is drawn: the aim, the arrival it predicts and the landing behind it are unchanged, which is what lets the guide still be checked against real flight.

The curved aiming guide uses the same 120 Hz steering and swept collision rules as real flight, including moving planets and tangent-perfect arrivals. It stops at the first predicted capture, black-hole collision, or chart edge; a dangerous path ends in a copper warning mark. Prediction skips force-free straight sections, samples curves into a bounded polyline, and cannot award points or mutate the course. The long-transfer guide remains available.

Black holes lens the actual atlas background and starlight. A reusable 640-pixel canvas snapshots each visible region; concentric sampling bands stretch it outward near the dark center and settle smoothly at the field boundary. Fine tilted accretion rings retain the copperplate palette. Targets, orbit markers, the guide, and the player remain sharp and accurately positioned above the distortion. The subtle twist freezes on pause and is disabled for reduced motion, while the static lensing and gameplay pull remain.

The darkness starts advancing after 1.5 seconds at 22 world units per second, accelerating by 0.55 units per second of active play up to 150, and takes a quarter of the chart's own growth on top, so the fully developed pursuit reaches about 191 rather than 150 deep in a run. A tide fixed in world units would slacken exactly where the chart opens up; a quarter of the growth keeps the squeeze on a long transfer about what it is on a short one without letting the flood outrun what a skilled run can climb. Its edge stays near the bottom of the view while you ascend. The opening allows roughly ten seconds before idle play loses; later, consistently slow transfers become unsustainable. Speed-building stars and accurate transfers let the player stay ahead. The constellation reprieve still provides four seconds to recover or build speed, and pausing freezes the pursuit.

Skill relieves the pressure directly. Each consecutive perfect transfer past the second slows the pursuit by 3%, to a limit of 15% at seven in a row. An ordinary capture resets the streak to nothing. The relief scales the whole advance, so it is worth most against the fully developed pursuit: a run holding a long chain of perfect transfers faces 127.5 units per second where a run of ordinary captures faces 150.

The rising darkness is painted as indigo-black ink soaking into the atlas: overlapping washes, pooled pigment, paper fibres, and fine suspended flecks. A thin copper shoreline sits at the actual loss threshold and becomes clearer as it approaches the player. Completed constellations turn the edge pale gold as it retreats, then the colour settles back as the reprieve ends. Both pigment plates are cached and reused across viewports. Pause freezes the wash, and reduced motion keeps its decorative grain and fringe still while the real boundary continues to advance. The flood carries marginalia along its shoreline, and the gloss keeps off the chart: where it would drift across an orbit ring or a hazard it fades to nothing over a short reach and comes back once it is clear. The marginalia: a Leviathan of three coils, a thrown tail and a spouting head surfaces slowly at his own place along the edge every twenty-seven seconds, cropped by the waterline as he rises and sinks, and an engraved gloss `HIC SUNT DRACONES` drifts with the ink below the line. Both are cut once into sprites in the darkness's own pigment — the monster only bobs and fades, he is never re-engraved — both take the pale gold of a constellation reprieve, and both stand still under pause and reduced motion.

Ocean worlds, cratered moons, ringed giants, fractured ice planets, desert worlds, volcanic planets, and storm giants each have their own procedural surface and size. Restrained verdigris, ochre, warm grey, chalk, and sepia washes sit on a paper-coloured base. On the paper plate the engraving carries the form and the colour is secondary: the body is a dilute wash rather than a printed flat, the hatching and stipple are cut about a third heavier, the wash is about a third weaker, and it runs one to two pixels past the plate in the off-register direction before the burin keyline is printed over it, so the black line always reads on top of the colour. The night plate keeps its original balance. The hatching is laid as a left hand lays it, every stroke running down and to the right and darkening toward the limb, and it is never crossed: on paper the dark limb is deepened with a second set of parallel strokes that curve with the form, concentric with the limb, the way a pen follows a rounded body rather than crossing it. The same slant is kept on the chapter plates, the moon's night side and the eclipsed disc among them. On paper every keyline was first tried in red chalk: a broken sanguine circle a little off true, and a shorter trial arc, show beside the ink wherever the pen did not follow the chalk exactly, and each orbit ring carries the same chalk pass under its ink. On paper the dark side of every body is first laid in as a dilute sepia wash, deepening toward the limb, before a stroke goes on, so the hatch reads as strokes over tone. A cratered world carries a Sidereus Nuncius terminator of its own — a ragged day-and-night line a little right of the middle, the night side laid in behind it and the hatch beginning at the line, so the lit hemisphere is left as clean as Galileo left his — with small crater rims on the dark side catching the light on their far walls, and an earthshine, after the Codex Leicester's account of the old moon in the new moon's arms, that lifts the dark side faintly toward the limb. Its craters are drawn as Galileo drew them, with the sun on the left: a rim, a crescent of shadow on the floor against the wall nearest the sun, the far wall catching the light, and no rays. Uneven pigment pools, dry-brush flecks, dark coastlines, and slightly offset outlines give them a hand-coloured copperplate character. Fine globe meridians and broken survey arcs accompany each specimen. Every seven main nodes include all seven families, in a run-dependent order, with individually seeded terrain and axial tilts. Planet appearance is separate from the orbit's behavior.

Etched surfaces turn slowly beneath fixed lighting and ring shadows. Cloud filaments drift separately; volcanic fissures gently brighten, and ice worlds have a faint polar shimmer. These decorative movements stop when reduced motion is requested or the game is paused. Surfaces, lighting, and weather are generated once per planet and reused as cached layers, with at most 24 planets retained.

The four chapters each have a full illustrated atlas plate. The Quiet opens beside a monumental engraved moon and a smaller companion. The Drift is crossed by a huge tilted ring system and a distant desert moon. The Eclipse has a dark solar disc with a corona of fine copper strokes and heavy bands of ink below. The Deep reveals a large spiral star field built from chalk-like contours and thousands of stippled stars. These distant scenes have distinct indigo, copper, violet-grey, and slate palettes, with a darker central channel for readable foreground play. Coordinate arcs and small plate captions tie them together. The brighter stars have eight-point compass forms.

A scene change is a page turn. The new chapter's plate slides up from below the frame as a fresh sheet, carrying its own dust and its own marginalia, with a soft shadow along its leading edge and its own plate-mark inside it; the old sheet stays where it lies and fades away underneath. Each chapter is also pulled with its own registration offset of a pixel or two, carried in the world-to-plate mapping, so the whole chart — orbits, hazards, the guide, the comet and the rising ink together — sits very slightly off true against the frame as the sheet changes. Nothing moves relative to anything else in the chart, so no distance the player reads changes. The sheet lands after about three seconds and the blend settles on the new chapter over the same span as the old dissolve. Reduced motion cross-fades instead, with no sheet and no movement. The turn briefly writes a large chapter name and plate numeral onto the sheet itself, in the play channel below the HUD band. The line it is written on is chosen once, as the reveal begins: the line under the HUD band, or one of two lower lines when a planet or a hazard already sits across it, so the lettering never jumps while the pen is still writing. That line is then taken into world coordinates, and the chart carries the name exactly as it carries an orbit — a fast ascent slides it down the sheet, and it is held at the edge of the play channel rather than allowed to print into the margin. It is pulled on a small leaf of its own, one soft pass of the sheet's ground feathered to nothing, so the name reads over whatever the chart has scrolled beneath it, the Eclipse's dark disc included. Chapter lettering sits beside the play area on short landscape screens. Node captions ride above their planet but flip below it, clear of the HUD band and of the chapter lettering, when the node is too near the top edge to print them inside the frame. Illustrated scenery shifts gently with the ascent behind two finer dust layers. Reduced motion keeps the decorative scenery still and removes the chapter lettering's small upward movement; pause freezes transitions. All four illustrations are cached at a fixed resolution, preserve their proportions across viewports, and use a separate random stream from gameplay. The entire game remains procedural and offline.

Quiet ambient events alternate between a small distant comet at the edge of the view and a brief glint on the moon, rings, corona, or spiral arms. Only one can be present at a time, followed by 19–30 seconds of quiet time. Events fade away near playable planets, hazards, the player, the aiming line, and rising darkness. Chapter reveals, constellation celebrations, recent captures, and immediate danger hold back new events. The ambient layer adds no sounds or interactions, uses its own seeded random stream, pauses with the game, clears on restart, and is disabled by reduced motion.

The whole background is held back inside the play channel so the moving parts read first, and left fuller in the margins where nothing is played. The channel is the middle of the sheet, 168 scaled pixels either side of the centre or 30% of the width, whichever is wider, up to half the sheet. The illustrated chapter plate is left whole in the margins and washed back down the channel by a single pass of the sheet's own ground colour at 0.34 at night and 0.30 on paper, at full strength across the channel and fading out over a 60-pixel feather either side, so the contrast under the chart drops by about a third and the transition is graded rather than cut. The two drifting dust plates carry a third fewer marks, 2,150 / 3,500 / 1,950 / 910 far and 650 / 1,100 / 620 / 290 near against 3,300 / 5,400 / 3,000 / 1,400 and 1,000 / 1,700 / 950 / 440, and are printed at 0.40 and 0.62 of their weight at night and 0.17 and 0.27 on paper, where they were 0.54 and 0.84, and 0.22 and 0.34; the region wash beneath them drops from 0.31 to 0.25 at night and from 0.050 to 0.042 on paper. Inside the channel the background star field is set at half the stars, every other one skipped, and 0.55 of the contrast, while the margins keep the whole field at full strength. The Eclipse's radiating corona is cut from 560 strokes to 300, a quarter shorter and at about two fifths of the weight, and its dust shadow from 0.36 to 0.22. The Latin rim caption is lettered round every fourth main orbit rather than every third, at 0.15 rather than 0.24 at night and 0.22 rather than 0.34 on paper. The laid-paper tile multiplies at 0.35 instead of 0.44 on paper and is screened back at 0.055 instead of 0.07 at night.

Nothing printed over the plate shares a line with anything else, and nothing prints through a leaf. The frontispiece is a leaf: while the run is still to be dealt, the chart beneath it carries no captions at all, so no node label, chart name or slingshot caption can show through the title cartouche, and a light veil of the ground sits under the title. The frontispiece and the colophon are leaves: nothing the run has to say is written onto the chart while either is on the page, and the sheet is wiped of it when a run ends. The DOM score, pace and flow sit on a leaf of their own while a run is on, a soft pass of the ground under the middle of the HUD band, feathered to nothing, so the figures never print straight across a planet that has scrolled up beneath them. The plate captions and Galileo's marginal figures are set live at the foot of the sheet rather than baked into the print, so a narrow sheet no longer crops them against the frame. The `HIC SUNT DRACONES` gloss and the Leviathan stop rising at the footer band — the bottom 70 CSS pixels, 58 on a short landscape screen, plus whatever safe-area inset the browser reports, and the frame's inner rule above that — so the flood goes on past them but they never reach the chapter name or the buttons. The two upper corner ornaments are cut at 45% of the head and half the breath and tucked further into the corner, clear of the DOM `ORBIT` and `BEST` headings; the two lower corners are unchanged. A chart's caption rides above its star, flips below it and clears the HUD band exactly as a node caption does. The HUD's centre column is taken to reach 150 pixels either side of the middle rather than 104, so a caption set beside the score keeps below the whole band; the caption on the orbit being held, which is always set below its planet so that it cannot cover the release marks, is pushed clear of the band when below would still be inside it. A constellation's name, lettered round the rim of its entry star, turns to the bottom of the rim under the same rule when the star sits too near the top edge to print it inside the frame.

Everything the run has to say is written into the map rather than over it. There is no message bar and no instruction line: each description — a slingshot taken, a transfer landed square, orbits skipped, a chart named as its stars are traced, an observation made, the pressure the opening orbit set — is lettered onto the sheet beside whatever it is about, in the plate's own small caps, on a hairline leader that runs from the subject's rim out to the lettering with a tick left where it started, and ruled underneath as a plate rules a legend. The standing instructions of the opening rows are written the same way in the marginal italic, unruled, beside the orbit being held or the black hole that is bending the flight; each is kept on the sheet while its condition holds. An inscription is set once, in world units, so the chart carries it exactly as it carries the orbit it belongs to: it keeps its place on the sheet as the ascent scrolls beneath it, and it never fades — what the pen has set down is ink, drawn at full strength until the sheet has carried it under the plate's inner rule, where it is cut off as the orbits are. It is placed in whichever of eight positions round its subject is clearest of the chart, of the traveller, of the constructions still being drawn up at either end of the last flight, and of whatever is already written, and then slid back onto the sheet, so it is never set into the frame's margin, into the score band, or into the footer. Lettering is never set over lettering: if none of the eight places is clear the note goes further out on a longer leader, or is stacked above or below whatever stands in its way, and a note beside a wandering orbit is set clear for the whole of its drift, so two notes can never be carried into each other. A standing instruction the sheet has carried off the plate is set again rather than lost, and one that stops being asked for stays where it was written, as ink, rather than being struck out; asked for again while it is still on the plate, it is taken up rather than written twice. The sheet holds at most twenty-four at once and the lowest gives way; the pen writes them a glyph at a time with the nib riding the line, reduced motion prints them whole, a paused run freezes the pen where it stopped, and a proof pulled before letters keeps them, since what the run says is not part of the engraving. The same words go to a live region of their own as they are written, so a reader hears the page. The plate's own number and name are engraved at the foot of the sheet in the frame's ink — `TAB. II · THE DRIFT` — where a printer sets a running head.

Named feats are recorded as they happen, at most once each per run, and inscribed on the chart beside the traveller. Three perfect transfers in a row is Tres Perfecti; five orbits skipped in one flight, Saltus Quinque; reaching the chart's top speed, Velocitas Summa; grazing a black hole at that speed, Periculum; completing a constellation without a single ordinary capture on its route, Linea Pura; reaching row 40, Altitudo; and surviving three minutes, Vigilia. The run's observations are listed under the table on the end screen.

Every landing is surveyed. The arrival angle is the angle between the incoming line and the orbit's radius at the contact, so a line exactly tangent to the drawn ring reads 90 degrees; a smooth entry that joined a little inside or outside the ring reads a few degrees under or over, and a hard turn toward the centre reads far below. An arrival within a degree and a half of 90 is a square: it earns a further 10 points multiplied by arrival speed beside the landing reward, counts toward the ANGULUS RECTUS observation, and the guided perfect band spans roughly 78 to 101 degrees, so about one perfect transfer in seven is a square.

Both ends of a flight are then drawn up as a geometer's construction, and the construction stays on the sheet as dried ink. At the release, over four tenths of a second, the pen lays the radius from the planet's centre out to the release point, the departure line carried thirty world units along the tangent and closed with a small arrowhead, and the release bearing: a dotted north reference from the centre up to the ring, an arc swept clockwise from that north to the release radius with a tick at each end, and its reading in engraved figures set outside the arc, 0 to 359 — `212°`. The bearing figure is kept inside the ring, at about half its radius, so it can never cross the release marks or the perfect window; it is begun at the moment of release, which is the moment the player has already gone. At the landing, over six tenths of a second and in this order, the pen draws the radius from the centre out to the contact, the incoming line taken from the arrival velocity and carried a little past it, and between the two the arrival angle: a small arc with two tick ends and its numeral, `87°`, set outside the angle on its bisector. A square is marked with the geometer's right angle instead of the arc — a small square in the corner with a dot inside it, in gold on the night plate and ochre on paper. The points of each construction are lettered as a geometer letters a figure, in the same italic hand: `a` at the centre, `b` at the point on the ring, and `c` at the far end of the line, each written as the pen reaches its point, and set across the line on the side away from the other strokes so they never sit on a stroke. Beside the construction a short note is set in Fell italic: the arrival speed as `×1.4`, the landing reward, `ANGULUS RECTUS · +N` for a square, and `SKIP N` when orbits were skipped. The nib and its wet bead ride the end of whichever stroke is in hand, exactly as they do everywhere else the pen works; reduced motion prints both constructions whole, a paused run freezes one mid-stroke, and every stroke is cut in the ink the pen is charged with. The constructions are bounded, pruned as the camera climbs and cleared when a new run is dealt, precisely as the dried route is, and at most 48 are held. The marginal score note is unchanged and the score is never repeated in the construction. A square also inscribes `RIGHT ANGLE · +N` on the orbit it was landed on, when nothing with a stronger claim is already there, under two short tones.

Each capture starts at 10 points. Releasing before 1.25 turns increases flow, up to ×5; each flow step adds 2 points per capture. A smooth tangent transfer adds 5 points, each full main node skipped adds 10, and a gold detour adds 15. These capture rewards are multiplied by arrival speed divided by 150, from ×1.0 to ×2.4, with the landing reward and skip reward each rounded to whole points. At twice the speed, the same transfer earns twice the points. The current multiplier appears below the score and on boosted landing rewards. Constellation completions remain a fixed 60 points, and grazing a black hole remains 5 once per hole.

The end screen's table reports orbits, perfect transfers, best flow, and the row reached. The highest row of any run is kept separately from the score, and a run that beats it is marked with a note under the score. Local browser storage holds the best score in `orbit.best.v1`, the highest row in `orbit.bestRow.v1`, the sound preference in `orbit.sound.v1`, the difficulty in `orbit.difficulty.v1`, the chosen plate in `orbit.plate.v1`, the daily plate's record in `orbit.daily.v1` as `{"date","best"}`, and the almanac of drawn daily plates in `orbit.dailyLog.v1`.

The player is drawn as the pen itself: an engraved quill whose nib leads, cut to a point at the traveller's exact position and turned along the flight, with the barrel and feather trailing behind it. The vane flexes back as the flight quickens and breathes a little, and is held still under reduced motion; the nib is keylined light-on-dark so the moving point stays legible over a pale planet, and on paper a ring of reserved, unprinted sheet separates it from the vane. A bead of wet ink at the point brightens with the charge in hand. The whole route the run has flown stays on the chart as dried ink beneath the wet trail: a world-space line, one wash pass and three weights of burin stroke, heavier where the flight was faster, so a slingshot orbit leaves a ring and a long transfer a straight rule. It is bounded twice — everything that has passed below the sheet is dropped as the camera climbs, and a hard cap of three thousand points holds the rest — cleared when a new run is dealt, kept under reduced motion since a line already on the page is not motion, and frozen with a paused run because nothing is sampled. Its ink is registered per plate and follows the chosen trail ink. The wet trail follows the actual flight path, tapers with age, dries into the route at its head so the two are one line, and broadens subtly at slingshot speeds. Each segment is laid wet and dries as it ages: glossy blue-black to matte sepia on paper, bright to dim ink at night. A bead of ink pools where the player let go and dries lighter over about a second and a half. Captures leave a broken etched ripple at the planet; perfect transfers add a second ring, survey ticks, and a small fan at the contact point. Scores are written up as marginal notes in Fell italic beside the play field, each with a small engraved manicule pointing back in at the event. Effects pause with the run. Reduced motion shortens the trail and removes decorative filaments from the trail, particle bursts, expanding ripples, the creep of the pricked guide, the pooling of a release blot, marginal-note drift, and screen flashes. Trail history stays bounded and is cleared on restart.

The game fills the available viewport and requests the browser's Fullscreen API on the first tap when supported. The corner control toggles fullscreen. On browsers without that API, including some iPhone browsers, the game uses the full available dynamic viewport. Switching away pauses the run; tap to resume.

A real new run opens on three parallel targets, side by side in place of the usual single first node, each captioned in the atlas's own Latin rather than a modern difficulty label: **Tiro** the raw recruit, **Adeptus** the one who has attained, and **Magister** the master. Each is drawn as its own kind of world so the choice reads before its caption is even legible — Tiro a calm ocean world with a verdant ink accent, Adeptus the plainest ringed world in the atlas's ordinary ink, and Magister a volcanic world ringed in rubrication red — and all three are revealed at once so every choice is visible before the player commits. The opening orbit itself is held at a gentler pace than ordinary play, and the rising darkness does not advance at all while the choice is pending, so there is no rush to read the three worlds before aiming. Course generation likewise waits for the choice; capturing one sets the run's pressure, removes the other two, restores the ordinary pace, and continues exactly as an ordinary capture would. The choice scales how quickly the rising darkness accelerates thereafter (×0.72, ×1, ×1.35), is remembered in local storage for the next visit, and every other rule stays the same. The daily plate and every fixed course skip the offer and open on the ordinary single node.

Beside them is the daily plate. Switched on, the run's seed comes from a stable hash of the current UTC date, so everyone playing on the same day is given the same chart and every attempt that day repeats it. The daily plate is always played at Adeptus pressure, and its best is kept separately from the ordinary best under that date, so a daily attempt never disturbs the record for the free courses. The title and end screens read `Tabula diei` with the date, and the end screen offers a copy button that puts a line like `Orbit · Tabula diei 2026-09-03 · 420 points · row 31 · 2 constellations` on the clipboard where the browser allows it. The toggle itself is not remembered between visits: a new visit always opens on the ordinary chart.

Beside the switch on the frontispiece is the **ephemeris**, an almanac leaf of the daily plates: a ruled month table in the same hand as the catalogue, headed with the Latin month and the year in Roman numerals and its columns with the days of the planetary week, `Sol` through `Sat`. A day is written into the log the moment a daily run begins on it, and only while it is still that day; the ephemeris then prints that square as an entry in gold, the day's numeral over its best score, and choosing it deals that day's plate again from that date's own seed at the same Adeptus pressure. Every other square is printed too, and closed: a past day that was never drawn is a numeral over a blank rule, a day still to come is a numeral alone. Today's square is always open, captioned `hodie` until it has been drawn, and the plate now in hand is pricked with a small lozenge. A repeat says so — `Tabula diei 2026-09-01 · iterum` on the title and end screens, `(iterum)` in the copied line — and never enters a day in the log or touches the current day's record, though it does improve the record of the day it repeats. The leaf turns between the month of the earliest plate on record and the current month and no further, opens on the month of whichever plate is in hand, holds the gameplay input while it is open, and closes on its own button or on Escape. The log is one JSON document under `orbit.dailyLog.v1`, a map of date to `{"best","plays"}`; a day that predates it is folded in once from `orbit.daily.v1`, and a blocked or malformed store simply reads as an empty almanac, in which case today's plate is the only one open.

Two carried charges are captioned in the atlas's own Latin rather than a modern power-up label, the way Tiro, Adeptus and Magister are. A pale blue **Scutum** star appears roughly every ten main rows, off the main line like the gold detours. Capturing one arms a single charge, shown at the top of the screen and as a faint ring around the ship. The next contact with a black hole consumes the charge and deflects the flight outward instead of ending the run; only one charge can be carried at a time.

A violet **Repulsa** star appears roughly every thirteen main rows, off the main line the same way. Capturing one arms a charge of its own, shown beneath the Scutum line and as a broken, wider ring around the ship, so the two read apart at a glance even when both are carried together. The next time the flight would leave the chart's edge, the charge turns it back into the chart instead of ending the run — the sideways velocity is reversed and the traveller is set a little clear of the margin — rather than deflecting off a hazard; only one charge can be carried at a time. Without a charge in hand, the same exit is still a loss, spilling ink at the edge the flight left by rather than the ordinary burst.

A landing too steep to earn anything still counts toward something else: a lifetime total of them unlocks five more trail inks, common pigments first and precious ones last, the way the slingshot ladder runs chalk to gold leaf — **Umber**, **Woad**, **Vermilion**, **Malachite**, and finally **Ultramarine**, ground from lapis lazuli and once worth more than gold, at 10, 25, 75, 200 and 500 arrivals below the sixteen-degree graze.

A warm brown inkwell appears roughly every seventeen main rows. It always scores as an ordinary orbit, whether or not it pays out anything further. What it pays out depends on the streak standing when it is reached: three or more captures in a row, each one either landed below the sixteen-degree graze or a flight that skipped an orbit to get there, still earns a fresh colour for the trail; any capture in between that does neither breaks the streak, and an inkwell reached on a clean run simply runs dry. Filling enough of them on a standing streak earns the two hardest trail inks in the catalogue: **Bistre**, a warm sooty brown, and then **Orpiment**, the old illuminators' bright and faintly dangerous yellow-orange.

## Plates

A cached laid-paper tile is pressed over the finished frame in one fill per frame: laid wires every 1.5 pixels, chain lines every 27, and short fibres. On the paper plate it multiplies over everything, so every stroke breaks across the wires instead of lying on top of them; at night the same tile is screened back at a whisper. The score band and the screens are DOM and are unaffected; the inscriptions are on the plate and take the tile with everything else.

The atlas can be printed on either of two plates, chosen with the page icon in the footer and remembered in local storage. The **night plate** is the original artwork: pale ink and starlight on indigo. The **paper plate** is the same chart pulled on aged cream laid paper, with iron-gall sepia and black ink, ochre where the night plate uses gold, rubrication red-brown where it uses copper, and Prussian blue for drifting orbits. The sheet carries laid and chain lines, fibres, foxing, old tide marks, and a dilute ecliptic with hour numerals. On paper the planets are hand-coloured specimens with an off-register keyline and hatched night sides, black holes are pooled ink blots, and the rising darkness is literally spilled ink bleeding up the page along the fibres. Every render section registers its colours for both plates in one place, so the night plate is unchanged by the paper work. Switching plates rebuilds the cached artwork and never touches the simulation.

Both plates sit inside an engraved frame: a plate-mark where the copper pressed the sheet, a heavy double rule, and a graduated scale with right-ascension hours along the top and bottom and declination degrees down the sides that scroll with the ascent. A cherubic wind-head is cut into each of the four corners, a few dozen burin strokes each, seeded so no two are the same head, blowing along the diagonal into the chart at the margin's own tone. Wide screens add a compass rose in the left flank with `SEPTENTRIO · ORIENS · MERIDIES · OCCIDENS` lettered round its ring, a scale bar, an engraver's credit, and a MAGNITUDINES key to the six star forms, all in the margins outside the play channel. The frame is cached and costs one blit per frame.

Every engraved stroke in the atlas is cut by the same pair of burin primitives: the width swells and tapers along the run, the path wobbles by a fraction of a pixel, and the line skips where the tool lifted. They draw orbit rings, lit constellation routes, planet keylines, capture ripples and perfect-transfer rings, black-hole edges and accretion rings, and the plate frame's double rule. Static shapes are baked once into sprites or into the cached frame layer and blitted; the strokes drawn live each frame use short segment counts. Each orbit ring also carries a second faint hairline pass where the hand went round twice.

Every mark on the chart is drawn as the traveller reaches it. A render-side map keyed by element identity holds when the pen began each mark and how long it takes; it reads the simulation clock, so pausing freezes the pen mid-stroke, and reduced motion prints the whole sheet at once. The cartographer works in view: a planet, hazard or chart begins as soon as it has entered the top of the view by 24 world units and is finished within a second and a quarter — the orbit ring by six tenths of that, the planet's stages by nine, the caption a glyph every 40 ms afterwards. The view keeps the traveller well below its top, so the next ring is closed before the player can reach it even on a long transfer at full speed. At most three marks are in hand at once, and anything already inside the view is begun immediately, so a fork with two routes and three stars queues instead of stuttering while nothing that can kill you is ever invisible. Rings, their capture bands and their ticks are cut by a wedge opening from the ring's own start angle, with a bead of wet ink at the leading end. Planets are staged as a colourist works: the keyline cut around by angle, the hatching swept in by a band travelling along the strokes, the wash bloomed as an irregular blot from a seeded point off centre under a wet rim that dries lighter over the last third, the survey arcs and rings last. Black holes, flares and nebulas are a drop of ink that spreads through a ragged mask and darkens before the accretion rings are scratched in by wedge. Cached figure layers sweep along the spine through their three stars, and a route line is held back above whichever planet is still being drawn. A nib on a hairline shaft, with the occasional seeded fleck of spatter, rides the leading end of whichever stroke is in progress, and one very quiet scratch of the brushed noise marks its start. A used orbit is struck through with a single diagonal hairline over 300 ms before it dries to a hairline, and the plate frame draws itself once per run: the double rule by dash offset, then the graduated ticks around the perimeter, then the marginal ornaments, over 1.4 seconds on the opening sheet and half a second on a restart. Only a mark still being drawn costs anything; a finished one is drawn exactly as it always was, and the simulation is never told any of this is happening.

The chapter lettering is written rather than printed. `scripts/glyphs.mjs` reads the Fell faces out of the embedded stylesheet with fontkit, flattens and simplifies the capitals, figures, middle dot and solidus the plates actually set, and writes `src/glyphs.js`: advance widths and contours at 512 units to the em, about twenty kilobytes, committed, so neither the runtime nor the build nor the workflow ever needs the dependency. At runtime each letter's contours are stroked on by dash offset with a bead of wet ink at the nib and its counters then flood with ink, about 120 ms a letter and overlapping, and the rule beneath draws outward from its centre. Once the writing is finished — and always under reduced motion — the ordinary text rendering takes over, unchanged.

The same hand writes the DOM. A changed score, pace, flow or shield line is wiped in with a small nib crossing it; the frontispiece's title settles letter by letter while the cartouche draws itself around it; the colophon rules its leaf, its score frame and its table before the numbers are written into them. Every base style is the finished state and every animation starts from the unwritten one, so the reduced-motion rule already in the stylesheet prints all of it at once.

Lettering also runs on curves, set glyph by glyph and turned tangent to the arc, with a letter-spacing of its own: browser text metrics are used where the canvas provides them and an average advance where it does not. Three things are lettered this way. Every third main orbit carries a Latin caption round its outer rim in small caps at a whisper, drawn from a small seeded vocabulary — `ORBITA · TABULA · MOTUS · SILENTIUM · ASCENSUS · VIGILIA` — and printed only on orbits the player is not holding, so it can never cross the release marks. A live constellation's name is engraved round the rim of its entry star. The compass rose's four winds are named round its ring.

The background stars are set in six magnitude classes derived from each star's own brightness and size: a plain dot for the sixth, a ringed dot for the fifth, then four-, six- and eight-pointed forms, and a haloed eight-point for the first. The first magnitude is rare, a handful of stars in the field.

Lettering uses IM Fell English, IM Fell English SC, and IM Fell DW Pica, digitisations of the Fell types cut in the 1600s and used at Oxford, under the SIL Open Font License. The title screen is an engraved frontispiece with a strapwork cartouche, a Latin subtitle, and a printer's line; the end screen is a colophon with a ruled table of the run, pulled on its own leaf — a near-opaque paper panel inside a double engraved rule, so every line stays legible however far the spilled ink has risen behind it.

Each constellation fork carries the figure it is named for, drawn behind its three stars in the manner of Hevelius. All twelve catalogue entries are cut: a sailmaker's needle threaded through the stars; a lateen sail on its spar; a chelys lyre; a jewelled diadem; a pair of dividers hinged on the top star, gripping its lead at the middle one, with a graduated sector arc across the opening; an hourglass whose waist pinches at the middle star and whose plates rest on the outer two; a serpent whose body crosses the spine at each star and rears into a head above; a ship with its keel deepest at the bottom star, its mast stepped on the middle one and its truck on the top; an astrolabe whose graduated limb rings the middle star and whose alidade carries a sighting vane on each of the others; a quill cut to a nib at the bottom star, opening its vane at the middle one; a hanging lantern whose flame at the middle star throws its light past the glass; and a moth with its wings either side of the middle star and its feathered antennae on the top one. Each figure is mirrored onto the side its fork branches from. All are broken ink contours that hatch in as stars are visited, take a dilute wash on completion, and fade to hairlines when a chart expires. Each is cached per chart and stays clear of the orbit rims. A generic asterism is kept only as a fallback for a name the plate has no engraving for.

The chapter plates quote Galileo. The Quiet's moon has a Sidereus Nuncius terminator with crater rims catching light on the dark side; The Drift carries his 1610 sketch of Saturn as a disc with two handles beside the caption; The Eclipse shows sunspot groups with hatched penumbrae across the disc; The Deep notes Jupiter and the Medicean stars as a small circle and four asterisks. Each plate has a second caption line naming the figure.

## Catalogue

Everything the atlas records is kept in one JSON document under `orbit.ledger.v1`: lifetime `captures` and `perfects`, the best `bestFlow` and `bestRow`, `constellations` as a map of figure name to completions, `deepestChapter` and `deepestHardcoreChapter`, `grazes`, `shieldsSpent`, `reflectorsSpent`, `maxSpeedSlings` (slingshot stars left at the chart's top speed), `inkwellsFound` (inkwells filled while a reckless streak still stood), `badAngles` (lifetime arrivals too steep to score), `runs` and `personalBests` as maps keyed by difficulty with daily runs counted under `daily`, `playSeconds`, `observations` as a map of key to count, and `allFourInOneRun`. The run's own figures are tallied in memory from the same simulation events the screens are drawn from, and folded into the document when the run ends and whenever the page is hidden, so a run that is never finished still counts what it did; a second fold adds only what has happened since the first. Blocked storage and a document that is not JSON are ordinary conditions: the ledger falls back to an empty one and the game plays exactly as it does with a full one. The two records that predate it, `orbit.best.v1` and `orbit.bestRow.v1`, are folded in once on the first load and left where they are — the best score becomes the personal best for the difficulty last played.

Storage keys are therefore `orbit.best.v1`, `orbit.bestRow.v1`, `orbit.sound.v1`, `orbit.difficulty.v1`, `orbit.plate.v1`, `orbit.daily.v1`, `orbit.dailyLog.v1` (the days whose daily plate was drawn on the day itself, as a map of date to `{best, plays}`), `orbit.ledger.v1`, `orbit.cosmetics.v1` (the chosen cosmetics as `{plate, mark, trail, capture, frame, figures}`), and `orbit.initials.v1` (three letters). The plate is written to both `orbit.plate.v1` and the cosmetics document, and `orbit.plate.v1` wins when the two disagree; a selection naming something the ledger has not earned is never honoured.

Forty entries can be earned. Six plates: the **Cellarius plate**, gold leaf on a deep blue ground, at 1,000 lifetime captures; the **Verdigris plate**, green-tinted copper on dark, for reaching chapter IV, The Deep, at Magister pressure; the **Foxed plate**, a sheet left a further century in a damp room, at 100 runs; the **Proof before letters**, the paper plate pulled with no captions, labels, numerals or legend at all, for tracing four constellations in one run; the **Carta azzurra**, blue prepared paper as the Florentine workshops made it, on which the night plate's pale ink becomes white heightening over a blue-grey ground, for grazing 25 black holes; and the **Sepia plate**, the whole chart in one brown ink as Galileo washed his moons — no hand-colouring, no rubrication, no Prussian blue, every body a sepia wash under a sepia line on the cream of the sheet — for tracing twelve constellations in all. Four observer marks: the **comet** the atlas used to be travelled by, at 25 runs, and then a Galilean **telescope**, a **moth**, and **Saturn with handles** as Galileo drew it, at 100, 500 and 1,500 perfect transfers. The quill is the default and needs no earning. Ten trail inks: red-chalk **sanguine**, **silverpoint** with a faint travelling shimmer, and **gold leaf** laid over a dark keyline, at 10, 50 and 200 slingshot stars left at top speed; five more for a lifetime of arrivals too steep to score — **umber**, **woad**, **vermilion**, **malachite** and **ultramarine** — at 10, 25, 75, 200 and 500 of them; and finally, hardest of all, sooty **bistre** and the old illuminators' **orpiment**, at 3 and 10 inkwells filled while a reckless streak still stood. Three capture marks — a compass **rose**, a wax **seal**, and a printer's **manicule** — at 250, 1,000 and 5,000 lifetime captures. Three frame ornaments — interlaced **strapwork**, **acanthus** scrolls, and **sea monsters** in the two lower corners — at best rows 20, 40 and 60. Two figure styles, the finer and more geometric **Bayer** hand at ten completions of any single constellation and the heavier, far more shaded **Bode** hand at twenty-five. The engraver's credit **Delineavit**, which sets the player's initials in the frame's engraver line, at 50 runs; and the **Ex libris** stamp on the colophon once the ledger holds a score at all three pressures. The remaining ten are the atlas's own named feats struck as medals — **Tres Perfecti**, **Saltus Quinque**, **Velocitas Summa**, **Periculum**, **Linea Pura**, **Altitudo**, **Vigilia**, **Angulus Rectus**, **Evasio**, and **Myrias** — each earned lifetime: the first eight the first time the ledger has ever recorded that observation, the last two simply a lifetime count crossing its own threshold; and listed under their own Named feats heading rather than among the six chosen categories, since a medal is held rather than selected. What is earned stays earned, and each category keeps one selection, defaulting to the classic look: the night plate, the quill, iron gall, the broken ripple, the wind-heads, and the Hevelius figures.

The cosmetics run through the machinery the two plates already use. A plate may now be *derived*: it names `night` or `paper` as its base and passes every token that base registers through one colour transform, so `definePlate` builds all six plates from the same two sets of values and `ink` still points at whichever is on the press. Cellarius, Verdigris and the Carta azzurra are duotones over the night plate — every tone re-inked along a three-stop ramp from ground to highlight, the blue paper's running from blue-grey through a paler grey to lead white — and Foxed ages the paper plate toward foxing brown without disturbing its hues, while Proof is a clean duotone that also sets the flag the lettering checks. Sepia is a duotone over the paper plate whose ramp is read so that its light stop is reached at the sheet's own tone rather than at white, so the ground keeps its cream while everything printed on it goes to the one brown; and because the planet painters use a few colours as literals rather than tokens — a shoreline's verdigris, a fissure's glow, a storm's indigo bands — this plate also presses every finished planet layer through its tint pixel by pixel, once, when the specimen is cached. The painted sheet underneath is dressed to match: the base plate's own fibres, laid lines and tide marks are washed toward the new ground colour and then given whatever else belongs to the plate — gold leaf, oxidation, a prepared blue ground with its pale fibres and pooled edges, or another century of foxing. The footer's page icon still cycles night and paper only; the other four are chosen in the catalogue, and every change rebuilds the cached artwork exactly as switching plates always has. On the proof plate the pen simply never writes: node captions and numerals, rim captions, constellation names, chapter lettering, the frame's numerals, legend, scale label, credit and wind names, the chapter plates' captions and the gloss on the flood are all skipped, and the figures, rings and ornaments are printed as usual.

The other five categories are the same idea one level down. Observer marks are alternative glyphs cut in the same local space — heading along the axis, the moving point at the origin — and every one of them carries the same shield ring and either the shared comet head or, as the quill does, its own keylined point, so the traveller stays legible over a pale planet whatever is chosen. Trail inks are registered per plate like every other colour, as a wet and a dry tone with the wash, dry-brush edge, bleed and release bead that go with them; silverpoint adds a shimmer that travels along the stroke and gold leaf a dark keyline beneath it, neither under reduced motion, and each carries the dried tone the run's route is printed in. Capture marks are alternative drawings of the ripple, in the planet's own space with the contact point along the axis. Frame ornaments are cut into the same four corners with the same burin, seeded, at the margin's tone — except the sea monsters, which are printed in the flood's own pigment so they still read once the page is half drowned. Figure styles are one object consulted by the four primitives every engraving is built from, and by the pen the layer is cut with, whose line weight is scaled as it is set, so all twelve figures answer to the chosen hand without being rewritten.

The catalogue itself is a ruled library page in Fell type, opened with the book icon in the footer from the title and end screens and closed by its own button or Escape. It opens on two tabs, **Record** and **Catalogue**, both always drawn onto the leaf with only the inactive one out of sight, so switching between them never redraws either. `Record` prints the ledger's figures as it always has — orbits captured, perfect transfers, constellations traced, highest row, runs, and time in the chart — and beside them everything else the ledger keeps that otherwise surfaces nowhere in the game on its own: best flow, the deepest chapter reached at any pressure and at Magister's, black holes grazed, Scutum and Repulsa charges spent, slingshot stars left at top speed, inkwells filled on a reckless streak, and arrivals too steep to score, followed by the daily streak, current and longest, read off the ephemeris's own log. Under that, one table gives the best score and the runs played at each of the three pressures and under the daily plate; a second lists all eight named feats by their Latin caption against how many times each has fired in all; a third lists all twelve catalogue figures against how many times each has been traced, an untraced one printing a plain nought rather than dropping from the list. `Catalogue` holds what the leaf has always held: one ruled section per category listing every item with its Latin caption. An earned item is a button that selects it; an unearned one is a blank rule with its condition and progress set greyed beside it. A three-letter initials field appears once the engraver's credit is earned. The page scrolls inside its own leaf on a phone, holds the gameplay input while it is open, and is printed on whichever plate is on the press. When a run's write earns something new, the colophon prints an engraved line, `NEW IN THE CATALOGUE · <name>`, under two short tones; nothing else interrupts play.

## Source

The source lives in `src/` as classic scripts that share one global scope, listed in load order in `src/index.html`. `simulation.js` holds the independent `OrbitWorld` class: seeded generation, fixed-step movement, swept collisions, scoring, and difficulty. `audio.js` synthesizes the Web Audio sounds. `plates.js` owns the render globals, storage, and the night and paper colour tokens along with the derived plates built from them; `ledger.js` holds the persistent ledger, the catalogue of unlockables and the chosen cosmetics; `backdrop.js` paints the two sheets; `planets.js` builds the procedural specimens; `celestial.js` draws the chapter plates and ambient events; `marks.js` bakes the burin rings, the route lines and the lettering set along a curve; `glyphs.js` holds the extracted Fell outlines and `reveal.js` the living pen: reveal state, the nib, and the masks each kind of mark is drawn through; `figures.js` draws constellation figures, nodes, lenses, hazards, and the aim guide; `inscriptions.js` writes what the run has to say onto the chart; `effects.js` paints the rising darkness, the comet, and its trail; `frame.js` builds the plate frame and composes each frame; `ephemeris.js` rules the almanac leaf of the daily plates and deals a past one again; `ui.js` handles the screens, viewport, and the single input. `scripts/bundle.mjs` inlines the modules into one script for both the build and the tests.

Optional development commands use only Node.js built-ins:

```
npm start
npm test
npm run build
npm run glyphs
```

`npm run glyphs` is the only command that needs a dependency (fontkit, a dev dependency). It regenerates `src/glyphs.js` from the faces embedded in `assets/fonts.css` and is needed only when those faces change.

The verification script checks the main, constellation, and charged-shortcut paths on 60 generated courses through row 48, including 360 completed constellations and 360 shortcut transfers, and runs the constellation route on the same 60 courses through row 60 to prove that the forks past the fourth region are reachable and completable. It checks exact tangent contacts in both directions at three speeds, moving-target guide agreement, symmetric gravitational flybys, retained speed, curved landing predictions at three speeds, collision warnings, lens sampling at clipped viewport edges, star acceleration and caps, speed-based rewards, long-flight regressions, darkness pressure and the perfect-streak relief, the opening difficulty choice setting the run's pressure and clearing the two unchosen targets, rewards and expiry, duplicate capture protection, bounded generation, pause/restart, and complete-script execution at five viewport sizes using native-API stand-ins.
