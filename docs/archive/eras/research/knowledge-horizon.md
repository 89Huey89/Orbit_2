# The knowledge horizon — what each century could know of a body

Research behind [../KNOWLEDGE-HORIZON.md](../KNOWLEDGE-HORIZON.md): the history of astronomy's
ability to say anything about a *body's surface*, as opposed to its position, laid against the
nine-era ladder. Every date in the design file and in the eras' *The bodies* sections is to be read
against this file, which is the one that cites.

**Sourcing note.** This pass ran `WebSearch` on every claim marked *verified* below and read the
returned snippets; no primary page was fetched end to end, so the standard here is "a reputable
page says so, and its URL is in the Sources," not "the primary text was read." A claim marked
*(unverified this pass)* is carried on general knowledge and should be checked before an artist
keys it. Two claims in the earlier discussion draft were wrong and are corrected in §4.

## 1. What this file is for

The design asks each era to show only what its own moment could know about a body, and it rests
on three historical claims: that before the telescope no astronomy differentiated planetary
surfaces at all, only brightness and the Moon's face; that the telescope tied each of the game's
seven families to one named body and one dated observation, and misread two of them for decades;
and that the families became a general *typology* not with the exoplanets of 1995 but in the
flyby years of 1965–1989, which fall in the gap between era VII and era VIII. This file checks
those three claims and the dates the design hangs on them.

The gap the table exposes is real: five of the seven "became a class" entries fall between 1965
and 1989, and none before 1965. Era VII closes in 1958 and era VIII opens in 1990.

## 3. Era by era

### Before the telescope (eras I–V)

**Rock.** No secure evidence of planet-tracking in the Upper Palaeolithic. The Lascaux
"Pleiades" reading, Marshack's lunar notations and the Warren Field pit alignment are contested
minority readings; [rock.md](rock.md) §12 already carries this and it was not re-searched here
*(unverified this pass, carried from rock.md)*.

**Disc.** The Venus tablet of Ammisaduqa records Venus's risings and settings over 21 years;
the surviving tablets are seventh-century BCE copies from Nineveh and the observations are
placed in Ammisaduqa's reign, c. 1646–1626 BCE, so planet-tracking is roughly contemporary with
the Nebra disc (c. 1600 BCE) though not in the Nebra culture *(verified)*. The disc itself
carries no planet; [disc.md](disc.md) §2 has its contents.

**Ceiling.** Senenmut's ceiling (TT353) shows Jupiter, Saturn, Mercury and Venus as deities in
barques; **Mars is absent**, a feature Neugebauer and Parker make characteristic of the whole
"Senmut family" of ceilings, and one reading has Mars as the empty barque in the west, retrograde
and away from the others *(verified)*. The Egyptian names: Jupiter "Horus who bounds (or
illuminates) the Two Lands"; Saturn "Horus, Bull of the Sky"; Mars "Horus the Red", the most
transparent of them; Venus "the crossing star"; Mercury *sbg(w)*, of unknown meaning *(verified,
Oxford Research Encyclopedia of Planetary Science)*. No Egyptian document differentiates a body's
face *(unverified this pass, by absence)*.

**Marble.** Hipparchus divided the stars into six classes of brightness, c. 129 BCE, and
Ptolemy's *Almagest* (c. 150 CE) preserves the system in a catalogue of 1,022 stars in 48
constellations, the direct ancestor of the modern magnitude scale *(verified)*. Ptolemy gives one
colour word, *ὑπόκιρρος*, to exactly six stars: Aldebaran, Arcturus, Pollux, Antares, Betelgeuse
and Sirius; it has traditionally been read "reddish", is argued by some to mean "yellowish" or
"less than yellow", and Sirius's inclusion is the famous "red Sirius" puzzle *(verified)*. The
*Almagest* puts the Moon's mean distance at 59 Earth radii and the Sun's at 1,210 *(unverified
this pass)*. Plutarch (c. 46–120 CE), *De facie in orbe lunae*, treats the Moon as an earth with
a face, and is the acknowledged source of much of Galileo's description of it *(verified)*.
Aristotle's division of a changeless heaven from a changing sublunary world is general knowledge
and was not searched *(unverified this pass)*.

**Globe.** Al-Ṣūfī's *Book of Fixed Stars*, 964 CE, gives positions, magnitudes and colours for
the *Almagest* stars, re-observed; he records the Andromeda nebula as *al-laṭkha al-saḥābiyya*,
"the little cloud", and the Large Magellanic Cloud as *al-Bakr*, the White Ox, of the southern
Arabs *(verified)*. [globe.md](globe.md) carries the rest of the era.

### The telescope (era VI, 1600–1801)

- Galileo, *Sidereus Nuncius*, March 1610: the Moon's mountains and craters, Jupiter's moons
  *(verified, general)*. Saturn: in July 1610 Galileo circulated the anagram of *altissimum
  planetam tergeminum observavi*, "I have observed the highest planet triform" *(verified)*.
- Huygens resolved the handles into a ring in February 1656, announced Titan in *De Saturni luna
  observatio nova* (1656) with the ring hidden in an anagram — *annulo cingitur, tenui, plano,
  nusquam cohaerente, ad eclipticam inclinato* — and published the theory in full in *Systema
  Saturnium*, 1659 *(verified)*. The Cassini Division, 1675 *(unverified this pass)*.
- Riccioli's *Almagestum Novum*, 1651, with Grimaldi's map, introduced the nomenclature still
  used — *Mare*, *Oceanus*, *Terra*, craters named for scholars — and *Mare Tranquillitatis* was
  named there *(verified)*.
- Jupiter: Hooke reported a spot in 1664, probably in another belt and possibly a moon's shadow;
  Cassini's "permanent spot" of 1665 was followed to 1713; a 48-year gap makes its identity with
  today's Great Red Spot inconclusive, and recent work places the present spot's first
  observation in 1831 *(verified)*.
- Mars: Cassini saw a polar cap in 1666, Huygens the south cap in 1672; Herschel's 1784 paper
  "On the remarkable appearances at the polar regions of the planet Mars" described their
  seasonal growth and shrinking, and most observers took them for water frost until the space
  probes *(verified)*. Herschel's Martian seas and inhabitants *(unverified this pass)*.
- Herschel, "An Account of Three Volcanos in the Moon", read 26 April 1787, *Phil. Trans.* 77,
  229–232: three volcanoes seen on the dark side on 19 April, one "in actual eruption"
  *(verified)*. Modern readings make them earthshine on bright craters or an impact flash.
- Herschel, "On the Nature and Construction of the Sun and Fixed Stars", *Phil. Trans.* 85
  (1795), 46–72: the visible Sun is a luminous atmosphere over a planet-like body, the spots
  openings in it, and the Sun "inhabited like the rest of the planets"; modified in 1801 with a
  shielding layer of dark cloud *(verified)*. **This is 1795, not 1781**; 1781 is Uranus.
- Cassini and Richer, 1672: Mars's parallax from Paris and Cayenne at the October opposition
  gave an Earth–Sun distance of about 140 million km, the first fairly accurate value *(verified)*.
- Sunspots: Harriot December 1610, Johannes Fabricius's publication June 1611, Scheiner 1611,
  Galileo's *Letters on Sunspots* 1613 *(unverified this pass)*.

### The plate (era VII, 1838–1958)

- Bessel announced the parallax of 61 Cygni in 1838, the first stellar distance, narrowly ahead
  of Struve and Henderson *(verified)*.
- Hubble found the first Cepheid in Andromeda on 5 October 1923, published distances to M31, M33
  and NGC 6822 in 1925, and the velocity–distance relation on 15 March 1929 *(verified)*.
- Schiaparelli's *canali*, 1877; Lowell observing from 1894 and publishing *Mars* in 1895
  *(verified, general)*. Antoniadi at Meudon's 83 cm Grande Lunette during the 1909 opposition:
  "the geometrical 'canal' network is an optical illusion; and in its place the great refractor
  shows myriads of marbled and chequered objective fields, which no artist could ever think of
  drawing"; his *La Planète Mars* followed in 1930 *(verified)*.
- Vulcan: Le Verrier's unexplained 43″ per century of Mercury's perihelion, 1859; Lescarbault's
  transit of 1859; Le Verrier's announcement of 1860; retired by general relativity in 1915
  *(verified)*.

### The flyby years (1965–1989, the gap)

- Mariner 4, 15 July 1965: 22 pictures of a cratered, lunar-looking Mars, the first close-up
  photographs of another planet *(verified)*.
- Mariner 9, in orbit from November 1971: Olympus Mons and the Tharsis volcanoes, canyons, river
  valleys, "vast dune fields" and the polar ice *(verified)*.
- Mariner 10, closest approach 29 March 1974: Mercury cratered like the Moon *(verified)*.
- Uranus's rings: 10 March 1977, a stellar occultation observed from the Kuiper Airborne
  Observatory by Elliot, Dunham and Mink *(verified)*.
- Jupiter's ring: Voyager 1, 4 March 1979 *(verified)*. Io's volcanism: Linda Morabito, 9 March
  1979, a 270 km plume off the limb in a navigation frame taken the day before; eight plumes in
  the first set *(verified)*.
- Neptune: rings confirmed by Voyager 2 on 22 August 1989; the Great Dark Spot imaged late on
  23 August; closest approach 25 August 1989 *(verified)*.
- Europa's subsurface ocean from the Galileo magnetometer (Kivelson et al., 2000); Titan's dunes
  from Cassini radar, 2005–06; "comparative planetology" as a named discipline in the 1960s–70s
  *(all unverified this pass)*.

### The observatory (era VIII, 1990–)

- 51 Pegasi b, announced 6 October 1995 by Mayor and Queloz: the first planet around a Sun-like
  star and the first hot Jupiter *(verified)*. Kepler launched 6 March 2009 (7 March UTC); TESS
  launched 18 April 2018 *(verified)*. JWST's first science images, July 2022 *(unverified this
  pass)*.
- "Ocean world", "lava world", "ice giant", "super-Earth" as working vocabulary:
  [observatory.md](observatory.md) §Names carries what was found for the era; not re-searched here.

## 4. Corrections to the earlier draft of KNOWLEDGE-HORIZON.md

1. **Herschel's solid, inhabited Sun is 1795**, *Phil. Trans.* 85, not 1781. The design file and
   the engraving's bodies table now say 1795.
2. **The Venus tablet is contemporary with Nebra**, not "many millennia later" than it: its
   observations belong to c. 1646–1626 BCE. It remains many millennia later than Lascaux, and
   the Nebra culture itself has no attested planet-tracking, which is the claim that matters.
3. **Huygens's ring is 1656 in the observation and 1659 in the book**; the draft's bare "1655"
   is Titan's year. The caption pair in the engraving is now `1610` and `1659`.
4. **The Ceiling has four planets, not five.** Mars is absent from Senenmut's northern panel.
   The design file and the ceiling's bodies section now draw four barques and, on one reading,
   an empty one in the west.
5. **"Volcanic" is better hung on Herschel's 1787 lunar volcanoes than on sunspots**, which the
   draft used only because they were the one "fire" the century drew. Both are now in the
   engraving's table, the sunspot as the glance and the volcanoes as the attention reading.
6. **Cassini's 1665 spot may not be today's Great Red Spot.** The storm caption keeps 1665 but
   the era file notes the 1831 caveat.
7. **The families became a typology in 1965–1989**, not with the exoplanets of 1995; the
   observatory's claim to be "simply what the period's astronomy says" is true, but the period
   begins with Mariner and Voyager, which the ladder does not currently own. Open question I.

## 5. Still open

Everything marked *(unverified this pass)* above, in particular: the sunspot dates of 1610–1613;
Huygens's Syrtis Major of 1659; Ptolemy's distances of 59 and 1,210 Earth radii; Herschel's
Martian seas; the Cassini Division of 1675; the Voyager ice moons; Europa's ocean; Titan's dunes;
the first dated use of "comparative planetology"; and the exoplanet class vocabulary. None of
them changes the shape of the design; several change a caption an artist would key.
