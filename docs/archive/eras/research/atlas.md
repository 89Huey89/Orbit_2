# Research — V · The Atlas (1540–1763)

This file holds the research behind the atlas's (era V's) chapter lines and chart notes: the
Renaissance plate a run ships with today, whose lines are set in `src/ui.js`. Each line a player
reads is entered in `docs/CLAIMS.json`; a line moves off "unchecked" only once the source for it is
cited here. Where a line describes one of the atlas's own invented figures (Acus, Laterna,
Phalæna), that clause is the game speaking of itself and needs no source; the history set beside it
does.

## Claims register checks (2026-10-02)

A note on method: the proxy this pass ran behind refused direct fetches of most institutional pages
(Britannica, arXiv, the IAU archive, the Fitzwilliam, Ian Ridpath's *Star Tales*), so each verdict
below rests on the publisher's own text as returned by search, from the pages cited. Nothing is
labelled from memory.

**chapterLines 0.** "Ingolstadt, 1540. Peter Apian prints the heavens with paper wheels that turn,
to find where each planet stands." — **attested.** Apian produced the *Astronomicum Caesareum* at
Ingolstadt in 1540, a printed, hand-coloured book of woodcuts and twenty-one volvelles (moveable
paper discs) for calculating the positions of the planets, Sun and Moon on the Ptolemaic model.
- Fitzwilliam Museum, Cambridge, *Extraordinary Universe of Peter Apian* (exhibition, 2024),
  https://fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/plan-your-visit/exhibitions/extraordinary-universe-of-peter-apian
- "The extraordinary universe of Peter Apian: technical investigation of five copies of a
  16th-century astronomical book", *Heritage Science* (Nature Portfolio), 2024,
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-024-01306-1
- New York Public Library, *Peter Apian's Astronomicum Caesareum*,
  https://www.nypl.org/events/exhibitions/galleries/explorations/item/5392

**chapterLines 2.** "1582. Ten days are struck from October, to bring the calendar back into step
with the Sun." — **attested.** Under Gregory XIII's reform Thursday 4 October 1582 was followed by
Friday 15 October; the ten days removed returned the vernal equinox, which the Julian year had let
drift to about 11 March, to 21 March, where it stood at Nicaea.
- Encyclopedia Virginia (Virginia Humanities), "The Gregorian Calendar",
  https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/the-gregorian-calendar/

**chartNotes 0.** "Acus, the Needle, is this atlas's own figure. Chart-makers cut many such; the
sky was fixed at eighty-eight in 1922." — **attested.** At its first General Assembly, Rome, May
1922, the IAU agreed a list of 88 constellations with three-letter abbreviations; Delporte's
boundaries followed in 1928 (published 1930). Many figures cut by earlier chart-makers were dropped
from that list.
- IAU, "The Constellations", https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/
- Ian Ridpath, *Star Tales*, ch. 1, http://www.ianridpath.com/startales/startales1d.html
- Constellation Guide, "Former Constellations",
  https://www.constellation-guide.com/constellations/former-constellations/

**chartNotes 1.** "The Sail was still part of Argo, the ship, in 1603. Lacaille cut the ship into
keel, stern and sails in the 1750s." — **attested.** Bayer's *Uranometria* (Ulm, 1603) drew Argo
whole and lettered its stars alpha to omega as one figure. Lacaille observed at the Cape from
August 1751 to July 1752, presented his southern map in 1754, and the engraved planisphere and
preliminary catalogue, with Argo divided into poop, hull and sails, appeared in the Academy's
*Mémoires* in 1756; the Latin names Carina, Puppis and Vela were fixed in the posthumous *Coelum
australe stelliferum* of 1763. "The 1750s" covers observation, chart and first publication; 1763 is
only the Latinised final form, so the line stands.
- University of Oklahoma Libraries, History of Science Collections, "A Planisphere containing the
  Celestial Constellations" (Lacaille 1756), https://galileo.ou.edu/exhibits/planisphere-containing-celestial-constellations
- Ian Ridpath, *Star Tales*, "Vela", http://www.ianridpath.com/startales/vela.html
- *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, "Coelum Australe Stelliferum",
  https://www.britannica.com/topic/Coelum-Australe-Stelliferum
- Linda Hall Library, Scientist of the Day, "Johann Bayer",
  https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/johann-bayer/

**chartNotes 3.** "Ptolemy set two crowns among his forty-eight, a northern and a southern; the
northern is Ariadne's." — **attested.** Both Corona Borealis and Corona Australis are among the 48
constellations of the *Almagest*; in classical myth the northern crown is the one Dionysus gave
Ariadne.
- Universe Today, "The Corona Borealis Constellation",
  https://www.universetoday.com/articles/corona-borealis-constellation
- Constellation Guide, "Corona Borealis Constellation",
  https://www.constellation-guide.com/constellation-list/corona-borealis-constellation/

**chartNotes 5.** "Tucana, the toucan, is a bird of the Americas. Its stars never rise over Europe,
so Ptolemy's list has none of them." — **attested.** Tucana spans declination −56° to −75°: it
never rises north of about latitude 30–34°, which excludes all of Europe, and it is one of the
twelve figures Plancius made from Keyser's and de Houtman's southern observations of 1595–97, after
Ptolemy. The toucan is a South and Central American bird.
- *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, "Tucana", https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tucana
- Sea and Sky, "Constellation Tucana", https://www.seasky.org/constellations/constellation-tucana.html
- Linda Hall Library, Scientist of the Day, "Frederick de Houtman",
  https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/frederick-de-houtman/

**chartNotes 6.** "Serpens is the one figure cut in two: its head and its tail lie either side of
Ophiuchus, who holds it." — **attested.** Serpens is the only modern constellation in two
non-contiguous parts, Caput and Cauda, with Ophiuchus the Serpent-bearer between them; Delporte's
1920s boundaries made the split, following Gould's *Uranometria Argentina* (1879).
- EarthSky, "Serpens the Snake, the only constellation cut in 2",
  https://earthsky.org/constellations/serpens-the-snake-2-pieces-serpens-cauda-serpens-caput/
- Ian Ridpath, *Star Tales*, "Serpens", http://www.ianridpath.com/startales/serpens.html

**chartNotes 7.** "Argo, Jason's ship, was the largest of Ptolemy's figures. It was later broken
up, and no longer counts as one." — **attested.** Argo Navis was some 28% larger than Hydra, the
largest constellation today (and the second of Ptolemy's), and was divided by Lacaille (above); it
is not among the IAU's 88.
- Constellation Guide, "Argo Navis",
  https://www.constellation-guide.com/constellations/former-constellations/argo-navis-constellation/
- Obliquity.com, SkyEye, "Argo Navis", https://www.obliquity.com/skyeye/88const/Arg.html

**chartNotes 8.** "Pavo, the peacock, came home with the same Dutch voyage. Its brightest star is
called, simply, the Peacock." — **attested.** Pavo is another of the twelve from Keyser's
observations on the first Dutch voyage to the East Indies (sailed 1595, home August 1597), first on
the Plancius–Hondius globe of 1598. Alpha Pavonis (mag. 1.94) was named "Peacock" by HM Nautical
Almanac Office in the late 1930s for the RAF's Air Almanac.
- Linda Hall Library, "Frederick de Houtman" (as above)
- Sky at Night Magazine (BBC), "History of the Southern Hemisphere Constellations",
  https://www.skyatnightmagazine.com/space-science/southern-hemisphere-constellations-history
- Ian Ridpath, *Star Tales*, "Pavo", http://www.ianridpath.com/startales/pavo.html

**chartNotes 10.** "Laterna, the Lantern, is this atlas's own. Tycho fixed a thousand stars without
a telescope, to about a minute of arc." — **overstated; corrected.** The "thousand" stands: Tycho
completed a manuscript catalogue of 1004 stars in 1598 (Kepler printed it in the *Rudolphine
Tables*, 1627); the 1602 *Progymnasmata* printed a shorter 777. (The doubt as first posed had these
reversed.) But Verbunt & van Gent, comparing with Hipparcos, find standard errors of about 2′ in
both longitude and latitude, with some 15% of entries off by more than 10′ through copying or
computing slips. "About a minute" flatters him. Corrected text: "Laterna, the Lantern, is this
atlas's own. Tycho fixed a thousand stars without a telescope, to a minute or two of arc." —
**attested** as corrected.
- F. Verbunt & R. H. van Gent, "Three editions of the star catalogue of Tycho Brahe:
  machine-readable versions and comparison with the modern Hipparcos Catalogue", *Astronomy &
  Astrophysics* 516, A28 (2010), https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/full_html/2010/08/aa14002-10/aa14002-10.html
- "Astrometric accuracy during the past 2000 years", arXiv:1707.01020 (2017),
  https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.01020

**chartNotes 11.** "Phalæna, the Moth, is this atlas's own. Bayer printed a bee in the south; it
survives as Musca, the one insect left in the sky." — **attested.** The figure first appears in
Bayer's *Uranometria* (1603) as Apis, the bee; Lacaille renamed it Musca (Australis) in 1752 to
avoid confusion with Apus. It is the only insect among the 88. The northern fly, Musca Borealis,
was dropped, which is what "left" honestly records.
- Constellation Guide, "Musca Constellation",
  https://www.constellation-guide.com/constellation-list/musca-constellation/
- Universe Today, "Musca", https://www.universetoday.com/articles/musca
- Ian Ridpath, *Star Tales*, "Musca", http://www.ianridpath.com/startales/musca.html
