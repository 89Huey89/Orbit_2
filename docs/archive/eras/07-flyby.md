# VII · The Flyby

**The Solar System, 1965–present, opening on Mariner 4's first close-up of Mars, 14–15 July 1965, and centred on Voyager 2's Grand Tour, Jupiter in 1979 to Neptune on 25 August 1989.** This is the first sheet on the whole ladder where the point of view is not standing on the Earth looking up: it has left, and where the sky used to be a picture there is now weather, geology, chemistry — a physical place a machine can actually be sent to and actually touch. Every era before this one built a better representation of the same distant light; this one arrives, and the light turns out to have had rock, ice and storm in it the whole time. It is also this ladder's own answer to an old gap: the years 1965 to 1989, in which this game's seven planetary families were each first discovered for real rather than merely rendered, belonged to no single era on an earlier plan and belong wholly to this one now — every family below carries the date of the mission that actually found it, not a resemblance assigned after the fact.

## The documents

- **Mariner 4's first Mars flyby image, 14–15 July 1965.** A 200×200-pixel raster, each pixel a 6-bit value, radioed home at 8⅓ bits per second — one frame took roughly ten hours, the full 21-frame set about ten days. The image everyone actually remembers is not the developed photograph but the hand-tinted strip chart: JPL engineers, unwilling to wait for the imaging computer, pinned the teletype's own numeric printout to a wall and coloured the digits by hand in pastels bought that morning from a nearby art shop, then framed the result for director William Pickering — a paint-by-numbers grid keyed to a data table, not a photograph. Attested, [research/space-age.md](research/space-age.md) §1.
- **Viking 1 and 2 landers, Mars, 20 July and 3 September 1976.** Facsimile cameras scanned the scene line by line in azimuth with a rotating photodiode — a flatbed-scanner mechanism, not a broadcast tube — each frame carrying a reference test chart and corrected against reseau marks, a grid of crosses etched into the optical path itself so a known pattern travels inside the image rather than around it. Attested.
- **Voyager 1 and 2, launched 1977.** Jupiter in 1979, Saturn in 1980–81, Voyager 2 alone reaching Uranus on 24 January 1986 and Neptune on 25 August 1989 — the only craft ever to visit either, and Voyager's last planetary encounter. Every target was imaged as a framed mosaic, a grid of separately radioed vidicon tiles reassembled on the ground; the seamed look of every Voyager Jupiter or Saturn photograph, tile boundaries included, is that assembly, not a flaw in reproduction. Attested.
- **Io's volcanism, discovered 9 March 1979.** Linda Morabito, a JPL navigation engineer processing a Voyager 1 frame for ordinary spacecraft-position work, noticed a 270 km crescent cloud off Io's limb — the first active volcanism ever seen off Earth, found by accident in an engineering frame nobody had aimed at a discovery. Attested, Morabito's own 2012 historical review.
- **The rings of Uranus, 10 March 1977, nine years before a spacecraft ever arrived.** James Elliot and colleagues, tracking a stellar occultation from an airborne telescope, watched a star wink five times before Uranus itself occulted it and five times after — a light curve, not a picture, proving narrow rings existed years before Voyager 2 imaged them directly in 1986. Attested.
- **Neptune's Great Dark Spot, Voyager 2, August 1989.** An Earth-sized storm near 27°S, roughly 13,000×6,600 km, winds over 2,100 km/h — gone by the time Hubble looked again in the mid-1990s, unlike Jupiter's much older Great Red Spot. Attested.
- **Huygens on Titan, 14 January 2005.** The only lander ever to touch an outer-Solar-System body, photographing continuously through descent before returning roughly 350 images of icy "cobblestones" on a frigid floodplain. Attested.
- **New Horizons at Pluto, 14 July 2015.** Tombaugh Regio's western lobe, Sputnik Planitia, built from four monochrome LORRI frames and lower-resolution Ralph colour data, then colour-enhanced — stated plainly by NASA as not natural colour — specifically to make compositional boundaries legible. Attested.
- **Asteroid and comet shape models — NEAR Shoemaker/Eros, Hayabusa/Itokawa, OSIRIS-REx/Bennu, Rosetta/67P.** An irregular body cannot be a lit sphere with a terminator; a mission instead fits a polyhedral mesh to overlapping imagery or radar return, a wireframe assembling as more of the body is seen — the correct visual language for "becoming known" that a rendered sphere could never honestly offer. Attested.

## The grammar

Every earlier sheet on this ladder is a picture with instruments drawn around its edge — a scale bar, a compass, a filter label framing a body already finished. This one inverts that relationship for as long as an encounter actually lasts, because a real mission's document is never delivered whole: it is a raster of six-bit numbers before it is an image, a grid of separately radioed tiles before it reads as one planet, a wireframe mesh before it is a solid. The incompleteness a controller on the ground actually sat through, tile by tile and hour by hour, is this era's real subject, and [KNOWLEDGE-HORIZON.md](KNOWLEDGE-HORIZON.md)'s own reveal grammar — a first mark, a recognisable stage, a completion — is not a scheme imposed on this material from outside. It is a description of what these missions already lived through.

Two scan structures, not one, and they should not be drawn identically: a vidicon-era frame (Mariner, Voyager, a Viking orbiter) builds from horizontal scan lines with a visible pitch, the way a broadcast raster does; a Viking lander frame builds the same idea rotated, line by line in azimuth, a scanner's carriage pass rather than a tube's sweep. A reseau cross or a reference chart makes the same claim this ladder's later, Earth-bound register makes with a scale bar — *this can be checked* — but it makes that claim from inside the frame rather than around it, a known pattern etched into the optical path so distortion can be undone by comparing a measured position against the position that was always known. False colour appears only where it is earned and only once, never as unexplained wash: a real filtered exposure recording light outside what an eye sees, or separating a real difference too subtle to read unstretched, and a caption should always say which. Mission-control type runs Futura through the Apollo years, then Helvetica from NASA's own 1976 graphics-standards redesign onward — both attested, and either is a safe HUD anchor.

**Black space, and what it is made of.** Every earlier era on this ladder answers "what is the sheet made of" by naming a ground — limestone, plaster, hemp paper, brass, glass — and this one cannot, because for the first time there is no sheet at all, only vacuum. Read literally, "black space" is the single instruction on this whole plan most likely to produce the one failure the plan exists to prevent: a black rectangle, a plate with no substance in it. The fix is not to soften the black. It is to treat the black exactly as [01-rock.md](01-rock.md)'s own unlit rock is already treated — not as an absence, but as the real material it is, because vacuum, distance and radio silence are themselves attested textures the moment an instrument is running against them. Four things fill what would otherwise be bare, all of them grounded rather than invented ([research/space-age.md](research/space-age.md) §9): a field of parallaxing stars, exactly what a real optical-navigation frame already contains — the kind of frame Morabito was processing when she found Io's plume — reading as depth against the player's own motion at no added cost, since the scroll already computes it; a faint, ever-present margin of DSN signal-strength texture, carrier lock and signal-to-noise and a Doppler trace, so the ground is never only black, it is always also being listened to; the rare bright pixel or short streak of a cosmic-ray sensor hit, a genuine artefact of every deep-space camera ever flown, standing in for this era's own grain (**unverified** to any one named frame, but a standard, widely documented artefact of the imaging technology itself); and, over an irregular body, the shape-model wireframe, a faceted mesh assembling out of nothing, which is substance appearing out of black by a real technique rather than an invented effect. None of this argues with BLACK SPACE — the ground stays the starkest on the whole ladder, no paper grain, no plate emulsion, no engraved rule anywhere on it — it argues only that the black is not empty. Its name is vacuum, distance and measurement, and all three are being drawn.

This is also the seam where the ladder's own method visibly changes, and it is worth stating plainly what does and does not happen there. [PROGRESSION.md](PROGRESSION.md)'s ten-step transition is the same mechanism run once at every seam on the ladder, not a special case built for this pair: [The Lens](06-lens.md)'s own fog, silver mirroring and emulsion frilling ([THE-FRONTIER.md](THE-FRONTIER.md)) consume everything on that sheet but the transition object and the Observer Core, exactly as every era's decay does at its own close. What makes this particular seam read as dramatically as the brief wants is not a new mechanic — it is that this is the first time on the ladder that a decay uncovers nothing. Every earlier transition left a ground standing behind it: rock under ochre, plaster under paint, glass under silver. This is the first one where, once the old sheet has finished failing, there is no sheet underneath at all — only the transition object, the Core, and the vacuum this file has just spent naming. The historical medium is not stripped away by an effect built for the occasion. It simply has nowhere left to stand, for the first time in the game's history, once its own era's decay has finished running.

## Lettering and the hand

This era's script is the telemetry stream itself, the way this ladder's most instrument-heavy hands already treat a data frame as a script in its own right. A modern deep-space signal packages a small header and payload inside a transfer frame — the current CCSDS standard specifies a fixed frame header, virtual-channel and frame counters, a payload, and an optional error-control trailer — and this era borrows exactly that shape as its own visual grammar for reading a body's data before the data ever resolves into a picture: a fixed header, counters, a payload, a checksum. What must be said honestly rather than smoothed over is that CCSDS itself was only codified in 1982, seventeen years after Mariner 4 and six after Viking, so nothing here claims Mariner ever spoke literal CCSDS — only that the *concept* the standard later formalised is this era's own honest grammar throughout, not a period fact about its earliest mission. Numerals tick whole-digit by whole-digit rather than being written with any stroke at all, a convention this era arguably originates rather than merely shares with [The Probe](08-probe.md)'s own instrument readout, since a real telemetry counter has never in its history advanced any other way. Labels set in Futura through the Apollo years, then Helvetica from NASA's own 1976 graphics-standards redesign onward — both attested, either a safe HUD anchor; anything framed as a literal downlink figure sets in a monospace face, IBM Plex Mono or JetBrains Mono, the same family this ladder's other most technical sheet already reaches for. No shaping problem exists anywhere in this era's script: Latin letterforms and Arabic numerals only, the same easy case every instrument-lettered era on this ladder already finds.

## Currency and the rule

**The currency is propellant, read in Δv** — per [ECONOMY.md](ECONOMY.md), the strictest ink on the whole ladder: loaded once, on the ground, and never resupplied, so what remains at any moment is the entire margin for every manoeuvre still to come ([research/space-age.md](research/space-age.md) §6). No rule attaches beyond the one every era already answers to; this row differs from the shipped baseline in nothing but its name, exactly as [ECONOMY.md](ECONOMY.md)'s own table entry for this era states. What the currency's honesty does earn is a reason to reward this era's slingshot pickup harder than any other on the ladder: a gravity assist is not a stylised bonus standing in for good play, it is a real trajectory designer's actual job, the reason Voyager ever reached Neptune at all on the propellant it launched with. A twist was considered and set aside on purpose — the Deep Space Network's real scarce resource is contact scheduling, not propellant, and a flat acquisition tax per transfer would land close enough to the Lens's own exposure-and-attention economy to read as the same twist worn twice in a row. This era stays the plain no-twist reading instead, and the honesty of "you will never get more than you launched with" is left to speak for itself.

## The bodies

Before a craft is ever committed to it, a body on this sheet is a bright point wearing a small halo, its size and the colour of its rim carrying exactly what tracking data alone can give a mission before arrival — how big a target it offers, and whether the navigation team's own plot already marks it as a hazard. That point, the ring a capture actually needs, and anything the run depends on to fly a transfer to it are never staged and never withheld: a run is lost on this sheet to a bad transfer exactly as on every other, never to a light the mission chose not to resolve. What the phenomenon withholds is identity alone, and on this sheet that withholding carries the whole ladder's own argument at its sharpest: for six eras a light really was withholding nothing but a face, and now, for the first time, it is withholding a place.

The instrument the game renders around the moving point is not a human hand holding anything — it is the spacecraft itself, a small hull with a sensor face leading and a dish trailing or set off to one side, subordinate in the silhouette to the instrument actually doing the looking. The **Observer Core sits at the sensor's own boresight**, not the antenna's, for the reason [OBSERVER-CORE.md](OBSERVER-CORE.md) gives every tool on this ladder: the point that wants to know is the point doing the looking, not the point talking to Earth, and a real probe carries both as separate hardware pointed in different directions. This silhouette — instrument-forward, dish trailing or subordinate — is stated honestly as **unverified**, a generic deep-space configuration rather than a transcription of one named mission's own layout. It is also the first tool on the whole ladder that is not, in any sense, held. The crayon, the brush, the sighting tube, the alidade, the quill, the telescope all sat in a hand. This one is the hand's own extension, sent where the hand cannot go — the entire content of the brief's break, not a better instrument for looking, but the first instrument built to leave.

What that sensor produces is struck in the order an actual mission control room actually lived through, not a stylised unveiling. At capture, the first scan lines fill from the top of the frame down, exactly as Mariner 4's own ten-hour transmission did — a number grid becoming, one row at a time, the ghost of a shape, indistinguishable at this stage from noise to anyone not told what to look for. By a quarter-turn a mosaic's tiles have locked into place around it, seams still visible between them the way every Voyager Jupiter or Saturn frame genuinely shows its own tile boundaries, and a stated false-colour pass — never an unexplained rainbow, always a named reason: Io's sulphur, a comet's jets — resolves a real boundary an eye alone could never have separated. By half a turn the mosaic reads substantially documented: all but a rim of tiles locked, or, where the body is too irregular for a disc to mean anything at all, the shape-model mesh closed over most of its faces and left open-wireframe only on the side not yet imaged — a controller's own honest mid-pass state, not yet the finished frame. By two-thirds of a turn the seams close for good: a spherical body's mosaic resolves to one clean disc, or that same mesh closes whole, facet by facet, against the black behind it — the correct image for a body "becoming known" that a lit sphere could never honestly offer. Only now, at completion, does the instrument margin arrive: a scale bar, a filter label, a credit line, the same furniture [The Lens](06-lens.md)'s own rendered register already earns for a finished frame, held back here until the body has actually crossed from a sensed target to a place with coordinates, because that crossing is the whole content of this era's caption.

What results is a **mapped world**: sensed becomes place, gaining the coordinates and the instrument furniture that phrase implies, and gaining them only on arrival, never before it. The Moon carries the one entry on this whole ladder that is not a drawing of anything at all — Apollo's own photographed, landed-on surface, the first body in the entire game a human being has actually stood on, which this sheet marks rather than merely renders. A caption, where one is set at all, is a mission label dated exactly as the mission itself was — `1965 · MARINER 4` beside the first cratered Mars — the format this era's telemetry hand keeps for every family it earns. And this is the one era on the ladder whose entry in the wrong-belief column is not a mistake of its own making but a correction of somebody else's: Mariner 4's cratered Mars is what actually overturns [The Lens](06-lens.md)'s own canal-ruled Mars, the same body that plate era spent fifteen years believing carried a straight-lined irrigation network across its face. This sheet does not add a fresh error to the record. It is the record correcting the one before it, in the plainest way any era on this ladder ever manages — by arriving, and looking.

Four of the seven families are literally born in this era rather than merely rendered by it, each carrying its own founding date rather than a resemblance assigned afterward: **crater**, Mariner 4, 1965, the discovery that a body once imagined smooth or canal-veined was in fact closer to the Moon than to anyone's earlier guess; **ringed**, sensed by occultation in 1977 and only imaged in 1986, the moment "ringed" stops meaning Saturn alone; **volcanic**, Io, 1979, found by accident in a navigation engineer's own frame rather than at any scripted moment, staged in this era's own chapter plates the same way it was actually discovered — an anomaly appearing mid-orbit, never a beat announced in advance; **storm**, Neptune's Great Dark Spot, 1989, transient and gone within a decade, kept visually distinct from Jupiter's much older, much steadier Great Red Spot the Lens has already drawn. **Ice** belongs to Uranus and Neptune both, Voyager's own closing act in 1986 and 1989. **Ocean** belongs properly to Galileo's 1995–2003 evidence for a subsurface sea, not to Voyager's 1979 imagery of Europa's fractured ice alone — the two claims are kept separate rather than folded into one date, because the fracture and the ocean argument are a decade and a half apart in the actual record. **Dune** is this era's honestly weak family: its evidence is Mars orbital imagery from Mariner 9 and Viking's own orbiters, not a flyby proper, and it is flagged here rather than forced into a founding moment it does not really have.

## The frontier

This era's word for the boundary is **LOS** — Loss of Signal, the Deep Space Network's own bracket term, kept in the all-capital mnemonic style this era's telemetry hand already sets everything else in, because it names what is actually happening rather than reaching for a metaphor to stand in for it. Its rate, its grace and its shoreline are untouched by any of this, per [THE-FRONTIER.md](THE-FRONTIER.md); what changes, as on every sheet, is only the material failing behind the player.

A real telemetry stream loses lock the way any digital link does: a bad sync word, a failed checksum, a missing block — never grain, never static as decoration, a literal dropped frame. Drawn render-side that is a scan line replaced by a flat dropout band, a mosaic tile that never resolves and sits as a grey placeholder among tiles that did, a frame counter that skips rather than climbing — the honest structure a real Mariner or Voyager frame actually showed when a bit was lost, never television snow. It worsens with depth rather than switching on at one row, and for a physical reason rather than a dramatic one: received power falls with the square of distance while background noise holds roughly steady, so bit-error rate genuinely climbs the farther a craft gets from Earth — which is why LOS should read as a field that thickens with the row, not a curtain that drops at a line. Attested, [research/space-age.md](research/space-age.md) §4, and standard deep-space link-budget physics beside it.

The star behind a lost tile was there before any receiver ever locked onto it and stays there after the link drops; what LOS erases is only ever this mission's own account of having looked, which is why a dropped frame, once gone, is never re-requested — a real ground station does not get its missing scan line back either.

## The mission map

The background artefact this era fills is **the mission map completing, frame by frame** — not a chart in any older sense, closer to the tracking display a real encounter's own ground team actually watched fill in during a single spacecraft's pass. Empty, it is a bare telemetry margin over vacuum black: a frame counter reading zero, a signal-strength trace flat at nothing, no tile logged anywhere on it. Half filled, it reads the way an actual encounter sequence reads mid-pass: some targets fully mosaicked and captioned, others still a locked tile or two short of a disc, a shape model closed only on the side that has actually been imaged and left open-wireframe on the far side — an honest, uneven state of a real mission's own logbook, never a bar dressed as one. Complete, once this era's own ledger has armed, the map reads the way a finished encounter's photojournal does: every target it met resolved to a disc or a closed mesh, captioned and dated, the DSN's own margin steady at full lock — not a picture of the Solar System, a record of everywhere one craft actually went and what it found there.

Once armed, per [PROGRESSION.md](PROGRESSION.md)'s own ledger and designation, the next main body already dealt in the chart is designated the transition object and drawn exactly as any other body on this sheet — scanned the same way, its mosaic locked in the same stages, encountered the same way. What follows at that capture is LOS run to completion rather than to a shoreline: every tile this era ever locked, every mesh it ever closed, every mission label it ever set, drops out of sync all at once, the whole telemetry margin going to flat, unrecoverable dropout across the frame, while the transition object and the Observer Core at the sensor's own boresight are the two things that failure never touches. What is uncovered underneath, for the first time on the entire ladder, is nothing at all — no rock, no plaster, no paper, no glass, only the vacuum this era was already drawing as its own ground, so the world does not so much decay into a new medium as decay into the one it was already sitting in.

What crosses into that vacuum, cross-dissolving over the same second or two the world's forward advance is already easing for, is not another instrument at all. It is a **replication core**, per [OBSERVER-CORE.md](OBSERVER-CORE.md)'s own reading of the brief's own phrase — "at the heart of the final von Neumann probe" — taken literally rather than as a figure of speech. Every transition before this one replaced one held tool with another: a hand still closed around something different than it was a moment before. This is the one transition on the whole ladder where that stops being true. The Observer Core survives it exactly as it survives every other seam — unconditionally, by its own no-state rule, never specially protected because it was never conditional on era to begin with — but for the first time what now surrounds it is not a tool history put into anyone's hand, because there is no longer a hand in the loop to put it into. The mission that has been flying this sheet since 1965 is a human one at both ends of every signal it has ever sent; what launches from the far side of this transition is not. This is the last transition the ladder has, and it is the one where, in exactly the sense the brief means the phrase, the observer finally leaves — not abandoning the point that wants to know, which the Core carries forward precisely as it always has, but handing the tool built around that point to something that no longer needs a person standing at the other end of the link to keep watching.

This is not a lesser rehearsal for whatever finally learns to explore on its own, and it does not improve on six eras of hands that drew, sighted, measured and lettered the same sky from the ground. What this tradition did that no other era on this ladder did is *go*: send a machine, built by hands that would never live to see where it arrived, across a gap no eye and no instrument fixed to the Earth could ever close on its own — and have it arrive, and look, and be right, correcting a plate's own fifteen years of canals with nothing more than an honest ten-hour scan. The sky the Lens spent three centuries resolving from the ground is the same sky this era's own hulls actually touched; only the distance between the observer and the thing observed, which every earlier era on this ladder took for granted as fixed, is what this one finally closed.

## Frame and furniture

Almost nothing survives from any earlier sheet's frame, and the emptiness is deliberate: no cartouche, no double rule, no laid-paper sheet, no compass rose. In their place, a thin telemetry margin closer to a terminal window than a chart border — a frame counter, a signal-strength trace, a range figure, all ticking whole-digit by whole-digit rather than written; a small DSN glyph naming which complex currently holds contact, a bracket or a dish icon, never a map. Only once a body is fully mapped does an instrument-margin discipline arrive at all — a scale bar, a filter label, a credit line, inherited wholesale from [The Lens](06-lens.md)'s own rendered register and [research/observatory.md](research/observatory.md)'s documented practice — introduced late rather than present throughout, so its sudden arrival is itself the signal that a sensed target has just become a place.

## The signature sheet

**Chosen: Mariner 4's hand-tinted strip chart** — the teletype printout itself, pastel-coloured by hand and pinned to a wall before anyone had waited for the real photograph — the single image that most honestly answers this era's own hardest question, because it is not a photograph of Mars at all. It is a data table somebody decided to make legible with a box of pastels bought that morning. Later enrichment: a Voyager-style mosaic assembling over Uranus or Neptune, seams visible until the last tile locks and a ring system resolves from an occultation light-curve annotation into a drawn ring; Io's plume developing as an anomaly at the frame's own limb rather than at any announced beat; a shape model closing facet by facet over an irregular body, against pure black, with no sphere anywhere in the image.

## Sound

A short rising two-tone chirp for signal acquisition, distinct from any earlier era's confirmation cue — carrier lock, not a guide star found. A soft, regular tick advancing under a raster's own top-down fill. Static rising, then cutting to plain silence for loss of signal — an absence, not a klaxon, because a real LOS is exactly that. A short, dry hiss-and-cutoff for a trajectory correction burn, a discrete pulse rather than sustained motion. Underneath everything, a very low, faint carrier tone that never stops — the Deep Space Network's own continuous listening, playing this era's version of the water-drip ambient bed the Rock keeps at the opposite end of the ladder.

## The prototype

No prototype exists yet for this era. [OVERVIEW.md](OVERVIEW.md)'s own build order places the Flyby beside [The Engraving](05-engraving.md) as the fifth era to build and the first with no prior sheet of any kind to inherit from — every other unbuilt era on the ladder has either a shipped renderer to generalise or a near-finished one to assemble, and this one has neither. ## Risk

The CCSDS frame is a concept borrowed across a seventeen-year anachronism and must never be presented as something Mariner 4 literally spoke — stated above, worth repeating here as the single easiest fact in this file to blur by accident.
