# III · The Scroll

**China, Tang dynasty, c. 649–684 CE, centred on the Dunhuang star chart (British Library
Or.8210/S.3326), its diagrammatic grammar carried forward through the Song into the Yuan Bureau of
Astronomy, closing with Guo Shoujing's instrument-making at Dadu around 1276.** The sky as a
bureaucracy's own record: not a picture traced through stars but a filing system laid over them,
kept for nine centuries by an institution rather than a single hand, and reopened only because a
cave was sealed shut before it could be lost.

## The documents

- **The Dunhuang star chart, British Library Or.8210/S.3326.** Ink on paper — a continuous scroll,
  not silk — roughly 210 × 24 cm, found in 1907 by Aurel Stein in the sealed manuscript cache of
  Cave 17 at the Mogao Caves, Dunhuang, Gansu; now in the British Library. Twelve rectangular
  panels, each a strip of sky in quasi-cylindrical projection along the celestial equator, closed
  by a thirteenth circular panel — a circumpolar map looking straight up at the pole — and a drawn
  bowman in Tang dress whose identity this file's research could not establish **(unverified)**.
  The 2009 redating to Emperor Gaozong's reign (Bonnet-Bidaud, Praderie & Whitfield) rests on a
  taboo-character analysis, a textual method, not a material one; treat 649–684 as the current
  scholarly position, not a settled certainty.
- **The Suzhou planisphere stele, 1247 CE.** Engraved in stone by Wang Zhiyuan after an earlier
  drawing by the scholar-official Huang Shang, c. 1190–1193; survives in the Suzhou Museum of
  Inscribed Steles, read today mainly from ink rubbings. Its own carved title, **天文圖 (*Tiānwén
  tú*, "Chart of the Heavens")**, is a genuine period title, and this file's source for the game's
  own title (Names, below).
- **Su Song's *Xin Yi Xiang Fa Yao* (新儀象法要), 1092 CE.** Over sixty illustrations of a
  water-driven clock tower at Kaifeng, roughly twelve metres tall in three tiers — an armillary
  sphere tracking the sky, a celestial globe turning to match it, mechanical jacks striking bells
  on schedule below. Dismantled and carried off in 1126 during the Jurchen conquest of Kaifeng; the
  treatise is what survived.
- **The *Song Shi*'s record of the guest star of 1054.** A "guest star" (客星, *kè xīng*) recorded
  near the position later matched to Taurus, visible in daylight for roughly three weeks. A body of
  Chinese and Japanese guest-star records assembled by J. J. L. Duyvendak in 1942 led to its
  identification with the Crab Nebula — a Bureau entry doing real astrophysical work nine centuries
  later, and the clearest evidence this era's record-keeping has ever produced of its own value.
- **The imperial Bureau of Astronomy** — Sitianjian (司天監) under the Tang and Song, Qintianjian
  (欽天監) from the Yuan — continuous observation, calendar production, and the reading of
  celestial events as portents of state affairs, across dynasties into the twentieth century. This
  is the institution this era's whole grammar answers to, not a single astronomer's atlas.
- **The Mawangdui silk comet atlas**, a Han-dynasty tomb manuscript sealed at Changsha in 168 BCE —
  earlier and on a different material than the Dunhuang chart, kept here only as the source for
  this era's silk-ground option (Palette, below) and never imported into the Dunhuang chart's own
  paper grammar.

## The grammar

The sky's own division here is structurally unlike a Greek constellation, and the difference is
the whole of this era's argument. Classical Chinese astronomy divides the entire sky into
thirty-one fields: the **Three Enclosures (三垣, *sān yuán*)** — Ziwei, the Purple Forbidden
Enclosure, circumpolar and always visible; Taiwei, the Supreme Palace Enclosure; Tianshi, the
Heavenly Market Enclosure — plus the **Twenty-Eight Mansions (二十八宿, *èrshíbā xiù*)**,
unequal-width arcs along the celestial equator in four sets of seven under the Four Symbols (the
Azure Dragon, Vermilion Bird, White Tiger and Black Tortoise). The enclosures are named for parts
of an imperial palace — a forbidden precinct, a supreme hall, a marketplace — and hold asterisms
the way a floor plan holds rooms, a bureaucratic order laid over the stars rather than a myth drawn
among them. The mansions are not equal thirty-degree signs either; their job is to give the Moon,
Sun and five planets a fixed set of markers to measure *against*, a coordinate ruler across the
sky, not a picture of it.

A Greek constellation is a picture: a mythological figure traced through its stars, one of a small
fixed set that between them tile the whole sky. A Chinese asterism (星官, *xīngguān*, literally
"star office") is usually not a picture at all — a small named cluster of a few to a few dozen
stars, named for a mundane or bureaucratic object, a well, a chariot, a granary, a rank of
officials — where what matters is position relative to neighbours, never a silhouette. There are
some 283 of these in the fuller catalogues against Ptolemy's 48: a finer grid of relationships, not
a smaller set of larger pictures. A chart is built accordingly — a star placed against its
neighbours first, never silhouette-first — then a thin joining line drawn between one asterism's
members, then the group closed inside its mansion or enclosure's own boundary line. Dot, then
line, then boundary is real chart grammar; the tri-school colour convention below rides the same
grammar rather than decorating it.

**The three colours are provenance, not decoration.** Stars on the Dunhuang chart are small filled
discs in black, red, and pale white or yellow, corresponding to three pre-Han schools of
astronomer — Gan De, Shi Shen, and the older, semi-legendary Wu Xian — harmonised onto one chart,
one colour per school, credited to the third-century astronomer Chen Zhuo **(attribution
unverified this pass — widely repeated, not independently re-confirmed against a named source)**.
A star's colour is a citation: whose observation, centuries apart, is the one this chart is
standing on.

## Lettering and the hand

Formal captions set in **kaishu (楷書)**, the standard book-hand essentially settled by the Tang.
The one structural fact that matters most for how this renders: Chinese is written in logographic,
monospaced units — every character occupies one notional square of the same size (方塊字,
"square-block characters") regardless of stroke count — so there is no shaping engine to build, no
joining, no contextual forms, nothing equivalent to the Astrolabe's Arabic ligatures. A caption is
a row of equal boxes, and `scripts/glyphs.mjs`'s existing per-character extraction is already the
right shape for it — the simplest pipeline on the ladder despite looking the most exotic. Stroke
order (筆順) is real and codified — top to bottom, left to right, horizontal before vertical,
outside before inside — a candidate motion for the joining-line reveal, unconfirmed against any
specific primitive in `src/`.

**The instrument is the brush (毛筆), not the player's own tool** (see The bodies, below): animal
hair bound to a bamboo point, loaded from an inkstone where a solid ink-stick is ground fresh with
water immediately before use. Unlike a fixed-nib nib-angle, the whole expressive range of the brush
is pressure — press for a thick stroke, lift for a thin one. Brush, ink, paper and inkstone
together are the Four Treasures of the Study (文房四寶), the attested source for this era's
currency naming below.

**The CJK subset is the era's real production risk, and it is a sizing problem, not a shaping
one.** CJK Unified Ideographs runs U+4E00–U+9FFF, over 20,000 code points — but as with every other
era, the pipeline never ships the block, only the characters `src/` actually sets: `npm run
glyphs`/`npm run fonts` already scan the served source for its live charset before cutting a face,
and this era's own set — asterism and enclosure names, the three schools, the danger names below,
a handful of HUD words — is a few dozen glyphs, not a font. The trap is choosing the wrong *set*
before that cut ever runs: this era predates the 1950s–60s simplification reform by over a
millennium, so a Tang or Song chart used traditional forms (繁體字), and **Noto Serif SC**, the
obvious Google-Fonts candidate, sets Simplified Chinese — the wrong forms on their face. Noto also
ships **Noto Serif TC** (Traditional) under the same OFL terms, and that is the face this era
actually wants, checked before a single glyph is cut, not after. **Ma Shan Zheng**, a brush-script
display face, is styled after informal handwritten couplets, not a Bureau's scholarly book hand —
right register for a chapter-title flourish, wrong register for a catalogue caption. Both faces are
OFL. Whether captions should run in the historically correct vertical, right-to-left column or
compromise to a horizontal HUD line is not resolved here; see Risk.

## Currency and the rule

**The currency is the *mò* (墨), the ink-stick itself** — per [ECONOMY.md](ECONOMY.md), the
shipped resource renamed and nothing else: ground fresh against the inkstone immediately before
use, wasted if over-ground, dry-brushed if under, a consumed-by-labour resource in the most literal
sense the ladder has. No number changes; the HUD reads *mò* where it reads ink today.

**The one cosmetic depiction, render-side only, no rule attached:** the stroke drawn during a
capture cycles through the three school colours — Gan De's unmarked black, Shi Shen's cinnabar, Wu
Xian's pale white — by encounter count within a chapter, rather than one fixed ink colour for the
whole run. A player who orbits three bodies in a row sees, without being told, that they have drawn
from all three schools at once. Nothing about a hazard field, a capture window or a score number
changes; the frontier this era decays into, and the word it decays under, is
[the boundary's own concern](#the-frontier), not this one's.

## The bodies

Before a hand ever sights it, a body on this sheet is a single unnamed point, joined to nothing —
the exact condition of a newly recorded guest star, whose first *Song Shi* entry names only the
position it appeared at, never an identity of its own. That point's location, the size of the ring
a capture actually needs, and whatever marks it as a dangerous neighbour are never staged and never
withheld; a run is lost on this sheet to a bad transfer exactly as on any other, never to a light
the game declined to draw at full legibility. What the phenomenon withholds is identity alone —
whether this point belongs to anything yet, which is a question only a held sighting can answer.

The tool that answers it is not a brush. [The Ceiling](02-ceiling.md) already gave this ladder its
brush, at the wet tip of a rush fan, and a second scribal era in a row would read as the same
gesture in a different coat of paint rather than a different way of knowing the sky. What this era
actually contributes to the history of astronomy is not penmanship — it is **sighting**: fixing a
star's position against a graduated ring rather than merely marking that it was seen. The tool the
game renders around the moving point is accordingly an **armillary-inspired sighting tube (窺管,
*kuīguǎn*)** — a barrel nested inside one or two thin graduated rings, its forward aperture leading
exactly along the flight's own tangent, no fan, no bristle, nothing that reads as a writing
instrument at all. The tube's own best-documented form belongs to Guo Shoujing's 1276 *jiǎnyí* and
the Song instruments before it, three to six centuries after this era's own 649–684 CE window; its
presence this early is a **plausible reconstruction**, not a transcription of a dated object. The
**Observer Core sits at the tube's forward sighting aperture** — the one point on the whole
instrument actually doing the work of fixing a position — while the brush, the ink and the inkstone
stay where the historical record actually keeps them: in the hand of the Bureau's scribe recording
what the sighting found, a division of observing labour from recording labour this era genuinely
kept separate, collapsed here into one player figure as a **fictional gameplay translation** for
the sake of one tool and one tap.

What that sighting produces is struck in the chart's own attested order, not the brush's. At
capture, one disc is set down positioned against its nearest already-catalogued neighbours — no
line, no colour, no name, indistinguishable on its own from any other uncatalogued point, exactly
as a guest star's first entry is. By a quarter-turn a thin brush-line has grown from that disc
toward its nearest neighbour, the body reading as belonging to something before anything says what
it belongs to. By half a turn the group's enclosure or mansion draws its own boundary line closed
around the whole cluster, and the body takes one of the three school colours — provenance made
visible, per the grammar above, not ornament added late. By two-thirds of a turn the entry closes: a
kaishu caption names the asterism and its school in ink beside the boundary, the first time this
run that this particular colour has been so captioned; any body catalogued afterward under a colour
already captioned this run instead earns the smaller flourish, a seal pressed once in cinnabar
beside its own closed boundary — the well is dry, read off the seal rather than a gauge.

What results is not a portrait. It is a **cataloged member of a named asterism** — a star office
seated in its enclosure or mansion, coloured to the school whose observation it descends from,
which is exactly what "cataloguing" means on this ladder: individual light to catalogued
relationship, and nothing claimed about the light's own face. The Moon is 月, drawn as the one
point every mansion's width was actually set to measure the Sun and the five wanderers against, not
as a body with a surface of its own. No wrong belief is kept here, for the same reason none is kept
on the three eras before the telescope: this tradition claims a position, a name and a place in an
order, never a face, and there is nothing on a face yet to have gotten wrong.

## The frontier

This era's word for the boundary is **hundun (混沌, *hùndùn*)**, the classical term for the
formless chaos before the world had shape — older than this era's own seventh century but live
vocabulary throughout the classical tradition the Dunhuang chart belongs to, an attested concept
carried here as a fictional gameplay translation. Its rate, its grace and its shoreline are
untouched by any of this, per [THE-FRONTIER.md](THE-FRONTIER.md); what changes is only the material
failing behind the player.

Pine-soot ink is bound in glue and brushed onto hemp or mulberry paper, and what actually gives way
is less the ink than the paper carrying it: cellulose degrading by acid hydrolysis as a sheet ages,
embrittling it, while humidity cycling causes cockling, foxing, and eventual disintegration at
every fold and handled edge, the ink lifting and smearing wherever its own binder gives way
alongside it. Drawn render-side as fibres dissolving and the ink bleeding outward into soft diffuse
stains before the paper thins to nothing — softer and slower than the Engraving's own ink-corrosion
failure three eras later.

**The honest complication, and it is worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over:** the actual
Dunhuang chart did not decay this way at all. Cave 17 at Mogao, the "Library Cave," was sealed —
commonly dated to around the early eleventh century — and stayed shut for roughly nine hundred
years until Wang Yuanlu's 1900 discovery and Aurel Stein's 1907 acquisition; a dry, dark, thermally
stable rock-cut chamber gave light-driven fading, humidity cycling and biological attack nothing to
work on, which is the entire reason a millennium-old paper scroll survives to be drawn from at all.
This era's frontier depicts what resumes the moment that seal is broken and the record is exposed
to ordinary time — an overdue reckoning, not an ongoing one, and a fair thing for a player to know
even though nothing about the mechanic's own rate depends on it.

## The star chart

The knowledge structure this era fills is **the star chart becoming complete, asterism by
asterism**, in the diagrammatic order above rather than any modern sense of a progress display.
Empty, it is bare aged paper, the twelve equatorial panels and the circumpolar thirteenth drawn in
outline only, not one asterism yet closed. Half filled, it reads as a working chart genuinely does
mid-compilation: a scatter of joined-but-uncoloured groups beside others already boundary-closed and
captioned, uneven the way any real cataloguing project is uneven, dense where the Bureau's
attention has actually fallen and bare everywhere it has not. Complete, once this era's own ledger
has armed, the sheet reads as the Dunhuang chart itself reads today: hundreds of small coloured
discs, every one boundary-closed inside its mansion or enclosure, three provenances visibly
distinct on one page — not a picture of the sky, a record of who observed it and when.

Once armed, per [PROGRESSION.md](PROGRESSION.md)'s own ledger and designation, the next main body
already dealt in the chart is designated the transition object and drawn exactly as any other body
on this sheet — struck through the same four stages, captured the same way. What follows at that
capture is hundun run to completion rather than to a shoreline: every other entry this era ever
closed, every boundary line, every school colour, every kaishu caption, dissolves by the same paper
failure named above, all at once, while the transition object and the Observer Core at the sighting
tube's aperture are the two things that failure never touches. The tube itself then gives way to
[The Astrolabe](04-astrolabe.md)'s alidade, the Core carried over unchanged at the new rule's
forward pinnule, and the body beneath it is not replaced but redrawn: the same light that was, a
moment before, a cataloged star office seated in its enclosure becomes, in the next era's hand, "an
unadorned point of light, unsighted" once again — not because the Scroll's cataloguing was wrong,
but because knowing a star's place in an ordered system and knowing its measured value are two
different kinds of knowledge, and the second one this ladder has not yet earned.

This is not a lesser rehearsal for the instrument eras that follow it, and it does not improve on
[The Ceiling](02-ceiling.md)'s own naming and ordering of the sky before it. What this tradition did
that no other era on the ladder does is keep the record going: not one atlas by one hand in one
generation, but an institution's unbroken watch across dynasties, thorough enough that a single
day's guest star in 1054 could be matched, nine centuries later, to a nebula still visible today.
The sky Senenmut's ceiling named a hand-span at a time is the same sky this Bureau kept watching
long after any one astronomer who started the record was dead; only the length of the watch, and
what it made possible, is new here.

## Frame and furniture

No European-style cartouche or scale bar. This tradition's furniture is a title set in kaishu,
naming the chart directly — the Suzhou stele's own carved 天文圖 — followed by a closing text panel
recording who compiled or copied it and when, the same functional role a European colophon plays;
and seals (印, *yìn*), pressed in cinnabar paste rather than inked with a brush, marking ownership
or authentication in a second colour against the black-ink field. The HUD replaces RA/declination
hour-ticks with a graduated rim in 度 (*dù*) — 365¼ to the circle, matching the solar year rather
than 360°, a genuinely different-looking scale that is nonetheless attested rather than invented.
A run closes on a colophon-style line in this era's own order: title, then a seal-stamp mark, then
the compiler/place/date line rendered as player/plate/seed.

## The signature sheet

**Primary candidate: the Dunhuang chart's own thirteenth panel, the circumpolar map.** The point
every one of the twelve equatorial strips ultimately resolves toward — all three school colours
converging around the Ziwei enclosure at the pole, the sky's one fixed point standing in for the
emperor's own fixed residence at the centre of a moving court. That double meaning, an
astronomically genuine still point and a deliberately political one, is an unusually good match for
a fixed background element's own role as the one constant on a scrolling, ever-changing field.

**Later enrichment:** the Suzhou stele's full circular chart, rubbed in plain black ink off carved
stone rather than brushed onto paper, a harder and more graphic register worth alternating with
Dunhuang's softer inked-paper look; Su Song's clock tower in cutaway, its three tiers the source for
this era's instrument-tool grammar and its escapement sound (below); and the scroll's own closing
bowman figure, already tied to the slingshot's naming, its specific identity left unclaimed.

## Sound

Ink-stick ground against an inkstone, a soft wet circular grind, the resource being made rather
than spent, the right sound for a refill. A brush's stroke on paper, a supple absorbent hiss softer
than the Ceiling's dry reed-scratch. A seal pressed into cinnabar paste and lifted, a firm brief
press with no scrape — the confirmation for a completed catalogue entry. An armillary sphere's
rings turning, a slow mechanical creak with a periodic escapement tick, drawn from Su Song's tower;
its historical priority as an early escapement is argued, not settled, so the sound stays
suggestive rather than a claimed first. A guest star's arrival, a single struck and sustained tone,
announced once and cleanly — a new fact, not a process.

## The prototype

No prototype exists yet for this era. [OVERVIEW.md](OVERVIEW.md)'s build order places the Scroll
after [The Astrolabe](04-astrolabe.md) and ahead only of [The Rock](01-rock.md)'s own remaining
work; nothing in this file should be read as describing shipped or even spiked behaviour. The
sighting-tube silhouette is the geometry most worth a first pass, for the same reason
[OBSERVER-CORE.md](OBSERVER-CORE.md) flags the Astrolabe's alidade: a straight barrel laid along
the flight's own tangent risks visually fusing with Orbit's existing pricked aim guide, and that
needs checking against a live transfer preview before the era's full art is committed. The CJK
subset pipeline and the dot-then-line-then-boundary reveal timing are the two other candidates for
an early spike.

## Risk

The armillary sighting tube's best-documented form is three to six centuries later than this era's
own window; its use here is stated as a plausible reconstruction and must not be presented as a
dated object. Star and asterism counts for the Dunhuang chart disagree slightly across the sources
this pass could reach (1,339 vs. 1,345 stars; 257 asterisms against 283 for the fuller Chen
Zhuo/Suzhou tradition) and need pinning to the Bonnet-Bidaud/Praderie/Whitfield paper's own tables
before any figure appears in player-facing copy. The closing bowman's identity is unverified beyond
"a bowman in Tang dress." Chen Zhuo's attribution for the tri-school harmonisation rests on general
knowledge, not a source independently checked this pass. The crosswind's naming (風角) is a
practice-class rather than one anchored incident, weaker evidentially than the other three danger
rows, and should be captioned as general practice rather than a dated event. Traditional-versus-
simplified characters is a real, easy-to-miss production risk: Noto Serif TC, not the more obvious
Noto Serif SC, is the face this era wants, and its OFL status was not independently re-confirmed
this pass. Vertical, right-to-left column layout is the historically correct direction for this
script, and whether the game should render captions that way or compromise to a horizontal HUD line
is an open question carried in [KNOWLEDGE-HORIZON.md](KNOWLEDGE-HORIZON.md)'s own "still open" list,
not settled here. A richer, gameplay-affecting idea — letting a body's capture window vary with
which of the twenty-eight mansions it is assigned to, since mansion widths genuinely vary — is
deliberately not adopted in this file; it would make capture geometry era-dependent and needs a
wider architectural conversation before it is treated as safe. Finally, nearly every claim in this
era's research rests on search-result summaries rather than a directly fetched primary source, since
the network tools available to that research pass could not reach the usual hosts; nothing above
should be treated as more certain than that, and anything not independently corroborated should be
re-checked before it is presented as settled.
