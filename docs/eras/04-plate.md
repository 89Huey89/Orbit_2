# IV · The Plate

**1887–1958.** The era the figures were abandoned and the sky was photographed instead. The
cheapest second era to build, and possibly the most beautiful.

## The documents

- **The Carte du Ciel / Astrographic Catalogue** (begun 1887) — the first international attempt
  to photograph the entire sky to a fixed standard, twenty observatories on matched astrographs.
  Its plates carry a **réseau**: a fine grid photographically printed onto the emulsion before
  exposure, so positions could be measured against it. That grid is this era's single most
  distinctive mark.
- **E. E. Barnard, *A Photographic Atlas of Selected Regions of the Milky Way*** (1927, edited
  posthumously by Frost and Calvert). Fifty-two original prints from Barnard's own negatives,
  taken with the 10-inch and 6¼-inch Bruce refractors at Yerkes and Mount Wilson on glass
  negatives up to 12 inches square. Only 700 copies printed. Barnard inspected all 35,700 plates
  himself. The dark nebulae he catalogued still carry his numbers — **B33** is the Horsehead.
- **The Palomar Observatory Sky Survey** (POSS, 1949–58) — the 48-inch Schmidt, the survey that
  every subsequent atlas was checked against.
- **Argelander's *Bonner Durchmusterung*** (1859–62), folded in here: the moment stars stopped
  being parts of figures and became magnitude dots with catalogue numbers.

## The grammar

**Astronomers work in the negative.** A photographic plate is a glass negative, and the prints in
Barnard's atlas are read as such: **dark stars on a pale ground**. This is the one era in the
ladder that inverts the game, and that inversion is its whole identity — it is also, conveniently,
almost exactly what the existing **paper plate** already does, which is why this era is cheap.

There are no figures. There are no mythological forms at all. What replaces them: **catalogue
numbers, fiducial marks, the réseau grid, plate numbers, exposure times, emulsion defects,
scratches and dust, and the observatory's own stamp in the margin.**

## Palette

One channel. A photographic plate is monochrome — silver on glass — so the whole era is a single
ramp from clear emulsion to dense silver, with a faint chemical tone: warm sepia for the older
plates, a cold blue-grey for the mid-century ones. **Reuse the existing duotone machinery
exactly.** This era genuinely is a duotone over the paper plate, which is what makes it a
half-cost era rather than a full one.

## Lettering

A grotesque or an early sans, plus a plate-number hand. Anything from the OFL grotesques will do;
matching the actual annotation on a Carte du Ciel plate means a condensed sans in small caps for
the margins and a typewriter face for the numbers. Some annotation on real plates is **handwritten
in ink directly on the emulsion** — worth reproducing for one or two marks, since the game already
has a pen that writes.

## Names

Catalogue designations, and only those. This is the era's voice and it is a stark, deliberate
contrast to Latin poetry and Arabic epithets:

- **B33**, **B72** — Barnard's dark nebulae
- **BD +30 3639** — a Bonner Durchmusterung number: declination band, then running number
- **NGC 7000**, **M42** — the catalogues the era inherited
- **POSS-I E-1087** — survey, epoch, plate number
- Exposure annotations: `2h 15m`, `Bruce 10-inch`, `Yerkes 1905 Jul 12`

## How the seven families are depicted

**Blur.** A body on a photographic plate is an overexposed disc with a soft halo and no detail at
all — the seven families are distinguished by *size and density*, plus a bracketing annotation
naming what they are. The ringed world shows the ring as an elongation, not as a ring. This is the
era where the game's bodies are least legible as objects and most legible as *measurements*, and
that is the point.

Diffraction spikes belong to reflectors; the Bruce refractors did not produce them. **No spikes on
this plate** — that is era V.

## Chapter plates

1. A Barnard field of the Milky Way in Sagittarius, the star clouds and the dark lanes through them.
2. The Horsehead (B33) against IC 434, as the 1900s plates first caught it.
3. A Carte du Ciel plate with its réseau grid over the whole field and its margin annotations.
4. A plate half-spoiled: a tracking error trailing every star, and the observer's ink note about it.

## Risk

Lowest of any unbuilt era. It is a duotone, a grid, a blur and a set of annotations, all over
machinery that already exists. **Build this second.**
