# II · The Disc

**European Bronze Age, c. 1800–1000 BCE (the disc itself commonly dated c. 1600 BCE).** The sky as a small object you could hold: a hammered bronze disc studded with punched gold, built by a culture that tracked the sky rigorously and wrote nothing down at all.

## The documents

- **The Nebra sky disc**, Landesmuseum für Vorgeschichte, Halle — bronze, 30–32 cm, hammered gold
  foil let into chiselled grooves (damascening), reworked across at least five phases over
  150–200 years. The only object in the ladder that is itself a small painted-metal sky, and the
  document the era's grammar is pulled from whole.
- **The Trundholm sun chariot**, National Museum of Denmark — a bronze horse and six-wheeled
  chariot bearing a disc gilded on one face, spiral-engraved on both, staging the sun's transit as
  a moving event rather than a fixed position.
- **The gold hats** (Schifferstadt, Avanton, Ezelsdorf-Buch, Berlin) — conical sheet gold worked
  entirely in repoussé bands of circles: count by repeated identical mark, the same grammar as
  the disc's punched stars.
- **Nordic Bronze Age razors**, c. 1100–500 BCE — engraved ships, sun-discs and sun-crosses;
  Flemming Kaul's reading of the corpus as one running solar myth grounds the era's ship
  iconography.
- **Rock carvings, Tanum/Bohuslän**, Sweden — thousands of pecked ship and sun-cross images on
  bare rock, the source for what a Bronze Age sun-ship looks like drawn at full scale.

## The grammar

No register bands, no ground line: the disc is a disc, its sky scattered across the whole circular field the way stars actually are — the earliest surviving instance of that grammar in the ladder. No perspective, no size-coded magnitude: every star is the same small punched gold disc regardless of brightness, differentiated only by position. The sun/moon disc and crescent are larger because they are a different kind of object, not because size encodes brightness. Composition was added, not planned: the disc's final state is a real palimpsest, the horizon arcs physically covering two of the original 32 stars, one star moved to stay visible — the chart correcting itself in place rather than being redrawn. The horizon arcs are the one piece of true measurement on the object: their 82° angle matches the solstice sunrise/sunset angle seen from the Mittelberg hill — sky tracked, not merely depicted. Not present: any figure, any register or band layout, any writing to gloss what is shown.

## Lettering and the hand

No script exists in temperate/Nordic Bronze Age Europe — the load-bearing fact of the era, and its honest answer to Rule 2: there was nothing to write, and here is what people did instead. The disc's stars are individually punched or foil-cut gold discs, laid one at a time, identical in kind — a tally, not a notation, unary by repetition, read by counting the marks rather than a sign for the count. Two instruments, and they move differently: **damascening** (the disc's gold) — a groove chiselled along the mark's outline, a pre-cut gold shape laid in, the bronze either side hammered flat over its edge — cut, place, close, three discrete strikes per mark, no continuous line to animate; **repoussé/chasing** (the hats) — worked from the reverse over a resilient backing, pushed into relief, refined by chasing from the front, a rhythmic press-and-lift band by band. Neither is a nib stroke, so the reveal is a mark that **appears**, struck into place with one percussive beat and an audible "settle" as foil seats into its groove, paced by the punch rather than handwriting speed. No typeface is loaded: `Noto Sans Linear B` (OFL 1.1, Google Fonts, mechanically usable) was considered and explicitly declined — Linear B is a Mycenaean palace-economy script, culturally and geographically distant from the Únětice/Nordic world the disc belongs to, and using it would undercut the one fact that makes this era distinct. The only Latin letters permitted are small, quiet modern museum glosses beside the game's own term — a find-name or an asterisked reconstruction, never set as if the Bronze Age had written it.

## Currency and the rule

Currency is **gold foil**, rationed by heft. The disc's own gold differs measurably in composition between its five phases and traces to Cornish alluvial placers, carried by trade networks that produced it in irregular parcels, never a steady flow; **Ösenringbarren** ring ingots, common across Central Europe before weighing scales existed in the region, cluster tightly enough in mass (roughly 70% judged equivalent by hand alone, per the research) to function as genuine proto-money by heft. Rule (class A, render-side assist): **the arcs** — the perfect window is drawn as the disc's own two gold horizon arcs; no `HAZARD_KINDS`, cost or scoring number changes. Deferred: the research's "Pleiades rule" — completing the Pleiades-analogue constellation while carrying a reskinned pickup ("the young moon") inserts a free row that skips hazard escalation, echoing the disc's own leap-month correction — graded by the research itself as sitting closer to option B than A, and held back pending that sign-off.

## The seven families

**Ocean** is a plain, unadorned gold-foil disc left smooth and bright — the disc's own moon, exactly this. **Crater** is the same gold disc pocked with individual punch-marks at irregular intervals, the disc's own star-punching technique turned inward. **Ringed** is the gold-hat treatment at body scale: horizontal repoussé bands of small repeated circles running around the circumference. **Ice** is worked in high-silver electrum rather than warm gold, left almost plain but for one hard white highlight edge. **Dune** carries parallel raked lines across its face, close and slightly uneven, the ship's-oar technique repurposed as texture. **Volcanic** breaks the gold/bronze palette deliberately, raw unalloyed copper-red, radiating cracked lines chiselled outward from an off-centre point. **Storm** is the spiral again, but loose and open — two or three concentric bands rather than one tight inward coil, distinguished from the attractor by looseness and turn-count.

## Frame and furniture

Nothing in this era's documents has a border, cartouche, scale bar or colophon — there is no page to frame. What the disc has is a physical rim, pierced with an estimated 35–40 small holes for mounting, and that rim is the era's one real piece of frame furniture: the plate-mark and RA-hour/declination ticks become the disc's own punched holes, evenly spaced, no numeral needed beside them. The two gold horizon arcs run the frame's margins and double as its scale, since their 82° span is itself the era's one attested measurement. The solar barge sits along the footer. There is no compass rose, no maker's mark, no colophon — inventing one would be furniture the record doesn't have.

## The signature sheet

**The horizon closes** — the disc with both horizon arcs in place and the ship wedged between the stars, two original stars now hidden beneath gold-on-gold, the barge carrying the sun down out of the field: the disc's own darkening, an eclipse-themed plate without inventing an eclipse myth that isn't attested, and the state the prototype itself paints. Later enrichment adds the other three from the research: the disc at rest (Phase I, bare and unweighted), the sun chariot's transit (Trundholm), and the counted hat (a gold hat's full banded height).

## Sound

A single bright punch-tap (capture), tap-then-hush as foil seats fully home (perfect landing), a short dry tool-on-bronze scrape (release), a rising quench-hiss (darkness advancing), a dull flat thud with a low tearing sound — echoing the disc's own attested final act, one arc torn free before burial (loss).

## Risk

This era has no lettering system at all, and Rule 2 has nothing period to hand it beyond grouped tally-marks and modern find-names wearing a Bronze Age costume — spiked in the prototype but untested at scale. The currency's "Pleiades rule" twist is not cosmetic: it changes row progression on a trigger, closer to option B than A, and needs the same sign-off DANGERS.md defers elsewhere. The crosswind is the weakest-attested of the four dangers, built on a disputed reading of the ship's fringing strokes, and should be labelled as such if shipped. The entire research file rests on WebSearch summaries — every direct WebFetch was blocked all session, so nothing here was cross-checked against primary text: the exact rim-hole count (~35–40), the gold hats' unreconciled "154 bands" figure (use the 28/21 zone counts instead), and the 2020 dating-dispute resolution (Bronze Age reaffirmed, but no primary paper read in full) all need a direct check before shipping.
