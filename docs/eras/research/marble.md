# III · The Marble — research

**Greco-Roman, c. 300 BCE – 150 CE, with a Carolingian afterlife to c. 816 CE.** This is the era
`OVERVIEW.md` already flagged and deferred, in the paragraph beginning "Greek/Roman, c. 150 CE
(the Farnese Atlas; Ptolemy's *Almagest*)" — right about the risk, wrong about the conclusion; §12
says why.

## 1. The documents

- **The Antikythera mechanism**, c. 150–100 BCE (dated by the wreck's other cargo; the device may
  be older). Bronze, badly corroded, recovered off Antikythera in 1901; National Archaeological
  Museum, Athens. Not a picture of the sky — a geared calculating machine — but its covers carry
  the densest surviving Greek technical lettering from antiquity: a parapegma (star-phase/weather
  calendar keyed to the zodiac dial) in letters down to 1.2 mm, legible today only via CT and
  raking-light imaging. The source for how tiny, worked bronze lettering should look.
- **The Farnese Atlas** (Naples, MANN, inv. 6374). A 2nd-c. CE Roman marble copy of a lost
  Hellenistic original, 191 cm tall, the globe itself 65 cm across, a kneeling Titan shouldering
  the sky. **This is the document the era's grammar is pulled from.** 41 (some counts: 42) of the
  48 classical constellations are cut in low relief, each crossed by a small set of engraved great
  circles — equator, tropics, colures, ecliptic, arctic/antarctic — the sphere's only coordinate
  system. **No stars at all**, not faded or lost: never given any. A hard constraint, see §2/§12.
- **Schaefer's Hipparchus claim (2005) and the rebuttal.** Bradley Schaefer argued the Farnese
  figures' drawn positions preserve the epoch and content of Hipparchus's lost 2nd-c.-BCE
  catalogue. Dennis Duke and others contested this: the figures disagree substantially with the
  coordinates Hipparchus does give in his one surviving text (below), and a sculptor copying a
  bronze original through at least one Roman recutting is not a reliable transmitter of arcminute
  positions. Treat "the globe encodes Hipparchus" as striking, disputed, and unproven.
- **The Mainz Globe** (Landesmuseum Mainz), one of only three complete ancient celestial globes
  (with Farnese and Kugel). Bronze/silver alloy, Roman, c. 150–220 CE, probably eastern
  Mediterranean. Shows all 48 constellations and the earliest known full Milky Way on a globe; made
  as a sundial gnomon's finial. Exact dimensions and whether it carries inlaid star-points were not
  independently confirmed this session — (unverified) until checked against the museum's own page.
- **The Kugel Globe**, held by Paris dealers Galerie Kugel, the third surviving ancient globe.
  Roman and small; material, precise date and technique not independently confirmed this session —
  mark (unverified) throughout, prefer Farnese and Mainz.
- **Aratus of Soli's *Phaenomena*** (c. 275–270 BCE), versifying an earlier lost prose original by
  Eudoxus of Cnidus — the only reason Eudoxus's constellation content survives at all. Text, not
  image, but the era's most-copied astronomical poem and literary ancestor of the Leiden Aratea.
  **Hipparchus's** only surviving work is a commentary correcting Aratus against Eudoxus — our one
  dated (c. 129 BCE) window onto his own observations, his catalogue itself being lost (a 2022
  multispectral find recovered a fragment, a page of numbers, not images).
- **Geminus's *Isagoge*** ("Introduction to the Phenomena"), probably 1st c. BCE, a Hipparchan
  textbook — evidence the sky was already systematized into circles and named risings before Rome.
- **Ptolemy's *Almagest*** (Alexandria, c. 150 CE), ancestor of MAGNITUDINES (§4). A text — 1,025
  stars in 48 constellations, coordinates plus brightness class — not a picture, and no illustrated
  antique manuscript survives; the earliest copies are medieval Greek minuscule (the archetype
  reached the Vatican only in the 13th c.; none of its own copies predates the 16th — a contemporary
  uncial Almagest is not supported by what could be verified here). Use for §4/§5 only, never
  grammar — the caution `OVERVIEW.md` already raised.
- **The Leiden Aratea** (Leiden UB, Voss. Lat. Q. 79), c. 816 CE, Lorraine, court of Louis the
  Pious. Not Greco-Roman itself, but its subject is: a Carolingian copy of Germanicus's (1st c. CE)
  Latin translation of Aratus, 39 full-page miniatures — among the first surviving *pictures*,
  rather than reliefs, of the Greek figures. 99 folios, 225×200 mm. Listed here, not as its own
  era, because its figures and captions are inherited almost unchanged seven centuries on — this
  era's afterlife and the bridge to era IV.

## 2. The grammar

Three grammars, not one, because the surviving media never overlap: **stone relief** (Farnese),
**engraved metal** (Mainz, Kugel, Antikythera) and **painted parchment** (the Aratea). The era's
core identity is the first: **modelling by light and shadow on an unpainted surface, with no
stars.** A figure is a raised contour, cut by point-chisel, claw-chisel and drill, its volume read
entirely by how the relief catches raking light — no line as such, only the edge where cut stone
meets its own shadow. The sphere carries the *only* non-figural marks: a small number of compass-
drawn great circles, the sole coordinate grid. Composition is a sphere, not a page — no edge, no
frame, no register bands, and (unlike era I) no flattening of the sky into rows. Perspective is
sculptural foreshortening only.

The engraved-metal grammar supplies what marble cannot: **fine incised line**, and — on the Mainz
globe — the era's only attested example of stars actually being marked, since the Farnese sphere
has none. Build the game's grammar across this seam: **figures and celestial circles from the
marble, stars from the bronze.** No Greco-Roman object shows figures, stars and colour together;
synthesizing that is this era's one necessary invention, done openly (see §12).

Not present: colour on the primary document (unpainted stone, §3), a perspective grid, register
bands, ground/sky distinction, or captions on the Farnese globe — wholly silent. Lettering must be
borrowed from Antikythera, Roman inscriptions, or the Aratea, never from the atlas itself.

## 3. Palette

The Farnese globe carries no pigment today and was very likely never painted — ancient authors
describe painted marble sculpture extensively (§12), but a scientific instrument like a globe, as
opposed to a cult statue, is a different class of object, and no source here claims polychromy for
it. The palette below is built from **material colour**, not paint, plus the one painted document
(the Aratea):

| Swatch | Source | Hex (approx.) | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pentelic/Carrara white | sun-bleached marble, slightly warm, not printer's white | `#EDE6D6` | ground |
| Deep relief shadow | shadow pooling in a deep drill-cut, stone-grey not black | `#4A463E` | "ink" — the only dark this era has |
| Weathered ochre patina | the honey-brown skin old excavated marble takes on | `#C9A66B` | age/wear accent |
| Bronze, unpolished | Mainz/Kugel/Antikythera ground metal, oxidised | `#6B5A3E` | metal ground |
| Verdigris | bronze's corrosion colour, a word the shipped game already uses for a plate | `#5B8A72` | metal accent/age |
| Struck silver | the Mainz globe's silver inlay, bright against bronze | `#C7C4B8` | this era's metal "gold" |
| Tyrian purple | murex-dye parchment ground, the grandest Carolingian pages (exact Aratea folio colours need a plate-by-plate check — some run closer to blue) | `#4B2E52` | Aratea ground, luxury |
| Gold leaf | the Aratea's star-points and highlights | `#D4AF37` | this era's true gold |
| Lamp-black / iron-gall | Aratea outlines; paint filling shallow-cut lettering elsewhere | `#2A2620` | written ink, manuscript half only |

Two grounds, deliberately not blended: **stone** (white-to-ochre, unpainted) and **purple-to-blue
parchment with gold** (the Aratea half). This era should probably ship as two variants rather than
force one palette across a grammar this divided — see §12.

## 4. Lettering and how people wrote

**Two scripts, both left-to-right — a real point of contrast with era II's Arabic and a real
saving for the reveal engine, which already assumes LTR.**

- **Greek epigraphic capitals** — squared, lightly-serifed monumental letterforms cut directly into
  stone with a point and a flat chisel, guided by scored guidelines and a compass for curves. No
  brush underdrawing is attested for Greek lapidary work the way it is for Roman (below); the mason
  worked straight from ruled guide lines. This is the hand for anything "spoken" by the sculpture,
  and for the tiny Antikythera inscriptions (down to 1.2 mm), which argue for a burin or fine point.
- **Roman square capitals**, the Trajan's Column hand — per Fr. Edward Catich's rubbing studies, a
  two-stage motion worth animating: **first painted, then cut.** A flat, chisel-edged brush lays
  the letterform at roughly 20–35° to the stroke, producing a 2:1 thick–thin modulation in one
  continuous pull; the serif is the residue of the brush lifting off or starting a stroke, not a
  separate mark. Only then does the mason cut a V-section groove along the painted guide, its two
  sloped walls catching light as a soft-edged shadow rather than a hard black line. **This is the
  same two-stage "wet lead, then commit" motion `penLettering()` already performs for era I's
  outline-then-flood hand** — here the flood step becomes the V-cut's shadow gradient, and the
  brush pass should visibly precede and slightly overshoot the chisel pass.
- **Greek numerals**: alphabetic (the "Milesian"/Ionic system), not positional — 24 letters plus
  three revived obsolete ones (stigma ϛ=6, qoppa ϟ=90, sampi ϡ=900) cover 1–999 in ones/tens/
  hundreds; a trailing keraia mark (ʹ) flags a number. Attested from the 4th century BCE.
- **Roman numerals** for the HUD, per the brief — and **the shipped game already does this**
  (`numerals=['I','II','III','IV']`, `src/plates.js`). Keep them for chapter/row/rank only; a
  five-figure score in Roman numerals is unreadable and no ancient accounting rendered sums that
  way (a counting board did that work). Set the score itself in plain digits, in the era's face.
- **Ptolemy's own magnitude system is the direct ancestor of MAGNITUDINES.** He calls brightness
  **μέγεθος** (*megethos*, "size/greatness" — root of "magnitude"), ranking stars `α′` (1st,
  brightest) to `ϛ′` (6th, faintest) in Greek numeral letters. For anomalous stars: **νεφελοειδής**
  (*nephelo-eidēs*, "cloud-shaped," used seven times in the *Almagest*, e.g. for Praesepe and the
  Perseus Double Cluster, both inherited from Hipparchus). This era's MAGNITUDINES legend should
  read `α′ β′ γ′ δ′ ε′ ϛ′` with `νεφ.` for the nebulous rank, not `1–6`.

Trajan itself (the commercial digitisation of the column's hand) is **not** free and must not be

needs contextual shaping — one glyph per codepoint, no joining — so `textAlongArc` and the

precomposed codepoint, so `npm run fonts` must subset against whatever accented forms the chosen
captions actually use. Quoting only proper names and mottos (as era II does with Arabic) should cut
GFS Porson's set to well under 100 glyphs, the way the Fell faces were cut to 110.

## 6. Currency and one rule

**No-twist reading:** rename `ink` to **the edge** (Latin *acies*), the same 0–1 gauge, spent by
distance flown, restored the same ways. This is a correction, not just dressing: "ink" presumes a
stylus-and-fluid medium, and this era's grammar is *subtractive* — stone or metal is cut away,
nothing added. A depleting chisel-edge, dulling with use and restored by a pause to hone it (the
existing orbit-hold ink-gain mechanic), is the era's real rationed resource — carving was metered
by tool wear and stamina, not a poured fluid.

**The twist:** give the six-class Ptolemaic **μέγεθος** (magnitude) scale to the *main nodes*
themselves, not just decorative background stars. The engine already renders six magnitude forms
for background dots (`plain / ringed / 4-/6-/8-point / haloed 8-point`, 6th to 1st); reuse that
asset on the nodes the player actually flies to. Each main node gets a Ptolemaic class 1–6 at spawn
(seeded, as today): a 1st-magnitude node — the haloed, most emphatic mark — pays the richest score
but demands a **narrower** perfect-tangent window (`rimWindow` shrunk, not widened); a 6th-
magnitude node is plainest, forgiving, and pays least. This is Ptolemy's own idea — brighter is
harder to mistake but a fainter target easier to catch — and costs almost nothing: no new node
type or `HAZARD_KINDS` field, just a tag read by two systems that already exist. It fits the
economy the way a graze or perfect landing already does: a visible, seeded trade read before
committing.

## 7. Dangers

Per `DANGERS.md`, depictions of the three existing rows only (not new gameplay):

- **Attractor (VORAGO) → Charybdis.** The whirlpool of the *Odyssey*, seething like a cauldron
  drawing water in and roaring hideously letting go — already a specification for a radial pull
  field. Drawn as a deep drilled spiral (the running-drill technique Roman workers used for
  undercut hair/drapery, applied to a spiral), or on the metal grammar as engraved rings tightening
  toward a punched centre.
- **Repulsor (MACULA) → Phaethon's fall.** Phaethon, son of Helios, lost control of the sun's
  chariot and was struck down before he could set the world alight — outward, scorching, once
  averted; drawn as a radiate solar disc with a falling chariot-wheel motif at its rim. **Caution:**
  *Phaethon* is also the attested Greek name for Jupiter (§5) — gloss once, or use *Hēliou harma*
  ("the Sun's chariot") for the danger specifically, to keep the two apart.
- **Crosswind (VENTUS) → the Anemoi.** Named winds — Boreas (N), Zephyros (W), six others — carved
  as winged figures on the octagonal Tower of the Winds, Roman Agora, Athens (Andronicus of
  Cyrrhus, c. 50 BCE, Pentelic marble), each on the face its wind blows from. Very likely the
  typological ancestor of every corner wind-head on a Renaissance map (Vitruvius describes the
  tower in *De Architectura* I.6, which Renaissance cartographers knew), but a direct citation
  chain to era III's specific wind-heads was not confirmed this session — mark (unverified).
- **Obscurer (nebula) → the Milky Way as Hera's milk.** Γαλαξίας (*galaxias*, "the milky one," the
  literal root of "galaxy") — from the myth of Hera's spilled milk nursing the infant Heracles. A
  soft, diffuse, non-lethal band is already the obscurer's function; the era's own name is the
  modern one too.

## 8. The seven families

Every family is modelled by relief and drill-work, not colour or line — the one rule that must
hold for all seven, or the plate stops reading as this era.

- **Ocean** — the calmest surface: a broad, unbroken low dome, abrasive-polished to a soft sheen
  the way flesh is finished on the Farnese figure, with no drill texture; a plain sphere reads only
  by a faint burnished highlight.
- **Crater** — a rasped, pitted ground: shallow point-chisel stipple for fine texture, a few deeper
  drilled punctures (true running-drill holes) for large craters, each pooling hard shadow at one
  edge under raking light.
- **Ringed** — concentric low-relief bands cut with a flat chisel guided by compass and straight-
  edge: literally the technique that cuts the globe's own celestial circles, reused as the family's
  defining mark.
- **Ice** — left almost entirely smooth and burnished, cold read as unworked mirror-bright stone; a
  few fine comb-lines suggest facets without breaking the polish. **Dune** shares its claw-chisel
  stroke but left deliberately unsmoothed, ridged and raking, reading as wind-ripple.
- **Volcanic** — the roughest family: broken, rustic-finish stone (the surface a mason leaves
  before final smoothing), deep undercut drill-work standing for vents, raw-vs-smoothed contrast
  doing the work colour would do elsewhere.
- **Storm** — the most heavily drilled family, borrowing the deep undercut channels of Roman
  portrait "drill-work" curled hair to produce a turbulent, light-swallowing texture with no single
  edge.

## 9. Chart furniture, frame, HUD

The Farnese globe carries no border, cartouche or colophon — its only non-figural marks are the
engraved great circles that give it a coordinate system at all. That is the frame this era gets:
**the frame becomes the circles.** The existing RA-hour/declination ticks map onto this almost
without translation — re-skin them as the globe's own equator/tropic/colure/ecliptic lines rather
than adding new furniture. Corner wind-heads become the Anemoi from the Tower of the Winds (§7).
HUD readouts move to a **tabula ansata** — the winged tablet Roman inscriptions were framed in —
rather than a margin box; a colophon-equivalent credit can use **FECIT** ("made this"), matching
the shipped game's habit of naming unlocks in Latin (`Delineavit`, `Ex libris`).

## 10. Four chapter plates

1. **The Antikythera mechanism, opened** — corroded bronze plates, parapegma gear-teeth and 1.2 mm
   inscriptions picked out by raking light: proof this sky was calculated, not just described.
2. **The Farnese Atlas globe**, close enough to lose the Titan: a figure crossing an engraved
   circle, drill-worked shadow standing in for a constellation's line.
3. **The Mainz globe** on its sundial gnomon, small and bright against dark bronze, Milky Way band
   and inlaid stars curving away at the edge — the chapter that finally shows stars.
4. **A Leiden Aratea opening**, purple-to-blue ground, gold-point stars, a painted figure mid-page
   — the bridge forward, explicitly not sculptural, ending on the page era IV's medium becomes.

## 11. Sound

- **Capture** — a single mallet-on-chisel tap, short and dry, replacing the shipped brush tick.
- **Orbit hold** — a continuous fine abrasive hiss (rubbing-stone on marble), rising almost
  imperceptibly the longer the orbit is held — stands in for `scratch()`.
- **Release** — a short stone-dust scrape, a grain of grit skittering off a cut edge.
- **Perfect transfer** — the tap gains a second, higher harmonic: two chisels landing near-
  simultaneously, as a finishing point-chisel doubles a rough strike.
- **Death/loss** — a single low crack, a struck-off chip, no ringing tail (stone doesn't sustain
  the way the shipped descending tones do — keep it dry and short).
- **Antikythera accent** (chapter 1, optional) — a faint bronze-gear tick-and-catch under ambient
  sound, a mechanism ratcheting one tooth.

## 12. Risks and open questions

- **Answering `OVERVIEW.md`'s deferral directly.** The paragraph that shelved this era gives two
  reasons: the *Almagest* is textual, and "the constellation tradition it fixes is the one era II
  and era III both already inherit." The first is true, and this file keeps the *Almagest* strictly
  to §4/§5, never the grammar. The second undersells the case: sharing a constellation *tradition*
  (which figure is Orion) is not sharing a *grammar*, and this era's — unpainted relief modelled by
  chisel-cut shadow, circles as the only coordinate marks, no stars on the primary document —
  overlaps neither era II's gold-on-paper colour nor era III's engraved cross-hatching. It
  duplicates a neighbour's *content*, as every era after era I must, and no neighbour's *look*.
  Read against rule 3 rather than rule 2, it clears the bar the other deferred candidates
  (Babylonian, no image at all; Dunhuang, contemporary with era II) do not.
- **The three-grammar problem is real and unresolved.** No single object gives figures, stars and
  colour together — building it means compositing marble (figures/circles, no stars/colour), the
  bronze globes (stars, fine line, no colour) and the Aratea (colour, gold, a different medium
  centuries later). Spike one test node early to see whether marble-relief and manuscript-gold read
  as one plate, or whether the era needs two variants, the way night/paper split era III.
- **Missing stars on the primary document is the sharpest engine mismatch.** Every other era's
  document at least implies where stars go; the Farnese globe's total absence of them should
  probably be the plate's own reveal — stars appearing only as drilled points cut *through* the
  relief at capture.
- **The wind-head lineage to era III (§7, §9) is plausible but not proven** as a direct chain; it
  rests on Vitruvius's tower description being known to Renaissance mapmakers, attested generally
  but not traced to a specific era-III source this session.
- **Greek shaping is cheap, unlike era II's Arabic** (no joining, one glyph per codepoint), but the
  polytonic accent set adds real inventory to `glyphs.mjs` and should be subset from the *actual*
  captions chosen, per `LETTERING.md`.
- **Polychromy caveat, so the game doesn't overcorrect:** ancient sculpture *in general* was
  routinely painted and "pure white marble" is a documented post-Renaissance misreading
  (Winckelmann chief among the causes), but no source here claims the Farnese Atlas *specifically*
  carried paint — unpainted stone (this file's default) is the supportable option; a painted
  variant is speculative dressing, not restoration.
