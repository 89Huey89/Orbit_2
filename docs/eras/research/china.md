# Research — The Scroll (Chinese Astronomical Tradition, Tang star chart c. 649–684 CE, to the Yuan Bureau of Astronomy, 1276–1437)

repository has no prior planning for it at all, and no earlier research pass to correct — only a
working brief (not itself checked into this repository, so treated here as this document's own

## 1. The documents

- **The Dunhuang star chart, British Library Or.8210/S.3326.** Found in 1907 by Aurel Stein in the
  sealed manuscript cache of Cave 17, Mogao Caves, Dunhuang, Gansu; now in the British Library.
  **Ink on paper, not silk** — a continuous scroll, per the 2009 reassessment (Bonnet-Bidaud,
  Praderie & Whitfield, *Journal of Astronomical History and Heritage*, also arXiv 0906.3034) some
  210 cm long by 24 cm wide. **Dating: the c. 649–684 CE range used above rests on that same 2009
  paper** (search summary only), which redated the chart from an earlier attribution to Emperor
  Zhongzong's reign (705–710) to Emperor Gaozong's by reading which imperial-name taboo-characters
  the scribe did and didn't avoid — a textual-dating method, not a material one. Treat 649–684 as
  the modern scholarly position, not a certainty this session could check against the paper's own
  tables (blocked).
- **Structure, attested (search summary):** twelve rectangular panels, each a strip of sky in
  quasi-cylindrical projection centred on the celestial equator, then a thirteenth circular panel —
  a circumpolar map in azimuthal projection, the sky looking straight up at the pole — closing with
  a drawn bowman in Tang dress whose specific identity this session couldn't establish
  (**unverified**: "a bowman," not a named figure). Search summaries give **1,339–1,345** stars in
  **257** named asterisms; the two star counts disagree slightly across sources reached and should
  be checked against the primary paper before use in exact form.
- **The three-colour scheme, attested (search summary):** stars are small filled discs in **black,
  red, and white or yellow**, corresponding to three named schools of pre-Han/Han astronomer — **Gan
  De** (甘德, Warring States, Qi or Wei), **Shi Shen** (石申, Warring States, Zhao) and **Wu Xian**
  (巫咸, a semi-legendary, much older shaman-astronomer). Harmonising the three schools' separate
  catalogues into one chart, one colour per school, is generally credited to **Chen Zhuo** (陳卓), a
  3rd-century CE Jin-dynasty astronomer — a widely repeated attribution worked from general
  knowledge rather than a source fetched this session (**unverified this session**). The
  colour-coding is provenance made visible, not decoration.
- **The Suzhou planisphere stele, 1247 CE.** A circular chart engraved in stone by Wang Zhiyuan
  after an earlier drawing by the scholar-official Huang Shang (c. 1190–1193, tutor to the crown
  prince); survives in the Suzhou Museum of Inscribed Steles, known today mainly through ink
  rubbings taken off the stone. Its own carved title is **天文圖 (*Tiānwén tú*, "Chart of the
  Heavens")** — a real historical title, not a gloss invented here. It shows the whole sky visible
  from central China on a polar projection — equator, ecliptic, the twenty-eight mansions' boundary
  lines, the Milky Way's outline — and (per search summaries) some 283 asterisms and roughly 1,565
  stars, larger than Dunhuang's, consistent with descending from Chen Zhuo's fuller synthesis.
- **Su Song's *Xin Yi Xiang Fa Yao* (新儀象法要), 1092 CE**, commonly "New Design for an Armillary
  Sphere and Celestial Globe" (English titles vary across sources reached; treat the translation as
  approximate). Over sixty illustrations describing Su Song's water-driven clock tower at Kaifeng:
  roughly 12 m tall, three tiers — an armillary sphere on top, rotated by the mechanism to track the
  sky; a celestial globe below, turning to show the sky's current state indoors; mechanical jacks at
  the bottom striking bells and drums on schedule. It used an early escapement (Joseph Needham
  argued for a line of influence toward later Western clockwork — historically argued, not settled)
  and, per search summaries, the oldest known illustration of an endless chain drive. In 1126,
  during the Jurchen conquest of Kaifeng, the tower was dismantled and carried off; the treatise
  preserved the design.
- **The imperial Bureau of Astronomy** — Sitianjian (司天監) under the Tang and Song, Qintianjian
  (欽天監) from the Yuan/Ming, with a separate Huihui Sitianjian (回回司天監, "Muslim Bureau of
  Astronomy") founded 1271 for Central and West Asian astronomers alongside the Han bureau (search
  summary). Its remit: continuous observation, calendar production, and reading celestial events as
  portents of state affairs, across dynasties into the twentieth century. Records this thorough are
  why a single day's guest star could be dated to the day, eight and a half centuries later.
- **The guest star of 1054.** On (per search summaries) 4 July 1054, Song court astronomers
  recorded a "guest star" (客星, *kè xīng*) near the position later identified with Taurus, visible
  in daylight "like Venus" for roughly 23 days. The fullest surviving accounts are in the *Song Shi*
  and *Song Huiyao*, redacted within decades of the event. A body of Chinese and Japanese
  guest-star records assembled by sinologist J. J. L. Duyvendak in 1942 led to the object's general
  identification with the **Crab Nebula** — a Bureau record doing real astrophysical work eight
  centuries later. Chinese practice distinguished this from a **"broom star" (彗星, *huìxīng*)**, a
  tailed comet — a distinction this era's danger table leans on (§7).

## 2. The grammar — and the three reveal states

**The sky's actual division, attested (search summary), structurally unlike a Greek constellation.**
Classical Chinese astronomy divides the whole sky into **31 fields**: the **Three Enclosures (三垣,
*sān yuán*)** — Ziwei (紫微垣, Purple Forbidden Enclosure, circumpolar, always visible), Taiwei (太微垣,
Supreme Palace Enclosure) and Tianshi (天市垣, Heavenly Market Enclosure) — plus the
**Twenty-Eight Mansions (二十八宿, *èrshíbā xiù*)**, unequal-width arcs along the celestial equator
grouped in four sets of seven under the **Four Symbols** (四象: Azure Dragon east, Vermilion Bird
south, White Tiger west, Black Tortoise north) — the "southern horn" position cited for the 1054
guest star sits inside this system. The enclosures aren't sky-pictures; named for parts of an
imperial palace (a forbidden precinct, a supreme hall, a marketplace), they *hold* asterisms the way
a floor plan holds rooms — a bureaucratic order projected onto the stars, not a myth drawn among
them. The mansions aren't equal 30° zodiac signs either: their widths vary by many degrees, because
their job is to give the Moon, Sun and five planets a fixed set of markers to measure *against* — a
coordinate ruler across the sky, structurally closer to a graticule than to a picture.

The load-bearing difference from Greek practice: **a Greek constellation is a picture** — a
mythological figure traced through its stars, one of a small fixed set (48 in Ptolemy, 88 in the
modern IAU list) that between them tile the whole sky. **A Chinese asterism (星官, *xīngguān*,
literally "star office") is usually not a picture at all** — a small named cluster of a few to a few
dozen stars, named for a mundane or bureaucratic object (a well, a chariot, a granary, a rank of
officials, or a large group like the 45-star Yulin/羽林, "Forest of Feathers," the imperial guard),
where what matters is *position relative to neighbours*, not a silhouette. There are 283 of these in
the fuller catalogues (search summary, §1) against Ptolemy's 48 — far finer-grained, organised by
where a group sits in the 31-field system rather than by what shape it draws.

**The three reveal states, mapped onto that record, attested and translated parts kept separate:**

- **DISTANT PHENOMENON — isolated star points, weak relationships.** Attested translation: exactly
  what a newly observed light is before it has a place. A guest star is first recorded as a single
  unnamed point near a known asterism — *Song Shi* entries name the position it appeared at, not a
  name of its own — and only afterward, if at all, gains a fixed identity. An unknown body drawn as
  a lone dot with no joining line, colour-coded school, or enclosure membership is a faithful
  translation of that gap, not an invention.
- **DURING ORBIT — brush lines, positional relationships, catalog marks, grid/regional sky
  organisation, asterism structure.** Attested chart-making practice, translated into a build-up
  order: a chart plots each star as a disc positioned relative to neighbours first (never
  silhouette-first, unlike a Greek figure), then draws the thin straight lines visible on both the
  Dunhuang chart and Suzhou stele joining one asterism's members, then places the group inside its
  mansion or enclosure's boundary line. Dot, then joining line, then boundary is real chart grammar;
  pacing a progressive reveal across an orbit with it (dot at capture, joining lines by ~90°,
  boundary and school-colour by ~180–240°) is this document's translation into a mechanic, not an
  attested sequence in itself.
- **UNDERSTOOD OBJECT — a body with a place in an ordered celestial system.** Attested as exactly
  what "cataloguing" means here: a star fully entered carries a name, membership in an asterism,
  that asterism's home enclosure or mansion, and (on a multi-school chart) a colour recording whose
  observation it descends from — read off the historical record's own logic rather than coined for
  the game.

**Materials note, because the brief specifically asks for both:** the Dunhuang chart itself is
**paper**, not silk — search summaries are explicit that the scroll is "a roll of thin paper drawn
with ink." Silk belongs to an earlier layer of the same tradition: the **Mawangdui silk manuscripts**
(Han-dynasty tomb, Changsha, sealed 168 BCE) include a comet atlas on silk depicting 29 named "broom
star" forms (search summary) — the oldest attested pairing of ink-on-silk with this exact subject
matter, and the right source to cite for "silk" rather than importing it into the Dunhuang chart
itself. Use paper for the star-catalogue plates and silk for anything drawing on the older
comet-atlas material. Pigment sourcing beyond ink and cinnabar (e.g. malachite or azurite greens
common in Tang/Song painting more broadly) was not confirmed for star charts specifically this
session — not included in the table above rather than asserted on a general Chinese-painting basis.

## 4. Lettering and how people wrote

**The script:** formal captions use **kaishu (楷書, "model/regular script")**, the standard book-hand
essentially settled by the Tang dynasty. The single most consequential structural fact for this
era's rendering, and a real contrast with every alphabetic script on this ladder: Chinese is written
in **logographic, monospaced units** — every character, regardless of stroke count, occupies one
notional square of the same size (方塊字, "square-block characters") — so there is **no shaping
engine to build**, no joining, no ligature, nothing equivalent to Arabic's contextual forms
([`globe.md`](globe.md) §4) or Egyptian quadrat-stacking. A caption is a row (or, historically, a
column — see below) of equal-sized boxes; `scripts/glyphs.mjs`'s existing per-character extraction
is already the right shape for this script — arguably the simplest pipeline on the ladder despite
looking the most exotic.

**Stroke order (筆順, *bǐshùn*)** is real, codified, and taught as a fixed rule per character —
top-to-bottom, left-to-right, horizontal before vertical, outside before inside — unlike the Arabic
qalam's two-pass joined-run-then-dot motion (general knowledge, not run through either tool this
session). Whether this maps cleanly onto any existing reveal primitive in `src/` is not established
here — a claim about a specific function's fit would need to be checked against the current source
rather than asserted from the tradition alone, so it is left as an open production question in §12
rather than a settled design fact.

**The instrument:** the brush (毛筆, *máobǐ*) — animal hair bound to a point on a bamboo shaft —
loaded from an **inkstone (硯, *yàn*)**, where a solid **ink-stick (墨, *mò*)**, pine soot bound with
glue, is ground with water immediately before use. Unlike the qalam's fixed nib-angle, **the whole
expressive range of the brush is pressure**: press down for a thick stroke, lift for a thin one.
Brush, ink, paper and inkstone together are the **Four Treasures of the Study (文房四寶, *wénfáng
sìbǎo*)** — an attested, named set, and the direct source for this era's "inkwell" naming (§5).

**Numerals.** Captions use the plain logographic numerals (一二三四五六七八九十 for 1–10, 百/千 for
hundreds/thousands) — attested, simplest for HUD legibility. A distinct system existed alongside it
for calculation rather than writing: **counting rods (算籌, *suànchóu*)**, laid out in positional
decimal notation for the arithmetic behind ephemeris tables — the Bureau's computational engine, not
something a caption would ever be lettered in, but worth keeping distinct from the numerals set.

**Typefaces (OFL, checked this session):**

| Face | Style | Notes |

**A real production risk:** this era predates the 1950s–60s simplification reform by well over a
millennium. A Tang or Song chart used **traditional forms (繁體字)**; **Noto Serif SC sets Simplified
Chinese**, the wrong set on its face. Noto also ships **Noto Serif TC** (Traditional) under the same
OFL terms — not separately re-confirmed this session but a reasonable extrapolation from shared
Noto licensing — and that, not SC, is the face actually wanted; treat this like the Mashriqi-versus-
Perso-Arabic digit flag in [`globe.md`](globe.md): small, easy to miss, real. Unicode: **CJK Unified
Ideographs, U+4E00–U+9FFF**, is enormous (20,000+ code points), but as with every other era the
pipeline only needs the closed set of characters actually set — `scripts/glyphs.mjs`'s scan-then-cut
approach applies unchanged.

**Direction, an open question:** classical Chinese was written in **vertical columns, right to
left** — a third distinct layout logic beside this ladder's horizontal Latin and horizontal
right-to-left Arabic. Whether captions should render this way for authenticity or compromise to a
horizontal HUD line is not resolved here; flagged in §12.

## 6. Currency and one rule

**The era's resource is ink (墨, *mò*), and it needs no renaming at all.** A solid ink-stick, ground
by hand against an inkstone with water immediately before use, is a real, physically consumed
quantity — grind too little and the brush runs dry mid-stroke; grind too much and the surplus dries
and is wasted. Every stroke on every chart in this tradition was paid for exactly the way a
consumed-resource mechanic wants: consumed by use, replenished only by more (ground) labour.

**No-twist reading:** keep the resource as ink, renamed *mò* in the HUD, every number identical to
what ships today — legitimate on its own, and a safe default if this era is not enriched further.

**The proposed twist, kept small and cosmetic:** the tri-school colour convention (§1, §3) is
provenance, not decoration — three astronomers' three catalogues, kept visibly distinct on one
chart. Let the ink stroke drawn during a capture cycle through the three school colours (Gan De
black, Shi Shen red, Wu Xian pale) by encounter count within a chapter, rather than always drawing
in one fixed ink colour — a player who orbits three bodies in a row sees, without being told, that
they have drawn from "all three schools." This changes no scoring or hazard field, only which of
three pre-existing ink colours a capture's stroke uses — a render-side choice keyed off a counter
the ledger would need to hold in any case for a discovery-streak bonus, if this era ends up wanting
one. A richer, gameplay-affecting alternative — mansion widths genuinely vary (§2), so a body's
capture window could vary by which of the 28 mansions it is assigned to — is **not** proposed here,
flagged instead in §12 as a real, larger idea that would need a wider architectural conversation
before it touches the simulation.

## 7. Dangers

Same four-role danger shape (an attractor, a repulsor, a crosswind, an obscurer) that recurs across
this ladder's other eras; this era's own attested names and images over each, depiction only — no
new field, no new rule proposed.

- **Attractor — 熒惑守心 (*Yínghuò shǒu xīn*, "Mars lingering at the Heart").** *Yínghuò*
  ("the dazzling and confusing one") is this tradition's name for Mars, and its single most dreaded
  configuration was its retrograde station near the Heart asterism (containing Antares) — attested
  in multiple historical episodes (e.g. Lord Jing of Song, 480 BCE) as the omen most associated with
  an emperor's death or a state's fall. Depict as a baleful reddish point the eye is drawn toward and
  held at, not released — "guarding" read visually as a pull that refuses to let go.
- **Repulsor — 彗星 (*huìxīng*, "broom star").** A tailed comet, terminologically and
  observationally distinct from a guest star (§1) precisely by its tail; the Mawangdui silk atlas
  names 29 such forms. Depict the tail itself as the pushing shape — a trailing brush-stroke sweeping
  outward from the body, the opposite gesture from the attractor's held point.
- **Crosswind — 風角 (*fēngjiǎo*, "wind angle").** An attested class of Han-dynasty military and
  court divination reading the compass direction, strength and quality of wind for omens, catalogued
  among the *fangshi*'s (方士) techniques in the *Book of Later Han*. No single quotable
  historical incident anchors it the way *Yínghuò shǒu xīn* anchors the attractor — treat this row as
  attested-practice-general rather than one named event, and say so in-fiction if a specific claim is
  ever made.
- **Obscurer — 日食 / 月食 (*rìshí* / *yuèshí*, literally "sun-eating" / "moon-eating").** The plain
  classical terms for a solar or lunar eclipse — the word for the event *is* the omen, describing
  something visibly being consumed. Historical response, attested generally: the reigning emperor
  observed vegetarian restrictions, avoided the main palace, performed rescue rites, and sometimes
  issued a self-blaming edict. Depict as a literal bite taken out of a disc — the etymology needs no
  translation into a new image at all.

## 8. The seven families

Translated through this era's own asterism-naming logic and its own ink-painting technique
vocabulary, both attested in general Chinese art history rather than confirmed for star charts
specifically this session — flagged once here rather than on every row.

**Ocean** draws on **天河 (*Tiān Hé*, "the River of Heaven")**, this tradition's name for the Milky
Way, imagined as a real celestial river: a dilute ink wash brushed in one continuous gesture (水墨,
*shuǐmò*, "water-ink," the base wash technique of Chinese painting), pooling unevenly rather than
sitting flat. **Crater** draws on **井 (*Jǐng*, "the Well")**, itself one of the twenty-eight
mansions: a disc pocked with small ink-pooling blots, each a tiny flooded well rather than an impact.
**Ringed** draws on the enclosures' own boundary-line convention (§2) — the San Yuan are literally
drawn as an enclosing wall-line around their member stars — so a ringed world is one or more
concentric boundary-lines drawn exactly as an enclosure's own edge would be.

**Ice** uses **留白 (*liúbái*, "leaving blank")**, an attested Chinese painting technique of
deliberately leaving the paper unmarked to stand for mist, cloud or snow: the family reads by what
is withheld, not by what is drawn. **Dune** uses **飛白 (*fēibái*, "flying white")**, an attested
dry-brush technique — traditionally credited to the Han calligrapher Cai Yong — that leaves
streaky, wind-textured gaps within a stroke: a natural, on-theme fit for a dune's ridged surface.
**Volcanic** takes the Shi Shen-school's own cinnabar red as its ground, veined with black ink
fissures. **Storm** draws on **羽林 (*Yǔlín*, "Forest of Feathers")**, an attested large, dense
asterism of some 45 faint stars representing the imperial guard: the densest, most crowded cluster
of marks the chart's own catalogue offers, borrowed wholesale for the family that needs to read as
"too many points to resolve."

## 9. Chart furniture, frame, HUD

No European-style cartouche or scale bar: this tradition's furniture is a **title**, set in kaishu,
naming the chart directly (the Suzhou stele's own carved 天文圖, §1); a closing text panel recording
who compiled or copied it and when — the Suzhou stele's own inscription names Wang Zhiyuan as
engraver and Huang Shang as the source drawing, dated 1247, the same functional role a European
colophon plays; and **seals (印, *yìn*)**, pressed in cinnabar paste (朱砂, *zhūshā*) rather than
inked with a brush, marking ownership or authentication in a second colour against the black-ink
field. For the HUD: replace RA/declination hour-ticks with a graduated rim in **度 (*dù*)**, this
tradition's own degree — 365¼ to the circle, matching the solar year rather than 360° — a genuinely
different-looking scale that is nonetheless attested rather than invented; and close a run with a
colophon-style line in this era's own furniture (title cartouche, then a seal-stamp mark, then the
compiler/place/date line rendered as player/plate/seed) — a pattern akin to the manuscript-mode
furniture [`globe.md`](globe.md) proposes for its own era, in this era's vocabulary instead.

## 10. Candidate signature sheet

**Primary candidate: the Dunhuang chart's own thirteenth panel — the circumpolar map.** The point
every one of the twelve equatorial strips ultimately resolves toward: all three school-colours
converging around the Ziwei/Purple Forbidden Enclosure at the pole, the sky's one fixed point
standing for the emperor's own fixed residence at the centre of a moving court. That double meaning
— an astronomically genuine still point and a deliberately political one — makes an unusually good
match for a fixed, always-visible core element's own role as the one constant across a scrolling,
ever-changing field.

**Secondary candidates, for later enrichment:** the Suzhou stele's full circular chart, rubbed in
plain black ink off carved stone rather than brushed onto paper — a harder, more graphic register
worth alternating with Dunhuang's softer inked-paper look; Su Song's clock tower in cutaway, the
three tiers (armillary sphere, celestial globe, mechanical time-jacks) as the source for this era's
instrument-tool grammar (§11's escapement sound, and a candidate visual for an armillary-inspired
pointer in place of another brush); and the Dunhuang scroll's own closing bowman figure, already
tied to the slingshot naming in §5.

## 11. Sound

- **Grinding the ink-stick on the inkstone** — a soft, wet, circular grinding sound, distinct from
  both the reed's dry rasp and a quill's scratch; the resource being made, not spent, and therefore
  the right sound for a refill rather than a stroke.
- **The brush's stroke on paper** — a supple, absorbent hiss, softer and more yielding than a reed's
  dry scratch or a burin's dry cut; paper drinking the stroke rather than resisting it.
- **A seal pressed into cinnabar paste and stamped down** — a firm, brief press-and-lift with no
  scrape, a faint waxy give distinct from an inked stamp; the confirmation sound for a completed
  catalogue entry.
- **An armillary sphere's rings turning, with an escapement's tick** — a slow mechanical creak plus a
  periodic release, drawn from Su Song's tower (§1); the escapement's historical priority is argued,
  not settled, so keep the sound suggestive rather than claiming a "first mechanical clock" in-fiction.
- **A guest star's arrival** — a single struck, sustained tone, announced once and cleanly, unlike
  the crosswind's gust or the attractor's held pull: a guest star is a single unambiguous new fact,
  not a process, and its sound should say so.
- **Heavy paper unrolling** — a continuous scroll's own sound, a long low unfurl rather than a leaf
  turning, distinct from a codex-format era.

## 12. Risks and open questions

- **Star and asterism counts for Dunhuang disagree slightly across sources reached this session**
  (1,339 vs. 1,345 stars; 257 asterisms for Dunhuang against 283 for the fuller Chen Zhuo/Suzhou
  tradition) — only secondary summaries of the 2009 Bonnet-Bidaud/Praderie/Whitfield paper were
  reached, not its own tables (`WebFetch` to the paper's usual hosts and to Wikipedia/IDP was
  blocked). Pin an exact figure to that paper before it appears in player-facing copy.
- **The closing bowman figure's identity is unverified** — nothing found this session names who or
  what it represents beyond "a bowman in Tang dress"; treat as an image detail, not an identified
  character, until a primary source is checked.
- **Chen Zhuo's attribution rests on general knowledge, not a source fetched this session** — widely
  repeated in the secondary literature, but not independently re-verified this pass; confirm against
  a named source (e.g. Sivin's history of Chinese astronomy) before treating it as settled.
- **The *Xin Yi Xiang Fa Yao*'s title is translated inconsistently across sources reached** — treat
  the English title as approximate, the Chinese title as the fixed point.
- **Traditional-versus-simplified characters is a real, easy-to-miss production risk** (§4): Noto
  Serif SC sets the wrong-era character forms; Noto Serif TC is wanted instead, and its OFL status
  was inferred rather than independently re-confirmed this session.
- **Vertical, right-to-left column layout is the historically correct direction**, and whether the
  game should render captions that way or compromise to a horizontal HUD line is not resolved here
  (§4) — an open layout question, not a settled one.
- **Whether any existing reveal primitive in `src/` already fits stroke-order rendering (§4) is
  unverified** — noted as a plausible fit worth checking against the current source, not confirmed
  by reading it this session.
- **The crosswind's naming (風角/*fēngjiǎo*) is a practice-class, not anchored by one named
  incident** the way *Yínghuò shǒu xīn* anchors the attractor — weaker evidentially than the other
  three danger rows; caption it as general practice, not a single event.
- **Variable lunar-mansion widths as a capture-window twist (§6) is a genuine larger idea**,
  flagged rather than adopted: it would make capture geometry era-dependent, which would need to be
  checked against however the daily-plate seed contract is actually implemented before it is treated
  as safe.
- **This document's own opening framing (the era's place in a nine-era ladder, the specific brief
  wording quoted) is not corroborated against any file in this repository** — no `NEW-BRIEF.md` or
  equivalent was found under `docs/eras/` this session; treat the framing as this document's own
  working assumption, not an attested repository plan.
- **Nearly every claim here rests on `WebSearch` summaries, not a directly fetched page**, since
  `WebFetch` was blocked this session for every primary host tried (`en.wikipedia.org`, `idp.bl.uk`).
  Treat any claim without a second, independently-worded corroborating snippet with real caution,
  and re-verify against primary sources before this material is treated as final.
