# I · The Disc — research

**European Bronze Age, c. 1800–1000 BCE.** No era file exists for this slot yet — `docs/eras/01-ceiling.md`
is a different, later-numbered era (Senenmut's Egypt) and is untouched by this document. This is new
ground, built almost entirely around one object, so the risk OVERVIEW.md names ("an invented era is the
one thing this ladder cannot afford") is higher here than anywhere else. Everything below is sourced or
flagged; nothing is invented.

## 1. The documents

- **The Nebra sky disc** (*Himmelsscheibe von Nebra*), Landesmuseum für Vorgeschichte, Halle. Bronze disc,
  30–32 cm diameter, 1.5–4.5 mm thick (thinner at rim), ~2.0–2.2 kg, hammered gold-foil appliqués let into
  chiselled grooves (damascening). Found 1999 in an illegal metal-detector dig on the Mittelberg hill near
  Nebra with two swords, two axes, a chisel and spiral arm-ring fragments; recovered from looters in a
  2002 police sting in Basel. This is the document the era's grammar is pulled from — the only object in
  the whole ladder that is itself a small painted-metal sky, not a manuscript page or a ceiling.
- **The Trundholm sun chariot** (*Solvognen*), National Museum of Denmark. Bronze horse and six-wheeled
  chariot bearing a bronze disc, c. 1400 BCE, found in a Zealand bog in 1902. Whole object ~54×35×29 cm;
  the disc ~25 cm across, gilded on one face and spiral-engraved on both — the ungilded face reads as the
  sun by night, the gilded face by day. The horse and wheels make it the one Bronze Age object that stages
  the sun's transit as a moving event rather than a fixed position.
- **The gold hats** (*Goldhüte*), four known: **Schifferstadt** (found 1835, 29.6 cm, 350 g, c. 1400–1300
  BCE), **Avanton** (found 1844, ~55 cm surviving, 285 g, brim lost, c. 1400–1000 BCE), **Ezelsdorf-Buch**
  (found 1953, ~90 cm, tallest known, c. 1000–900 BCE, 28 horizontal decorative zones), and the **Berlin
  gold hat** (acquired 1996, findspot unrecorded, c. 1000–800 BCE, 21 horizontal zones). All conical sheet
  gold, worked entirely in repoussé bands of circles, rings and eyes — no engraving, no gemstone. A "154
  bands" total is reported for one of these hats but sources disagree on which; the zone counts (28, 21)
  are the more reliable figures. These are calendrical bands, not calendrical stars — the disc counts sky
  objects, the hats count repeating rings, and both are the same grammar: count by repeated identical mark.
- **The Bush Barrow lozenge**, associated with the Stonehenge landscape (Normanton Down). Sheet gold over
  an organic backing, 1 mm thick, 184×156 mm, c. 1950 BCE — two to three centuries *before* the disc,
  contemporary with Stonehenge's sarsen phase rather than built during it. Cross-hatched incised geometry,
  not damascening — the whole object is gold sheet, no inlay. Its sharpest angle is close to the local
  summer/winter solstice angle. Included for its incised-line technique, not for sky imagery — a lozenge
  is not a sky.
- **Nordic Bronze Age razors**, Denmark/southern Sweden, c. 1100–500 BCE — later than the disc, included
  for the ship-grammar only. Bronze, engraved with ships, sun-discs and sun-crosses; Flemming Kaul reads
  the corpus as one running solar myth, day-ships and night-ships carrying the sun through an underworld
  transit. Best-documented source for what the disc's own added ship (below) is doing.
- **Rock carvings, Tanum/Bohuslän** (Sweden, UNESCO World Heritage), thousands of images on 600+ panels
  along a former fjord shoreline, made c. 1700–200 BCE. Pecked into bare rock, unpainted at the time —
  the red paint seen today is modern conservation, not ancient pigment. Ships dominate; sun-crosses and
  wheel symbols recur. Source for "what a Bronze Age sun-ship looks like drawn," versus the disc's single
  schematic arc.
- **Kokino** (North Macedonia), a hilltop site in use from c. 1800 BCE, stone markers aligned to solstice/
  equinox sunrise-sunset and lunar extremes; a 19-year lunar cycle has been proposed. No image survives —
  a built alignment, not a drawn chart. Evidence the culture tracked the sky rigorously, not a grammar
  source.
- **Goseck circle** — checked and **excluded**: Neolithic, built c. 4900 BCE, abandoned by c. 4700 BCE,
  three-plus millennia before the disc despite sharing the region and the solstice-gate idea. Named here
  only to rule it out.
- **Linear B tablets** (Aegean, c. 1450–1200 BCE) — contemporary with the later end of this era's range,
  but a Mycenaean palace-administration script, not a sky record, and geographically the Aegean rather
  than Nordic/central Europe. Assessed in §4; does not belong in this era's document set.

## 2. The grammar

No register bands, no ground line: the disc is a disc, its sky scattered across the whole circular field
the way stars actually are — the earliest surviving instance of that grammar in the whole ladder (era
III's night field is the same idea two and a half millennia later). No perspective, no size-coded
magnitude — every star is the same small punched gold disc regardless of brightness, differentiated only
by position. The sun/moon disc and crescent are larger because they are a different *kind* of object, not
because size encodes brightness. Composition was added, not planned: the disc was reworked across at
least five phases over roughly 150–200 years, so its final state is a palimpsest — the horizon arcs
physically cover two of the original 32 stars, and one star was moved to stay visible, a real instance of
the chart correcting itself in place rather than being redrawn.

The horizon arcs are the one piece of true measurement on the object: the 82° angle between their
endpoints matches the sunrise/sunset angle at the summer and winter solstices as seen from the Mittelberg
hill — sky *tracked*, not merely depicted. The added ship (a gold arc fringed with short parallel strokes,
read as oars, a hull-and-rays, or possibly a rainbow or the Milky Way — genuinely disputed) is the one
narrative element, and the last major addition before the disc's ritual disfigurement (one arc torn off)
and burial. Emphatically **not present**: any figure, any human or animal form, any register/band layout
(contrast the gold hats, all band and no figure), any sequential narrative order, any writing to gloss
what is shown.

The disc's own contrast carries the era: dark, near-black bronze ground against bright, thin gold — "gold"
is the accent (as in era II), but the ground is worked metal, not paper or sky-blue, and should read as
cast and hammered, never flat colour. No pigment belongs to this era's objects; everything is metal, and
bare stone for Tanum.

## 4. Lettering and how people wrote

No script exists in temperate/Nordic Bronze Age Europe. This is the load-bearing fact of the era, and
should read as a feature: the one slot in the ladder that can honestly say *there was nothing to write,
and here is what people did instead.*

**What replaced writing: the punched count.** The disc's stars are individually punched or foil-cut gold
discs, laid one at a time, identical in kind — a tally, not a notation. The gold hats' bands stretch the
same principle into rings: repeat an identical stamped motif around a horizontal zone a counted number of
times, then start a new zone. Neither object numbers anything with a symbol meaning "32" or "28" — they
*show* that many, by making that many marks. Unary, by repetition, read by counting the marks rather than
reading a sign for the count.

**Instrument and motion — two techniques, and they move differently.**
- **Damascening** (the disc's gold): a groove is chiselled into the bronze along the mark's outline, a
  pre-cut gold foil shape is laid in, and the bronze either side is hammered flat over its edge to clamp
  it — *cut, place, close*, three discrete strikes per mark. The punch that makes each star is a single
  vertical tap, not a drawn stroke — categorically unlike a pen or burin, with no continuous line to
  animate.
- **Repoussé/chasing** (the hats, the lozenge): worked from the reverse over a resilient backing (pitch,
  wax, hide), pushed into relief from behind, then refined by chasing from the front — a rhythmic
  press-and-lift over a small area, band by band.

**What this means for the reveal animation.** Neither technique is a continuous nib stroke, so the era's
equivalent of `penLettering()`/`writeText()` should be closer to era IV's develop-in than era II's qalam: a
mark **appears**, struck into place with one percussive beat, an audible/visual "settle" as foil seats
into its groove, then the next strikes — paced by the punch, not by handwriting speed. Where the HUD needs
a number, the honest equivalent is repeated identical marks, grouped for legibility the way the hats'
bands already are, not digits.

**Typefaces: none needed, and that is the honest answer.** No OFL face belongs to this culture, because it
wrote nothing — reaching for one would be the "invented era" mistake relocated into type. **Linear B is
the one live alternative worth naming and declining.** `Noto Sans Linear B` is real: SIL OFL 1.1, on
Google Fonts, covers the Linear B syllabary block (U+10000–U+1007F) at roughly 90 glyphs, and would work
mechanically in the `fontkit` pipeline. But it is a stretch and should not be used: Linear B is a
Mycenaean palace-economy script, culturally and geographically distant from the Únětice/Nordic world the
disc and chariot belong to, and borrowing it would undercut the one fact that makes this era distinct —
this century didn't write. If a future Aegean era is ever built (OVERVIEW.md's strongest deferred
candidate), Linear B belongs there, not here.

**Numerals: none.** The HUD's numbers should be set as grouped punch-marks or gold-hat-style bands, not
digits in any typeface, for the same reason.

## 6. Currency and one rule

**Currency: gold foil**, exactly as the object rations it. The disc's gold differs measurably in
composition between its five phases, and provenance work traces phase-I gold to Cornish alluvial placers,
carried by the same Únětice-controlled network that moved Cornish tin and Baltic amber across Europe —
gold arrived in irregular parcels, whenever a trade connection produced some, never a steady flow. Bronze
itself has an even more concrete parallel: **Ösenringbarren**, open ring ingots common across Central
Europe c. 2150–1700 BCE, made and exchanged before weighing scales existed in the region, clustering
tightly enough in mass (documented: roughly 70% of examined rings close enough to be judged equivalent by
hand alone) to function as genuine proto-money, rationed by heft rather than count.

**The one rule twist — "the Pleiades rule."** The disc's best-supported functional reading (Ralph Hansen,
building on the Babylonian Pleiades-leap intercalation rule later recorded in MUL.APIN) is that a young
crescent moon (3–5 days old) seen beside the Pleiades cluster told the culture to insert a leap month,
keeping the moon-count true to the season-count — an observed coincidence triggering an extra unit of
time, a rule in the literal sense. The game already has the matching hook: completing a constellation pays
a flat +60 and a 4-second full pause of `darknessSpeed` (`darknessGrace`). Proposed twist: when the
Pleiades-analogue constellation is completed while the run carries a specific pickup state (reskinning an
existing pickup as "the young moon" rather than adding one), the reward is not just the usual grace but a
genuine extra row inserted into the ascent that doesn't count toward hazard escalation or `chartPace` — a
free beat in the climb, mirroring the leap month's job of correcting the calendar without moving the clock
forward. Fit: this extends the existing constellation-completion hook additively, but it changes `row`
progression on a trigger, which per `DANGERS.md`'s framing sits closer to option B ("dangers are era
rules") than option A, even though it touches pacing rather than `HAZARD_KINDS` — flag it as a real
decision, not a free one. **No-twist reading:** rename only — ink → gold foil (a narrowing foil strip, not
a percentage), perfect → "a true seam" (groove struck exactly, foil fully seated), graze → "a glancing
strike," darkness → "the patina" (the disc's own attested green tarnish creeping across the bronze) —
nothing about scoring, pacing or hazards changes.

## 7. Dangers

Per `DANGERS.md`'s standard: draw and name the three existing rows in the era's own language, change none
of the field/core/reach numbers. This is the hardest fit in the ladder for that standard — unlike Egypt's
Apep or the Globe's tinnīn, **Bronze Age Europe left no surviving monster-devours-sky myth in its own
period record.** Norse myths (Fenrir, Sköll and Hati) are Iron Age/medieval text, centuries to a millennium
later, and using them would import exactly the kind of borrowing DANGERS.md's table otherwise avoids.

- **Attractor (VORAGO): the spiral.** Abundantly attested — Trundholm's disc is spiral-engraved on both
  faces, razor handles are frequently cast as spirals, paired spirals recur across Bohuslän rock art. A
  spiral is already a pulling-inward shape; drawn as tightening bronze-dark rings with a gold-foil thread
  spiralling into the core, it needs no invented myth.
- **Repulsor (MACULA): the sun-wheel/sun-cross.** One of the single most repeated motifs in Scandinavian
  rock art and Kaul's razor corpus — a wheeled or spoked disc with radiating arms. Drawn with hammered-gold
  spokes bursting outward from a bronze disc.
- **Crosswind (VENTUS): weakest attestation of the three, flagged honestly.** No wind deity survives for
  this culture the way Shu or *al-Rīḥ* do elsewhere. The one available thread: the disc's ship is fringed
  with short parallel strokes, read by most scholars as oars (some read rays or a rainbow) — propelling the
  barge. A crosswind drawn as a run of short parallel gold strokes reads as motive force, but the reading
  of those strokes as propulsion at all is disputed (§1) — this is built on a contested detail, not solid
  attestation.
- **Obscurer (nebula): the cup-mark swarm.** Cup-and-ring marks — small pecked hollows, sometimes clustering
  thickly enough to obscure the motif beneath them — are a widespread, well-attested Atlantic-European and
  Scandinavian rock-art feature persisting into the Bronze Age. A cloud of small dark pecked hollows
  overtaking part of the chart is a natural, attested obscurer.

## 8. The seven families

- **Ocean:** a plain, unadorned gold-foil disc left smooth and bright — the disc's own moon, exactly this,
  a plain gilt circle with no internal punch-work; unworked and brightest reads as calm against every
  other family's worked surface.
- **Crater:** the same gold disc, pocked with individual punch-marks at irregular intervals — the disc's
  own star-punching technique turned inward onto one body, each mark a small dark dimple in the foil.
- **Ringed:** the gold-hat treatment at body scale — horizontal repoussé bands of small repeated circles
  running around the circumference, concentric, the same count-by-repetition logic as the hats' zones.
- **Ice:** the coolest, palest reading — worked in high-silver electrum rather than warm gold, left almost
  plain like ocean but with one hard white highlight edge, differing by temperature of colour rather than
  pattern.
- **Dune:** parallel raked lines across the face, close and slightly uneven — the same short-stroke
  technique proposed for the ship's oars (§7), repurposed as texture rather than propulsion.
- **Volcanic:** raw, unalloyed copper-red rather than gold — the one family that breaks the gold/bronze
  palette deliberately, radiating cracked lines chiselled outward from an off-centre point, echoing the
  sun-wheel's burst but in hot copper instead of cool gold.
- **Storm:** the spiral again, but loose and decorative rather than pulling — two or three open concentric
  bands rather than one tight inward coil, distinguished from the attractor by looseness and turn-count
  rather than a different vocabulary (an intentional reuse: the source objects themselves reuse the spiral
  across decorative and cosmological contexts — the vocabulary was never large).

## 9. Chart furniture, frame, HUD

Nothing in this era's documents has a border, cartouche, scale bar or colophon in the era III sense — there
is no page to frame. What the disc has is a physical **rim**, pierced with an estimated 35–40 small holes
(sources disagree on the exact count; treat as "somewhat under 40," unverified to the digit), almost
certainly for mounting on cloth or wood so it could be carried or displayed. That rim of regularly spaced
perforations is the era's one real piece of frame furniture, and maps cleanly onto the game's plate-mark/
tick-ring: the frame becomes the disc's own rim, and the RA-hour/declination ticks become its actual
punched holes, evenly spaced, standing in for degree ticks with no numeral needed beside them. There is no
compass rose, no maker's mark, no running head, no colophon — a Bronze Age object simply doesn't carry that
kind of paratext, and inventing one would be furniture the record doesn't have (see §12).

## 10. Four chapter plates

1. **The disc at rest (Phase I).** Bare bronze field: 32 punched gold stars, the sun-or-moon disc, the
   crescent, nothing else added — the founding image, calm and unweighted, close in spirit to "The Quiet."
2. **The sun chariot's transit.** Trundholm's horse drawing the gilded disc low across a dark field, the
   spiral-engraved face catching what light there is — the one object that stages the sky moving, fitting
   a drift/motion-themed plate.
3. **The horizon closes (Phases II–III).** The disc with both horizon arcs in place and the ship wedged
   between the stars, two original stars now hidden beneath gold-on-gold, the barge carrying the sun down
   out of the field — the disc's own darkening, an eclipse-themed plate without inventing an eclipse myth
   that isn't attested.
4. **The counted hat.** A gold hat's full banded height, every horizontal zone distinct, read top to
   bottom as accumulated counting — the era's most literal image of time measured by repetition.

## 11. Sound

- **The punch-tap (capture):** a single bright, high, percussive strike — hammer-on-punch, not a bell —
  for each star seated.
- **The seating (perfect landing):** the punch-tap followed by a soft resonant settle, as if foil has been
  pressed fully home into its groove — strike, then hush.
- **The chisel-groove (release/flight):** a short dry scrape, tool-on-bronze, the era's version of the
  game's live-drawn scratch noise — harder and more metallic than a quill.
- **The quench (darkness advancing):** a low hiss, hot worked bronze plunged into water to set it, rising
  as the darkness accelerates.
- **The strike lost (death):** a dull flat thud — bronze dropped, not rung — with a low tearing sound,
  echoing the disc's own attested final act before burial: one arc physically torn free.
- **The dig (loss, ambient alternative):** a soft, muffled underground hush, earth settling.

## 12. Risks and open questions

- **This era has no lettering system, and rule 2 ("every era speaks in its own hand") has nothing to hand
  it.** Every other era answers with a real script; this one can only offer grouped tally-marks/ring-bands
  (attested-adjacent, untried anywhere else in the ladder, needs its own legibility work) or modern German
  find-names wearing a Bronze Age costume. Spike and decide before other Disc-era work begins — every
  other section here routes around this unresolved question.
- **The "one rule" twist (§6) is not cosmetic.** It changes `row` progression on a trigger, which per
  `DANGERS.md` sits closer to option B than option A even though it avoids `HAZARD_KINDS` directly. Needs
  the same architectural decision DANGERS.md defers; the no-twist reading is the safe fallback.
- **The crosswind (§7) is the weakest-attested of the four dangers**, built on a disputed reading of the
  ship's fringing strokes — should be labelled as such if shipped, not presented as equally solid to the
  spiral/sun-wheel/cup-mark proposals.
- **The 2020 dating dispute is resolved, cite it correctly.** Gebhard and Krause (2020) argued the disc and
  its hoard might be Iron Age, on stylistic-comparison and find-context grounds. Subsequent work in
  *Archaeologia Austriaca* and further archaeometallurgical study (2024) reaffirmed the Early Bronze Age
  date; the Landesmuseum states the critics relied on selectively cited, superseded material. Current
  consensus holds the Bronze Age date. (unverified beyond session search summaries — no primary paper read
  in full.)
- **The gold hats' "154 bands" figure is unreconciled** between Ezelsdorf-Buch and Berlin across sources
  consulted; the zone counts (28, 21) are more consistent and should be the load-bearing numbers.
- **Goseck circle was a live lead in the brief and is excluded on dates alone** (Neolithic, not Bronze Age)
  — flagged in §1 so a future pass doesn't reintroduce it by mistake.
- **Mycenaean/Minoan sky material is thin and mostly inferential** (archaeoastronomical alignment
  arguments about sanctuary orientation and seal iconography, not a surviving sky picture) — better held
  for a possible future Aegean era than folded in here, same as Linear B.
- **Nothing here was checked against a primary source's full text.** Every direct WebFetch to museum,
  Wikipedia, and academic-publisher domains was blocked by this environment's network egress rules this
  session; all findings above rest on WebSearch result summaries only. Treat every claim as "well-supported
  by multiple independent search summaries," not "confirmed against a primary source" — the exact rim-hole
  count, the band-count attribution, and the precise wording of the 2020 papers most need a direct check
  before anything here ships.
