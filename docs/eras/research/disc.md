# I · The Disc — research

**European Bronze Age, c. 1800–1000 BCE.** No era file exists yet for this slot (`docs/eras/01-ceiling.md`
is a different, later-numbered era — Senenmut's Egypt — and this document does not touch it). This is new
ground, not a deepening of prior work. Follow `OVERVIEW.md`'s warning literally: this era is built almost
entirely around **one object**, so the temptation to invent supporting material is higher here than
anywhere else in the ladder. Everything below is sourced or flagged; nothing is invented.

## 1. The documents

- **The Nebra sky disc** (*Himmelsscheibe von Nebra*), Landesmuseum für Vorgeschichte, Halle
  (Saxony-Anhalt). Bronze disc, 30–32 cm diameter, 1.5–4.5 mm thick (thinner at the rim), ~2.0–2.2 kg,
  with hammered gold-foil appliqués let into chiselled grooves (damascening). Found 1999 in an illegal
  metal-detector dig on the Mittelberg hill near Nebra, alongside two swords, two axes, a chisel and
  fragments of spiral arm-rings; recovered from looters at a Basel hotel in a police sting in 2002. This
  is the document the era's grammar is pulled from — **the only object in the ladder that is itself a
  small painted-metal sky**, not a manuscript page or a ceiling. Every other document below is context
  for it.
- **The Trundholm sun chariot** (*Solvognen*), National Museum of Denmark, Copenhagen. Bronze horse and
  six-wheeled chariot carrying a bronze disc, c. 1400 BCE, found in a Zealand bog in 1902. Whole object
  ~54 × 35 × 29 cm; the disc itself ~25 cm across, gilded with hammered gold foil on one face only and
  engraved with concentric spiral bands on both. The ungilded face is thought to represent the sun by
  night, the gilded face the sun by day — the disc, not the horse, is the sky object; the horse and
  wheels are what makes it a *narrative*, the one Bronze Age artefact that stages the sun's daily
  transit as an event rather than a position.
- **The gold hats** (*Goldhüte*), four known: **Schifferstadt** (found 1835 near Speyer, Historisches
  Museum der Pfalz Speyer, 29.6 cm tall, 350 g, c. 1400–1300 BCE), **Avanton** (found 1844 near Poitiers,
  Bibliothèque nationale de France, ~55 cm of a taller original survives, 285 g, c. 1400–1000 BCE, brim
  lost), **Ezelsdorf-Buch** (found 1953 near Nuremberg, Germanisches Nationalmuseum, ~90 cm, the tallest
  known, c. 1000–900 BCE), and the **Berlin gold hat** (acquired 1996, findspot unrecorded, Museum für
  Vor- und Frühgeschichte Berlin, c. 1000–800 BCE). All are conical, sheet-gold, worked entirely in
  repoussé/embossed horizontal bands of circles, rings, eyes and rosettes — no engraving, no gemstone.
  Ezelsdorf-Buch carries **28 horizontal decorative zones**; the Berlin hat's own museum account gives
  **21 zones**, with sources differing on whether "154 bands" refers to Ezelsdorf-Buch or Berlin —
  flagged (unverified) which hat the 154-band figure belongs to; treat the zone counts as the reliable
  number and the "154" total as approximate. These are calendrical *bands*, not calendrical *stars*: the
  disc counts sky objects, the hats count repeating rings, and both are the same underlying grammar —
  count by repeated identical mark, because there is no numeral to write instead.
- **The Bush Barrow lozenge**, Wiltshire Museum (on loan basis)/associated with the Stonehenge
  landscape. Sheet gold over an organic backing, 1 mm thick, 184 × 156 mm, from the "Bush Barrow
  chieftain" burial, Normanton Down, c. 1950 BCE — earlier than the Nebra disc by two to three
  centuries, contemporary with Stonehenge's sarsen phase (phase 3, c. 2500–2000 BCE) rather than built
  during it. Cross-hatched geometric incision, not damascening — no gold-on-bronze inlay here, the whole
  object is gold sheet. Its sharpest angle is close to the angle between summer and winter sunrise at
  that latitude, read by some scholars as solstice geometry pressed into a body ornament rather than
  displayed on a sky-object; worth the era's palette for its incised-line technique, not its imagery (a
  lozenge is not a sky).
- **Nordic Bronze Age razors**, distributed across Denmark and southern Sweden, c. 1100–500 BCE (later
  than the disc; included here for the ship-grammar, not as a contemporary). Bronze, engraved with
  ships, sun-discs and sun-crosses; Flemming Kaul's reading treats the corpus as telling one running
  solar myth — day-ships and night-ships, the sun carried by ship through an underworld transit. This is
  the best-documented *narrative* grammar for what the disc's own added ship (below) is doing.
- **Rock carvings, Tanum/Bohuslän** (Sweden; UNESCO World Heritage), thousands of images on 600+ panels
  along a 25 km former fjord shoreline, made across the Bronze Age and into the early Iron Age
  (c. 1700–200 BCE). Pecked into bare rock, unpainted at the time of carving (the red paint seen today
  on museum-maintained panels is a modern conservation aid, not ancient pigment) — flagged so the
  palette section below does not mistake it for period colour. Ships dominate; sun-crosses, wheel
  symbols and paired spirals recur. This is the source for "what a Bronze Age sun-ship looks like
  drawn," as opposed to the Nebra disc's single schematic arc.
- **Kokino** (North Macedonia), a Bronze Age hilltop site, in use from c. 1800 BCE, with stone markers
  aligned to solstice and equinox sunrise/sunset and to lunar extremes; a 19-year lunar cycle (Metonic-
  like) has been proposed for it. No image survives — it is a built alignment, not a drawn chart, so it
  belongs in this document as evidence that Bronze Age Europe *tracked* the sky with the same rigour the
  disc shows, not as a source for the grammar itself.
- **Goseck circle** (Saxony-Anhalt, near the Nebra findspot) — checked and **excluded**: it is Neolithic,
  built c. 4900 BCE and abandoned by c. 4700 BCE, more than three millennia before the disc. It shares
  the region and the solstice-gate idea but not the period; it does not belong in this era's document
  set and is named here only to rule it out explicitly.
- **Linear B tablets** (Aegean, Knossos/Pylos/Mycenae, c. 1450–1200 BCE) — contemporary with the later
  end of this era's date range, but a different world: a Mycenaean palace administrative script, not a
  sky record, and geographically the Aegean rather than temperate/Nordic Europe. Assessed in §4; it does
  not belong in this era's document set as a sky source.

## 2. The grammar

No register bands, no baseline, no ground line at all: the disc is a **disc**, and its sky is scattered
across the whole circular field the way stars actually are, the earliest surviving instance of that
grammar anywhere in the ladder (era III's night-sky field is the same idea two and a half millennia
later). There is no perspective and no scale hierarchy by size — every star is the same small punched or
foil disc regardless of brightness, differentiated only by position, not by rendering. The sun/moon disc
and crescent are drawn larger only because they are a different *kind* of object, not because size codes
magnitude. Composition is added, not planned: the disc was reworked across (at least) five phases over
roughly 150–200 years, so its final composition is a **palimpsest** — later additions (the horizon arcs)
physically cover two of the original 32 stars, and a star was moved to stay visible, which is a real,
attested instance of the chart correcting itself in place rather than being redrawn.

The horizon arcs, once added, are the one piece of true geometric measurement on the object: the 82°
angle between their end-points matches the angle between sunrise and sunset at the summer and winter
solstices as seen from the Mittelberg hill specifically — this is sky *tracked*, not merely sky
*depicted*. The added ship (a gold arc fringed with parallel short strokes, read as oars, a hull, or
possibly a rainbow or the Milky Way — genuinely disputed) is the one narrative element on the object, and
it is the last major addition before the disc's ritual disfigurement (one arc torn off) and burial.
Emphatically **not present**: any figure, any human or animal form, any register or horizontal band
(contrast the gold hats, which are all band, no figure), any linear/sequential narrative reading order,
and no writing of any kind to gloss what is shown.

The disc's own contrast carries the whole era: **dark, near-black bronze ground** against **bright,
thin gold**. That is the era's one non-negotiable pairing — "gold" is the accent (as it is in era II), but
the ground here is not paper or sky-blue, it is worked metal, and it should read as *cast and hammered*,
never as flat colour. No pigment of any kind belongs to this era's objects — everything is metal, worked
metal, and (for Tanum) bare stone.

## 4. Lettering and how people wrote

There is no script in temperate/Nordic Bronze Age Europe. This is the load-bearing fact of the whole era
and should be treated as a feature, not a gap to paper over — era I is the one slot in the ladder where
the game can say, truthfully, *there was nothing to write, and here is what people did instead*.

**What was made instead of writing: the punched count.** The disc's stars are individually punched or
foil-cut gold discs, laid one at a time, identical in kind to every other star — a *tally*, not a
notation. The gold hats' bands are the same principle stretched into rings: repeat an identical stamped
motif around a horizontal zone a counted number of times, then start a new zone. Neither object numbers
anything with a symbol that means "32" or "28" — they *show* 32, they *show* 28 rings, by making that
many marks. This is the era's writing system: **unary, by repetition, read by counting the marks, not by
reading a sign for the count.**

**The instrument and the motion.** Two techniques, and they move differently:
- **Damascening** (the disc's gold): a groove is first chiselled into the bronze along the mark's outline,
  then a pre-cut gold foil shape is laid in and the bronze either side is hammered flat over its edge to
  clamp it — the motion is *cut, place, close*, three distinct strokes per mark, and the punch that makes
  each star is a single vertical tap, not a drawn stroke at all. This is categorically unlike a pen or
  burin drawing a line; there is no continuous stroke to animate, only a sequence of discrete strikes.
- **Repoussé/chasing** (the gold hats, the lozenge): worked from the reverse with a blunt tool over a
  resilient backing (pitch, wax, or hide), pushing the metal out into relief from behind, then
  fine-tuned by chasing from the front — a rhythmic, repeated *press-and-lift* over the same small
  area, band by band, top to bottom or bottom to top, always by horizontal register.

**How this should drive the game's reveal animation.** Neither technique is a continuous nib stroke, so
the era I equivalent of `penLettering()`/`writeText()` should not be a clip-and-nib reveal at all — it is
closer to era IV's "develop-in" than to era II's qalam: a mark **appears**, struck into place with a
single percussive beat (a punch-tap or a hammer-strike), with an audible/visual "settle" as the foil
seats into its groove, then the next mark strikes, at a rhythm dictated by the punch, not by handwriting
speed. Where the game's HUD needs to communicate a number (score, row, ink remaining, a count of
anything), the honest period equivalent is **not digits, it is repeated identical marks** — struck one at
a time, the way the disc's 32 stars or a gold hat's ring-count are 32 and that-many strikes, respectively,
grouped for legibility (the actual gold hats *do* cluster their ornament into bands, which reads
naturally as a grouped-tally convention, e.g. bands of five or ten, without inventing anything the
material doesn't already do).

**Concrete typefaces: none needed, and that is the honest answer.** There is no OFL Latin, runic, or other
face that belongs to this culture, because it wrote nothing — reaching for any typeface here would be the
"invented era" mistake OVERVIEW.md warns against, just relocated into the type system instead of the
document set. **Linear B is the one live alternative worth naming and then declining.** `Noto Sans Linear
B` exists, is SIL OFL 1.1 licensed, is on Google Fonts, and covers the Linear B syllabary block
(U+10000–U+1007F) at roughly 90 glyphs — mechanically it would work in the game's `fontkit` pipeline like
any other face. But it is a **stretch, and should not be used**: Linear B is a Mycenaean palace-economy
syllabary, culturally and geographically distant from the Únětice/Nordic Bronze Age world the disc, the
chariot and the gold hats belong to, and using it would import a script this culture never had, undercutting
the one fact ("this century didn't write") that makes the era distinct in the ladder. If a later era ever
wants a genuine Aegean slot (OVERVIEW.md already flags Greco-Roman as the strongest deferred candidate),
Linear B belongs there — not here.

**Numerals: none. The HUD's numbers should be set as grouped punch-marks or gold-hat-style ring bands**,
not as digits in any typeface, for the same reason.

## 6. Currency and one rule

**The currency: gold foil**, exactly as the object itself rations it. The disc's own gold is not
uniform — archaeometallurgy shows the gold used in each of its five phases differs measurably in
composition and, per recent provenance work, in source (the phase-I gold traces to Cornish alluvial
placers, transported via the same Únětice-controlled trade network that moved Cornish tin and Baltic
amber across Europe). The practical reading: **gold arrived in irregular parcels, whenever a trade
connection produced some, and a phase used what had turned up since the last one** — it was never a
steady, meterable flow. Bronze itself (the ground/currency-adjacent material) has an even more concrete
period parallel: **Ösenringbarren**, open ring-shaped copper ingots common across Central Europe
c. 2150–1700 BCE, made and exchanged *before* weighing scales existed in the region, and clustering
tightly enough in mass (documented statistical study: roughly 70% of examined rings fall within a range
close enough to be judged equivalent by hand-feel alone, a perceptual-threshold effect) that they
functioned as genuine proto-money, rationed and traded by heft rather than by number.

**The one rule twist — "the Pleiades rule."** The disc's best-supported functional reading (Ralph
Hansen's proposal, building on the Babylonian Pleiades-leap intercalation rule later recorded in MUL.APIN)
is that a specific sky alignment — a young crescent moon (3–5 days old) seen beside the Pleiades cluster —
told the culture it was time to insert a leap month into the lunisolar calendar, keeping the count of
moons true to the count of seasons. This is a *rule*, in the literal sense the brief asks for, not a
myth or an aesthetic: an observed coincidence triggers an extra unit of time. The game already has the
matching hook — completing a constellation currently pays a flat +60 and a 4-second full pause of
`darknessSpeed` (`darknessGrace`). The proposed twist: **when the Pleiades-analogue constellation is
completed while the run is carrying a specific pickup state (proposed: the crescent-moon/"young moon"
condition, reskinning an existing pickup rather than adding one)**, the reward is not just the usual
grace period but a genuine **extra row inserted into the ascent that does not count toward hazard
escalation or `chartPace`** — a free beat in the climb, mirroring the leap month's function of correcting
the calendar without moving the underlying clock forward. Fit to the existing economy: this extends the
constellation-completion hook that already exists (additive, not a new system) and stays inside the
`darknessGrace`/pacing surface rather than touching `HAZARD_KINDS`, but it is **not purely cosmetic** — it
changes `row`/`chartPace` progression, so per `DANGERS.md`'s framing it sits closer to option B/C than
option A and should be flagged as a real design decision, not assumed free. **The "no twist" reading**:
rename only — ink → gold foil (gauge drawn as a narrowing foil strip, not a percentage), a perfect
transfer → "a true seam" (the groove struck exactly, foil fully seated), a graze → "a glancing strike,"
darkness → "the patina" (green tarnish visibly creeping across the bronze ground as the run continues,
which is a real, attested transformation this exact object underwent), and nothing about scoring, pacing,
or hazards changes at all.

## 7. Dangers

Per `DANGERS.md`'s own standard: draw and name the three existing rows in the era's own visual language,
change nothing about the field/core/reach numbers. This era is the hardest fit in the ladder for that
standard, because — unlike Egypt's Apep or the Globe's tinnīn — **Bronze Age Europe left no surviving
monster-devours-sky myth in its own period record.** Later Norse myths (Fenrir swallowing the sun, the
wolves Sköll and Hati) are Iron Age/medieval textual sources, centuries to a millennium after this era's
end, and using them here would be exactly the kind of import DANGERS.md's table elsewhere avoids by
finding what the era *already drew*. The proposals below lean on the disc's and the rock art's own
attested motifs instead, and are honest about which is thinner.

- **Attractor (VORAGO):** the **spiral**. Genuinely and abundantly attested across the era — Trundholm's
  disc is engraved with concentric spirals on both faces, Nordic razor handles are frequently cast as
  spirals, and the paired-spiral motif recurs across Bohuslän rock art. A spiral is already a pulling-
  inward shape with no myth required to justify it; drawn as tightening bronze-dark rings with a bright
  gold-foil thread spiralling into the core, it needs no invented narrative to read as a danger.
- **Repulsor (MACULA):** the **sun-wheel/sun-cross**. A wheeled or spoked disc with radiating arms is one
  of the single most repeated motifs in Scandinavian Bronze Age rock art and appears on razors as a
  sun-symbol in Kaul's corpus; drawn with hammered-gold radiating spokes bursting outward from a bronze
  disc, it is a natural, attested repulsor.
- **Crosswind (VENTUS):** weakest attested of the three, flagged honestly. No wind deity or wind-figure
  survives from this culture the way Shu or *al-Rīḥ* do for the other eras. The one available thread:
  the disc's own added ship is fringed with short parallel strokes, read by most scholars as oars (some
  read them as rays or a rainbow) — propelling the barge across the sky. A crosswind depicted as a run of
  short parallel gold strokes, "the oar-strokes," reads as motive force blowing one steady way and is
  drawn from the object itself, but the *reading* of those strokes as propulsion at all is contested
  (§1), so this is a construction built on a disputed detail, not a solid attestation, and should be
  labelled as such if shipped.
- **Obscurer (nebula):** the **cup-mark swarm**. Cup-and-ring marks — small pecked hollows, sometimes
  clustering thickly enough to obscure the carved motif beneath or beside them — are a genuinely
  widespread, well-attested Atlantic-European and Scandinavian rock-art feature that persists into the
  Bronze Age. A cloud of small dark pecked hollows overtaking part of the chart is a natural, attested
  obscurer with no myth-borrowing required at all.

## 8. The seven families

- **Ocean:** a plain, unadorned gold-foil disc, the surface left smooth and bright — the disc's own moon
  is exactly this, a plain gilt circle with no internal punch-work, and "unworked and brightest" reads
  naturally as calm/fluid against every other family's worked surface.
- **Crater:** the same gold disc, but pocked with individual punch-marks scattered across the face at
  irregular intervals — literally the disc's own star-punching technique turned inward onto one body
  instead of scattered across the field, each mark a small dark dimple in the foil.
  **Ringed:** the gold-hat treatment, brought down to body scale — horizontal repoussé bands of small
  repeated circles running around the disc's circumference, concentric, the same "count by repetition"
  logic as the hats' zones.
- **Ice:** the coolest, palest reading — a disc worked in unalloyed or high-silver electrum rather than
  warm yellow gold, left almost entirely plain like ocean but with a harder, whiter highlight along one
  edge only, so it differs from ocean by temperature of colour rather than by pattern.
- **Dune:** parallel raked lines across the face, close together and slightly uneven — the same short
  parallel-stroke technique proposed for the ship's "oars" (§7), repurposed here as texture rather than
  propulsion, reading as combed/rippled rather than smooth.
- **Volcanic:** worked in **raw, unalloyed copper-red** rather than gold — the one family that breaks the
  gold/bronze palette deliberately, with radiating cracked lines chiselled outward from an off-centre
  point, echoing the sun-wheel repulsor's burst but rendered in hot copper rather than cool gold.
- **Storm:** the spiral, again — same motif as the attractor danger, but contained and decorative rather
  than pulling: two or three loose concentric spiral bands worked across the body rather than one tight
  inward coil, distinguishing "storm body" from "attractor hazard" by looseness and count of turns rather
  than by a different mark vocabulary. (This reuse is intentional and attested: the source objects
  themselves reuse the spiral across both decorative and, arguably, cosmological contexts — the
  vocabulary was never large.)

## 9. Chart furniture, frame, HUD

Nothing in this era's documents has a border, cartouche, scale bar, or colophon in the era III sense —
there is no page to frame. What the disc does have is a physical **rim**, pierced with an estimated
**35–40 small holes** (sources differ on the exact count; treat as "somewhat under 40," unverified to the
single digit) at some point after its main gold-work was finished, almost certainly for mounting on an
organic backing (cloth or wood) so it could be carried or displayed, possibly as a standard. That rim of
regularly spaced perforations is the era's one piece of genuine "frame furniture," and it maps cleanly
onto the game's existing plate-mark/tick-ring: **the frame becomes the disc's own rim, and the RA-
hour/declination tick marks become the rim's actual punched holes**, evenly spaced, standing in for
degree ticks without needing any numeral beside them. There is no compass rose, no maker's mark, no
running head, and no colophon — the HUD's equivalent of a title/printer's line has nothing to translate
to, and should probably not try (see §12): a Bronze Age object simply does not carry that kind of
paratext, and forcing one in would be inventing furniture the record doesn't have.

## 10. Four chapter plates

1. **The disc at rest (Phase I).** The bare bronze field: 32 punched gold stars, the sun-or-moon disc,
   the crescent, nothing else added yet — the founding image, calm and unweighted, close in spirit to
   the game's existing "The Quiet."
2. **The sun chariot's transit.** Trundholm's horse drawing the gilded disc low across a dark field, the
   spiral-engraved face catching what light there is — the one Bronze Age object that stages the sky
   *moving*, a natural fit for a "drift"/motion-themed plate.
3. **The horizon closes (Phases II–III).** The disc with both horizon arcs in place and the ship wedged
   between the stars, the two original stars now hidden beneath gold-on-gold, the barge appearing to
   carry the sun down and out of the visible field — the disc's own darkening, doubling as an
   "eclipse"-themed plate without inventing an eclipse myth that isn't attested.
4. **The counted hat.** A gold hat's full banded height, every horizontal zone distinct, read top to
   bottom as a depth of accumulated counting — the era's deepest, most literal image of *time measured by
   repetition*, fitting a "the deep" themed close.

## 11. Sound

- **The punch-tap (capture):** a single bright, high, percussive metallic strike — not a bell ring, a
  hammer-on-punch *tock* — for each star seated.
- **The seating (perfect landing):** the punch-tap immediately followed by a soft, slightly resonant
  settle, as if foil has been pressed fully home into its groove — a two-part sound, strike then hush.
- **The chisel-groove (release/flight):** a short dry scrape, tool-on-bronze, standing in for the
  scratch-noise the game already uses for a live-drawn line, just harder and more metallic than a quill.
- **The quench (darkness advancing):** a low hiss, the sound of hot worked bronze plunged into water to
  set it — rising in volume/pitch as the darkness accelerates.
- **The strike lost (death):** a dull, flat thud — bronze dropped, not rung — paired with a low tearing
  sound, standing in for the disc's own attested final act before burial: one arc physically torn free.
- **The dig (loss/burial, ambient):** a soft, muffled, underground hush — earth settling — as a quiet
  alternative to a stinger on run end, available for a gentler difficulty setting.

## 12. Risks and open questions

- **This era genuinely has no lettering system, and the ladder's rule 2 ("every era speaks in its own
  hand") has nothing to hand it.** Every other era answers rule 2 with a real script. This one cannot,
  honestly, without either (a) writing HUD numbers as grouped tally-marks/ring-bands, which is
  attested-adjacent but has never been tried anywhere else in the ladder and needs its own legibility
  work, or (b) quietly borrowing modern German find-names as "the era's captions," which are accurate
  labels but are 19th/20th-century museum prose wearing a Bronze Age costume. This should be spiked and
  decided before any other Disc-era work begins — it is the one open question every other section of
  this document had to route around.
- **The "one rule" twist (§6) is not cosmetic.** It changes `row` progression on a specific trigger, which
  per `DANGERS.md`'s own framework puts it closer to option B ("dangers are era rules") than option A, even
  though it touches pacing rather than `HAZARD_KINDS` directly. Needs the same architectural decision
  DANGERS.md defers before it can be built as specified; the "no twist" reading in §6 is the safe fallback.
- **The crosswind (§7) is the weakest-attested of the four dangers**, built on a disputed reading of the
  ship's fringing strokes. If the era ships without a strong crosswind justification, that should be
  stated in-repo rather than quietly presented as equally solid to the spiral/sun-wheel/cup-mark
  proposals.
- **The 2020 dating dispute is resolved but should be cited correctly.** Gebhard and Krause's 2020 paper
  argued the disc (and its associated hoard) might date from the Iron Age rather than the Early Bronze
  Age, largely on stylistic-comparison and find-context grounds. Subsequent work published in
  *Archaeologia Austriaca* by the Austrian Academy of Sciences, and further archaeometallurgical study
  (2024), reaffirmed the Early Bronze Age dating; the Landesmuseum states the critics' arguments were
  based on selective citation of superseded material. Current scholarly consensus holds the Bronze Age
  date. Worth one line in-game or in a colophon-equivalent, if the era ever wants to gesture at its own
  historiography the way a real museum label would — but this is a "could," not a requirement.
  (unverified beyond the summaries surfaced this session — no primary paper was read in full.)
- **The gold hats' exact "154 bands" figure is unreconciled between Ezelsdorf-Buch and Berlin** across
  the sources consulted this session; the zone counts (28 and 21 respectively) are more consistently
  reported and should be treated as the load-bearing numbers if this era ever needs to cite a specific
  count.
- **Goseck circle was a live lead in the brief and is excluded on dates alone** (Neolithic, not Bronze
  Age) — flagged prominently in §1 so a future pass doesn't reintroduce it by mistake.
- **Mycenaean/Minoan sky material is thin and mostly inferential** (archaeoastronomical alignment
  arguments about sanctuary orientations and seal iconography, not a surviving picture of the sky) — it
  does not offer this era a document the way Linear B might tempt a font choice; both are better held for
  a possible future Aegean era than folded in here.
- **No object in this document set was verified past what WebSearch's summarised results provided** —
  no primary museum page, journal PDF, or Wikipedia article was read directly this session (all direct
  fetches to museum/encyclopedia domains were blocked by network egress rules in this environment); every
  factual claim above should be read as "well-supported by multiple independent search summaries," not
  as "confirmed against a primary source," and anything load-bearing for a final design decision (exact
  hole count, exact band-count attribution, the precise wording of the 2020/rebuttal papers) deserves a
  direct primary-source check before it ships.
